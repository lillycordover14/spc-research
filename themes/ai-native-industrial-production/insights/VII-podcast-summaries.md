## 0. Cross-cutting macro

### 0.1 ★ "China Has Mass. Can America Catch Up?" — a16z Podcast

**Sep 29, 2025 · ~56 min · [Link](https://a16z.com/podcast/china-has-mass-can-america-catch-up/) · Source: src-20250929-452dcb (free a16z RSS audio, Whisper)**

**Who's talking.** Brian Schimpf (CEO, Anduril) and Chris Power (founder & CEO, Hadrian), with a16z's Marc Andreessen and Erik Torenberg. a16z discloses it may hold stakes in the companies discussed (0:38).

**Key takeaways**
1. The US bet for 30 years on a few "exquisite" weapons; Ukraine showed wars are long and industrial, so *mass* (how much you can produce) matters again.
2. In US war games, key munitions run out in about a week and take 2–3 years to refill.
3. The US outsourced high-volume manufacturing just as electronics took over every product, so the skills to mass-produce at scale were never built here.
4. Old US machine shops balanced about 70% commercial work with 30% defense; that cushion has gone.
5. Skilled workers are retiring ("basically 62"), so automation and software are the only realistic replacement.
6. Power's model is "factory first": build flexible capacity ahead of demand, because buyers (e.g., the Army wanting drones) won't commit until a factory exists.
7. Money problem: defense buys year by year, so lenders won't fund 10-year factory capex. Hadrian says it solves this by spreading capacity across many contracts.
8. Both expect the government to back 6–8 "national champions" with large purchase commitments, as it did for MP Materials.

**Macro / tailwinds.** Munitions gap; rare-earth and magnet chokepoints; government large-order (offtake) deals; a startup wave in factory software and automation over the last 3–4 years.

**Company claims (unverified).** Hadrian builds "gigafactories well ahead of the demand" and can pivot capacity across products (Power).

**Notable numbers.** Munitions last ~6–7 days, refill takes 2–3 years; old shops ran 70/30 commercial/defense; China "probably 20 years ahead" on factory automation (Power's view); a PS5 line is "~90%" automated (as said).

**Quotes**
- "we run out of munitions and missiles in like six to seven days" (:00)
- "everyone that's highly skilled in any manufacturing domain is basically 62" (9:02)
- "on pure manufacturing autonomy, they are probably 20 years ahead of us" (15:34)

**Why it matters for the primer / SPC thesis.** This is the clearest macro case for owner-operator producers: demand exists but nobody will fund the capacity, so whoever can raise and spread capex across many programs wins. It supports Manufacturing (Precision & Defense) and the "picked champions" policy tailwind.

**Bear case / skeptical points.** Both guests run (and a16z backs) the companies that benefit from this argument. No customer repeat orders or contract sizes are given. The plan depends on government offtakes that have not been signed at scale.

### 0.2 "How LA Is Quietly Becoming America's New Industrial Tech Hub" — Bloomberg *Odd Lots*

**Sep 21, 2026 · ~38 min · [Link](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/audio/2026-09-21/odd-lots-how-la-is-becoming-an-industrial-tech-hub-podcast) · Source: src-20260921-a51ab2 (free published transcript, PodScripts; a Whisper pass was also run but its second half was unusable)**

**Who's talking.** Chris Power (founder & CEO, Hadrian) and Tom Mueller (founder, Impulse Space; SpaceX employee No. 1), interviewed live in Hollywood by Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway (Bloomberg).

**Key takeaways**
1. Power's story of decline: great powers build industry, win, get lazy, financialize and offshore; the reserve currency follows industrial strength.
2. Hadrian describes itself as a "design agnostic" automated contract manufacturer, "Foxconn to every different brand". It makes parts from space components up to "entire missile[s]" and "30% of a Virginia-class submarine" (company claim).
3. Power says most factory work is not a robot problem: it is skilled people "driving an incredibly dumb computer" (90% of advanced manufacturing, his estimate). Closing the feedback loop in software lets a technician set up, press go and walk away.
4. Make vs buy: Mueller builds prototypes in-house to learn, then outsources production; even SpaceX now sends parts out (Hadrian is about to make Raptor parts). Power says every company should keep 10–15% in-house, but scale has to happen outside.
5. LA's ecosystem is real but tight on talent: startups poach from each other, Northrop and Boeing. "Software is done in Silicon Valley, hardware is done in LA" (Mueller).
6. Cost gap: Power says that even modelling his factory at zero labour and zero energy, some Chinese commercial parts land at about one-third of his cost, because of subsidised energy, capital and export rebates.
7. On tariffs, he backs the principle but says both administrations got the details wrong (transshipment through places like Singapore; rules on what counts as an assembly). He prefers bans on Chinese components in drones and robots.

**Macro / tailwinds.** Munitions run out in about seven days in war games; China built 2,600 ships versus six for the US (as Power put it); LA aerospace jobs are rising again because of SpaceX alumni companies (nuclear, drones, defense, 3D printing).

**Company claims.** Impulse: two craft came within 200 m of each other; Helios raises Falcon 9 payload to the Moon or Mars by about 5×. Hadrian: lights-out 24/7 software-powered factories; parts for an entire missile.

**Notable numbers.** SpaceX 165 launches last year and ~10,000 satellites (Mueller); China's parts cost about one-third of Hadrian's (Power); 10–15% in-house rule.

**Quotes**
- "to 30% of a Virginia-class submarine" (9:30)
- "driving an incredibly dumb computer. And that's 90% of advanced manufacturing" (10:42)
- "software is done in Silicon Valley, hardware is done in LA" (15:50)
- "there's three times the cost of the component imported from China" (32:57)

**Why it matters for the primer / SPC thesis.** This is the best plain statement of the "software-powered contract manufacturer" model and of the make-vs-buy line that decides how much work flows to new producers. The one-third cost gap shows why policy (bans and offtakes), not efficiency alone, sets the floor for US demand.

**Bear case / skeptical points.** Alloway openly challenges whether founders are "creating good footage for a 90 second tweet". No revenue, margin or repeat-order figures are given. If even a zero-labour US factory costs three times as much, the business depends on lasting policy protection.


### 0.3 "Can the U.S. Still Build Things?" (#34) — *The Drone Ultimatum*

**~95 min · [Link](https://www.thedroneultimatum.com/can-the-us-still-build-things-fixing-the-industrial-base-miles-arnone-podcast-34/) · Source: src-20261022-f423ef (free audio, Whisper)**

**Who's talking.** Miles Arnone, CEO and co-founder (with ex-Amazon executive Jeff Wilke) of Re:Build Manufacturing, a group of US industrial businesses; host is from the drone maker behind the show.

**Key takeaways**
1. Re:Build buys and builds engineering-led factories instead of building one megafactory: about 13 acquired businesses plus some built from scratch, across metals, machining, composites, automation and electronics design.
2. Investors are "permanently capitalized" — no 10-year private-equity fund clock — so it can hold businesses long term. Goal: grow production about 25% a year.
3. Revenue is roughly a third defense, a third medical/energy, a third automation and factory building.
4. Arnone is skeptical of hype: 3D printing needs far more capex and time per part; robotics and humanoids are oversold compared with lean manufacturing basics.
5. Vertical integration works for SpaceX and Tesla but they are "the exception that actually proves the rule"; most hard-tech startups die in the "scale" valley, which is deeper than the product-market-fit valley.
6. For drones, he argues for a US "DJI": standard airframes at 10,000+ a month, with payload companies building on top, phasing out Chinese components.
7. Re:Build incubated and spun out Slate, a single-model electric vehicle (factory for 150,000 a year; 125,000 reservations, company figure).

**Macro / tailwinds.** COVID exposed supply chains (US celebrated hundreds of masks while China made ~28 billion a month, as he put it); drone warfare needs domestic motors; policy whiplash between administrations is a risk to long-term industrial investment.

**Company claims.** ~13 acquisitions; 25%/yr production growth target; capability to build electric motor capacity "inside 24 months" with enough will.

**Notable numbers.** 10,000 drones/month as minimum viable scale; 3D printing "80 times the cap X" (his estimate); Slate 150,000/yr, 125,000 reservations.

**Quotes**
- "We b acquired about 13 businesses and we built some others" (2:56)
- "you need 80 times the cap X to make that thing" (11:00)
- "I do think we need to be at a minimum viable scale where we're producing 10,000 a month" (15:19)

**Why it matters.** The best *counter-thesis* to the primer: buy old shops and add engineering rather than build AI-native plants. Re:Build is a likely competitor, partner or acquirer for AI-native machine shops.

**Bear case / skeptical points.** This episode *is* the bear case on additive manufacturing, humanoids and venture-style vertical integration. Its own weak spot: no customer volumes or margins beyond Slate, and much of the second half is politics.

## 1. Raw Materials (Minerals & Metals)

### Stage A — Finding and mining

#### 1A.1 ★ "Critical Minerals: Mining for the Industrial Future" — a16z Podcast

**Jul 23, 2025 · [Link](https://a16z.com/podcast/critical-minerals-mining-for-the-industrial-future/) · Source: src-20250723-3f3ef8 (free a16z RSS audio, Whisper)**

**Who's talking.** Turner Caldwell (founder & CEO, Mariana Minerals; about 9 years at Tesla across factory design, cells, cathode and refining), with a16z's Erin Price-Wright and Ryan McEntush.

**Key takeaways**
1. Mariana came out of stealth with $85M raised. It calls itself a "vertically integrated software first minerals project developer and operator".
2. It runs two software layers: a Capital Project OS (AI that automates construction, engineering and buying paperwork, cutting a roughly 3-week lag between site and office) and a Plant OS (reinforcement learning to run refineries with about 1,000 control settings).
3. Goal: "enable 200 people to do what 10,000 people are needed to do".
4. Selling software into miners is a "death spiral" of pilots that never reach real plants; consultants' recommendations are often taken less than half the time. Owning the asset fixes this.
5. Mariana targets smaller "orphan" assets the majors skip, and uses only proven process steps so projects can get bank finance.
6. China commissions refineries in about 6 months versus 2–4 years in the West; one Chinese nickel site in Indonesia had 13,000 people on site.
7. Focus metals: lithium and copper. Mission: 10 projects in 10 years.

**Macro / tailwinds.** Copper demand trend; China's refining dominance; calls for price floors, lighter strings on federal money and faster exploration permits.

**Company claims.** Labour leverage of 200 vs 10,000; RL control will lift plant output (citing DeepMind's ~40% data-centre cooling energy cut as an analogy).

**Notable numbers.** $85M raised; 6 months vs 2–4 years to commission; 13,000 workers; a new pump in Australia can take 30 weeks.

**Quotes**
- "how do you enable 200 people to do what 10,000 people are needed to do today" (25:52)
- "I've been calling it a death spiral" (21:52)
- "the Chinese companies are doing it in six months" (31:33)

**Why it matters.** This is the founding case for the primer's owner-operator thesis in minerals: software alone can't get adopted, so the software company has to own the plant.

**Bear case.** No offtake buyers or revenue are named. Using only proven process steps limits how different Mariana can be. Results depend on commodity prices and on commissioning speed that has not yet been shown.

#### 1A.2 "Inside Earth AI: Cutting the Cost of Critical Mineral Discovery" (S2 E57) — Rare Earth Exchanges

**Feb 26, 2026 · 33 min · [Link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-dQONUziAxY) · Source: src-20251001-cf441c (free Anchor RSS audio, Whisper; new this job)**

**Who's talking.** Roman Teslyuk (founder & CEO, Earth AI; Ukrainian-born geologist who left a PhD in Australia), with host Dustin Olson (Rare Earth Exchanges).

**Key takeaways**
1. Discoveries are getting rarer: the world used to find about 150 new deposits a year; now it finds about 40–50. Finding a new ore body costs about $300M across the industry once failures are counted.
2. Earth AI trains on Australia's 50+ years of public geological archives at continent scale. The usual success rate is about 1 in 200 holes; Earth AI claims an 80% hit rate on mineralisation, "proven on five projects and four discoveries".
3. It is vertically integrated: its own geology and operations teams, its own drill hardware, 24/7 drilling and a lab in the works. Its drilling costs about a quarter of contractor rates, and its cost per discovery is under $1M (company claims).
4. Australia is the base because permits are fast (a mine can be permitted within a year; median 4–6 years from discovery to production). Earth AI is a US company (YC Summer 2019).
5. It holds 3.1M acres in New South Wales and the Northern Territory — the biggest landholder in NSW — with 750–800 pre-qualified targets. It has fewer than 40 staff.
6. Business model: prove up deposits and sell them to miners, keeping a royalty. A "mega mine" might be contract-mined instead. It is also looking at selling offtake early (directed to the US) and selling to new critical-mineral funds.
7. It ran 80 software pilots early on; take-up was poor, buyers pay little for software and it doesn't recur — which is why it moved to doing the exploration itself.

**Macro / tailwinds.** China, Russia and allies produce ~70% of strategic minerals vs ~7% for the West; 99 Western mines vs ~900 for that bloc (as he put it); new capital flowing into critical minerals.

**Company claims.** 80% hit rate; <$1M per discovery; drilling at 25% of contractor cost.

**Notable numbers.** 150 → 40–50 discoveries/yr; $300M per discovery (industry); 1 in 200; 3.1M acres; 750–800 targets; <40 staff; ~5% of in-ground value as a typical sale price ($20–50B of metal sells for "a couple billion").

**Quotes**
- "We used to be finding 150 new deposits per year" (2:10)
- "80% chance that's proven on five projects and four discoveries" (9:33)
- "We ran when we started, we ran 80 pilots" (24:26)
- "for us, the cost of discovery is like under 1 million" (27:22)

**Why it matters.** Strong evidence for the primer's core argument: software sold to miners didn't stick (80 pilots), so the company became the explorer. The 1-in-200 vs 80% and $300M vs <$1M claims are the key numbers to verify.

**Bear case.** All hit-rate and cost figures are the CEO's; a "discovery" here means grade, not a proven economic mine; selling projects at ~5% of metal value makes returns depend on a few big finds (he compares it to venture: 30 projects, 1–2 fund-returners).


#### 1A.3 "From Y Combinator to mining, ft Earth AI's Roman Teslyuk" — The Northern Miner Podcast

**Apr 28, 2026 · 66 min (interview from ~37:00) · [Link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KXbUJ2CjWbI) · Source: src-20260428-aa5557 (free SoundCloud RSS audio, Whisper; new this job)**

**Who's talking.** Roman Teslyuk (founder, CEO & CTO, Earth AI), interviewed by the Northern Miner Podcast hosts. The first ~37 minutes are a mining-news segment, which isn't summarised here.

**Key takeaways**
1. Earth AI calls itself a vertically integrated, "AI-centric" explorer. It has its own prediction model, mineral rights (3.1M acres), geologists, drill crews, self-built drilling kit and a newly opened lab.
2. Why it integrated: early pilots with explorers showed an underfunded industry with long timelines and a small market with slow sales cycles for service firms, so "being a service company is just not going to work".
3. Speed claim: the actual work of a discovery "can be done within two years". Explorers take 6–12 months per drilling cycle because of contractors (about six types per drill site) and ~3-month assay waits. Earth AI drills non-stop, gets core photos every shift, and expects 2–3-day lab turnaround at full production.
4. Cost claim: owning the rigs makes drilling a fixed cost rather than variable contractor spend, so it is "a couple of times cheaper" than normal campaigns.
5. Track record claim: hydrothermal mineralisation confirmed at ~30 prospects. It drilled 5, and 4 became discoveries (core grade equal to or better than producing mines). Next milestone: size the deposits, with initial results "in the next six to nine months".
6. The model ("MTP") is a focused deep neural net, not generative AI. It was trained on ~400M sites across Australia, linking historical sampling data to geophysics. A second tool, still in trial, estimates distance to the deposit core from drilling results.
7. Competition: for years it was Earth AI, KoBold and VRIFY. He estimates ~50 new startups since critical minerals became a theme. Four new rigs are on order.

**Macro / tailwinds.** Five core minerals (nickel, copper, cobalt, lithium, rare earths) need ~650 more mines by 2040, about 210 of them by 2030. Mines opened: three in 2024, one in 2025, two in 2023. ~40 discoveries a year, and only a fifth become mines. Discovery success rates fell ~70% over two decades. Fundraising got much easier in 2024 when critical minerals became a theme.

**Company claims.** 4 discoveries from 5 drilled prospects; "a couple of times cheaper" drilling; discovery work in ~2 years; 2–3-day assays planned.

**Notable numbers.** 650 mines by 2040 / 210 by 2030; 40 discoveries/yr, 1 in 5 becomes a mine; −70% success rate; 400M training sites; 30 prospects / 5 drilled / 4 discoveries; ~50 new startups; 4 rigs on order; a mine takes ~6 years minimum in Australia (1 yr discovery, 3 yrs definition and feasibility, 2 yrs construction); Series B "just over a year ago".

**Quotes**
- "being a service company is just not going to work out in this particular industry" (41:21)
- "the amount of work you need to do can be done within two years, really" (41:44)
- "It's three new mines in the world, 2024, one mine in 2025, two mines in 2023" (46:43)
- "it's not generative AI, it's a focus deep neural net" (62:00)

**Why it matters.** This is the longer version of the owner-operator story. Pilots failed → integrate → speed and cost come from owning drilling and the lab, not just the model. It also ties the "AI" claim to a narrow, specific model.

**Bear case.** No resource estimate yet; sizing results are 6–9 months out. "Discovery" means grade in core, not tonnage. The cost and speed claims are the CEO's own. Competitors grew from ~3 to ~50 startups in about two years.


#### 1A.4 "Critical Minerals: What Makes Mining Tech VC-Backable" — Deep Tech Catalyst

**2026 · [Link](https://www.thescenarionist.com/p/critical-minerals-at-venture-scale-deeptech) · Source: src-20261022-cd6c5c (free audio, Whisper)**

**Who's talking.** Jun Ku, principal at Main Sequence (Sydney venture firm co-founded with CSIRO; just over A$1B across 3 funds, 70+ investments).

**Key takeaways**
1. Critical minerals became investable because of new demand (electrification, AI data centres), miners willing to try new tech, and government grants that lower the net capital needed.
2. Matching government money roughly one-to-one is a common starting point, but it needs a real project, plan and likely buyer.
3. Value chain: exploration (combine data to kill bad projects sooner), processing (make stranded deposits, tailings and recycling pay) and operations (sensors, precision mining).
4. Business models range from licensing (capped upside) to tolling, joint ventures, royalties and owning a plant (rarer now because capital is expensive).
5. Some tech companies end up "buying their own assets and turning into operators themselves" — the Mariana path.
6. Miners like to be "first to be second"; smaller tier-2/3 miners move faster for pilots.
7. There is a funding gap for $10M+ cheques, and few exit comparisons yet.

**Macro / tailwinds.** US DOE and Australia's ARENA grant money; AI data-centre demand for metals.

**Company claims.** None material (investor view).

**Notable numbers.** A$1B+ AUM; 70+ deals; gap at $10M+ rounds.

**Quotes**
- "who eventually go down the pathway of buying their own assets and turning into operators themselves" (21:31)
- "for larger checks in this sort of 10 million plus range, this is where we're starting to see a little bit of a gap" (30:28)
- "there actually are not many comps out in the market to show" (31:03)

**Why it matters.** Gives the primer a neutral framework for judging mining-tech business models and confirms the Series B+ gap where a growth investor could add value.

**Bear case.** Exits are unproven; owning plants adds capex and commodity risk; licensing caps upside.

### Stage B — Refining and making metal

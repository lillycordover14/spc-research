# Vertically Integrated Industries: VC primer

_Version 10.0, dated 2026-10-27 (America/New_York). v10.0 shortens v9.9 (103 pages) by removing repetition; structure, evidence and views are unchanged. Version history: repo git log._

**The idea in plain English.** America has spent forty years outsourcing the "hard middle" of making things: digging and refining minerals, making metal, machining and welding parts, and producing chemicals. Now defense, industrial policy and China's export controls make that middle valuable again, and it is short of plants and people. We look for startups that **own the plant and sell the physical output** (a mineral, a metal, a part, a chemical) and use **AI, software and automation inside their own operations** to build faster and run with fewer scarce skilled workers than incumbents. Owning production is what lets them keep the margin and the data.

**The five industries (chapters 3–7).**
1. **Raw Materials (Minerals & Metals)** — Stage A, finding and mining ore; Stage B, refining and making metal (smelting, alloys, magnets, powders).
2. **Manufacturing** — metal supply / service centers, then **fabrication (sheet/structures)** and **precision (machining, 3D printing, castings, composites)**.
3. **Specialty Chemicals** — producers of pharma ingredients and intermediates, agrochemical actives, electronic chemicals, fragrances, coatings and additives, catalysts and performance polymers, plus AI molecule discovery.
4. **Advanced Materials** — producers of engineered materials (battery cathodes, cooling fluids), with a separate **Materials Discovery Labs** sub-group.
5. **Enabling Software & Robotics** — tools sold to other people's factories; tracked, **outside the core**.

**Three labels, used for every company.**
- **Fully integrated** — owns production and sells the product itself. _Core fit._ (Example: SendCutSend owns its lasers and ships parts.)
- **Hybrid** — owns some production or a lab, but also sells services, licenses its process, franchises, or relies on partners to make the product. _Partial fit._ (Example: Hadrian runs its own plants and also puts cells inside Lockheed's plant.)
- **Enabler** — sells software, design, robots or tools to other companies' plants. _Outside the core;_ tracked as competitor, supplier or comparable. (Example: Arda.)

**How to read this doc.** Every industry chapter has the same order: (1) the problem today, (2) macro and tailwinds ranked and marked structural or reversible, (3) headwinds at equal weight, (4) why an AI-enabled vertically integrated company is the answer and where that breaks, (5) what would have to be true and signals — then deeper macro detail, how the industry works, unit economics and data loop, company profiles grouped by label, bear case and key sources. The macro is deliberately the heaviest part. Statements are kept apart:
- Plain sentence ending in `[src-…]` = sourced fact.
- **Claim (who):** = a company, founder or expert assertion. Sourced, not verified.
- `[inference]` = my read. Not sourced; argue with it.

## 0. Decision summary

_In one line: the category is real and defense demand is strong, but most buyers do not pay much for speed alone, factories must be built before contracts arrive, and nobody has yet shown that the software makes each part cheaper or better over time._

**Verdict [inference].** Back capital-light owner-operators at Series B only where speed and qualification are already paid for (Senra, Freeform, Isembard, as diligence hypotheses). Use Hadrian, Divergent and SendCutSend as benchmarks. Do not pay software multiples for job-shop economics until a company shows unit cost or quality improving with volume. Nobody in the repo shows that yet.

**What the evidence says (102 podcasts and 28 expert calls, read in full).** Each point is a speaker's claim unless marked; detail sits in chapter 4.
1. **Price is not the wedge; speed is, and only for some buyers.** Hadrian's CEO: "most of the market just doesn't value speed. The market very much values flexibility, scale, total program cost" [src-20260921-876080]. Three Boeing voices say they pay parity or more ("we have actually paid probably more") [src-20260818-9b2d5c][src-20250712-e5e110]; a Northrop GM: "Cost is secondary. Cost is driven usually by schedule" [src-20250707-3b7a46]. The only "about 50% cheaper" report (Raytheon) was RFQ pricing before delivery, brokered by a Hadrian investor [src-20250708-2aa3ad].
2. **Capex comes before contracts.** **Claim (Chris Power):** "0% chance of growing revenue unless you spend a billion in CapEx" [src-20260921-876080]; also "close to a billion in revenue next year", about 30% "pure software" (a stated plan, not a reported number [inference]) [src-20260610-779f8b]. Debt for machines, equity for software (section 2.5).
3. **Cheap labor is not the edge.** Labor is "less than 10% of COGS" in a new automated plant; China wins on a co-located supply chain [src-20260513-bdc044]. SendCutSend can beat China on only about 20-25% of its work [src-20260519-2d6ceb]. High utilization comes from scheduling software, not robots (SendCutSend lasers at 86%) [src-20260626-541ac8].
4. **Quality and qualification are the gate.** "None of their parts right now are flying" (Boeing Defense) [src-20260818-9b2d5c]. OTA prototype contracts waive many mil-spec rules; in production "there's a gap" in lot inspection Hadrian "is still maturing" on [src-20260908-ed94f0]. The Navy: "we have an adoption problem" [src-20250319-f21bec]. Entry is through repair and replacement parts [src-20251015-e9edbf][src-20260821-1c5a08].
5. **Share per customer is capped and the edge leaks.** SpaceX caps a shop at about 1/3 of its revenue [src-20250708-342eaf]; Boeing "will never" put one part family on one supplier [src-20260818-9b2d5c]. SendCutSend customers "outgrow us" [src-20251002-6a5afd]; enablers (GrayMatter, CloudNC) sell the same automation to incumbents [src-20261022-3335bd][src-20261009-7cafd0].
6. **The only learning-over-time number** is Hadrian's: "within a couple of years, we saw a 50% cut" in order-to-first-article time; its software "is replicable" and the moat is "financial power" plus a head start [src-20261004-9b47f5].
7. **Benchmarks and demand.** Incumbent roll-ups run "effectively ... a 30% gross margin" and buy at "seven times EBITDA" [src-20241001-28c2aa]. The FY26 NDAA adds multiyear munitions buys and advance-payment purchase commitments (Secs. 804(b), 867), the first channels that could turn prototype wins into multi-year orders [src-20251218-f0d6ef][inference]. Materials R&D budgets have not grown "crazily" [src-20260909-af3e60] (low).

## 1. The theme in plain English

**What it is.** AI-enabled vertically integrated industries means companies that build and run their own factories (or, in one variant, the software that runs other people's factories) and sell the output: parts, metal, material. The product is the physical thing; the software is how they make it faster and with fewer skilled people than incumbents. SPC's slide 8 frames it as producers "that build and run their own production capacity, using in-house AI and software to quote, schedule, and control robots and machines" [src-20261022-80fd75].

**Legacy vs AI-native.**

| | Legacy | AI-native |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Job shops (small owner-run machine or fab shops that build to a customer's drawing), service centers (metal distributors that stock, cut and ship metal), brokers and marketplaces | Hadrian, SendCutSend, Senra, 1872, Nox, Mariana |
| Software | ERP and CAM bought off the shelf (CAM = software that turns a 3D model into machine toolpaths; ERP = the back-office system of record) | Built in-house around quoting, nesting (packing parts onto a sheet or block to minimize scrap), CAM automation, scheduling, robot control and inspection |
| Speed | Average aerospace part 8-12 weeks, 40% late or with quality issues [src-20251024-2e5383] (med, single memo) | **Claim (SendCutSend):** 48 hours design to delivery [src-20260227-e92d45]; **Claim (Senra):** harness lead time six months to two weeks [src-20260128-a16c79] |
| Learning | Knowledge sits in the heads of machinists averaging early-60s in age (**Claim, Chris Power**) [src-20260908-65fab6] | Thesis: every part made improves the next quote, schedule and QC. Evidenced at 2 of ~15 names (section each chapter's data-loop section) |

**Three business models** (taxonomy `model`):

| Model | Best example | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| owner-operator | SendCutSend: owns all its lasers and plants, bootstrapped to a $1.01B round in May 2026 [src-20260519-38c7ed], then a Series B at $2.1B five months later [src-20261020-487a39] (med, single trade write-up) | Owns the data and the margin; carries the capex and utilization risk |
| software-first | Arda: CAD-to-work-instructions agent inside Anduril's ArsenalOS; $160M Series A at $1.5B [src-20261020-914a64] | Capital-light, fast to revenue; needs a host plant for data and competes with Siemens and Rockwell on the installed base [src-20250326-cc1810][src-20260804-0e8130] |
| hybrid | Hadrian (owns factories, now embeds cells inside Lockheed's Grand Prairie plant on a 7-year deal) [src-20261014-015327]; Isembard (owned plus franchised factories on MasonOS) [src-20260909-b547c7] | Tries to get owner data with partner capital; harder to underwrite because economics are split |

The core tradeoff in one line: owner-operators buy data ownership and pricing power with capital intensity; software-first buys speed and margin with data dependence on someone else's plant [inference].

## 2. Cross-cutting macro (applies to all five industries)

**The short version [inference].** Four things are true at once:
1. Washington is spending, lending and even taking equity at a scale not seen since the Cold War, for anyone who can produce metals, magnets, munitions and chips in the US.
2. Building a factory has got more expensive and more uncertain: rates went back up, metal tariffs raise input costs, and factory construction spending is falling.
3. China has shown it will cut off critical minerals, and the current pause on those controls expires in weeks.
4. Labor is the binding constraint, not demand.

AI-native producers sell into points 1 and 3 and are hurt by point 2. Their labor pitch (fewer, more productive people) only works if the software is real.

### 2.1 Manufacturing in one page

- **How big it is.** US manufacturing added $2.90 trillion of value in 2025, 10.1% of GDP, employing 12.65 million people at average pay of $112,261 [src-20261013-a7c995].
- **How it is organized.** A pyramid. At the top are a few **primes** that sell finished systems to the government: in FY2023 Lockheed Martin ($61.4B of contracts), RTX ($24.1B), General Dynamics ($22.9B), Boeing ($20.1B) and Northrop Grumman ($16.3B) [src-20251215-98f650]. Below are tier-1 suppliers (sub-systems), then tier-2 and tier-3 (parts, castings, forgings, harnesses, raw metal). Primes fell from 51 to 5 since the early 1990s [src-20220215-45c353].
- **The bottom is small and old.** 17,156 US machine shops; 83% have fewer than 20 employees [src-20250626-23f6d9]. 4,874 were lost from 2002 to 2018, about 20% [src-20250909-29c826]. **Claim (Isembard):** the average owner is over 65 and 40% plan to retire within five years [src-20260310-45bab3].

**What this means [inference].** A fragmented, aging supply base under a few powerful buyers. That gap is the opening every company here is trying to fill.

### 2.2 Six forces behind "why now" (overview)

1. **Defense needs more munitions and ships, fast.** Concern about the industrial base "has focused on munitions production due in large part to high rates of expenditure" [src-20251215-98f650]. Detail: 2.6.
2. **China controls key inputs.** It leads refining for 19 of 20 strategic energy minerals, at about 70% average share [src-20250521-c62d29], makes about 94% of rare-earth magnets and cut magnet exports in April 2025 [src-20251103-ee9b27]. The US imports 100% of 16 mineral commodities [src-20260206-04fe3e]; almost 90% of the most advanced chips are made in Taiwan [src-20230928-c47558]. "Can we make this at home?" is now a security question, so governments pay for domestic capacity even when it costs more [inference]. Detail: 3.1.
3. **Tariffs make imported metal dearer.** Since April 2026, products made almost entirely of steel, aluminum or copper face 50%, products containing them 25%, and 10% if the metal was melted in the US [src-20260402-1b5d6c]. Nucor's margin rose from 14% to 20%, but its own fabrication arm reported "margin compression resulting from higher steel input costs" [src-20260812-77d6f5]. Tariffs are proclamations and can be reversed: a tailwind, not a foundation [inference].
4. **There are not enough skilled workers.** Detail: 2.7.
5. **The government now lends and buys, not just regulates.** Detail: 2.3-2.4.
6. **Money is available, but not cheap.** Hadrian raised $1.37B in one round [src-20260807-b0dc5b], but the Fed raised rates to 3.75-4.00% on September 16, 2026, saying "inflation remains elevated" [src-20260916-f0c016]. Detail: 2.5, 2.9.

### 2.3 Industrial policy and the policy toolkit

**From grants to stakes.** CHIPS (2022) gave about $39B of manufacturing incentives and $11B of R&D money [src-20220511-6efadf]. Now the government also takes equity: Commerce turned Intel's grants into a 9.9% stake ($8.9B), and Fortune counts about $26.7B of US government equity across roughly 30 deals, including $400M in MP Materials [src-20260726-39275b]. A "national champion" can get the state as shareholder and anchor buyer; a competitor it picks can crowd you out [inference].

**CHIPS is ending.** FY2026 was the last year of appropriated CHIPS manufacturing money, and the 35% 48D credit covers only projects started by December 31, 2026 [src-20260701-78183b]. Chip-adjacent materials startups (Periodic, CuspAI, Orbital) sell into an industry whose subsidy flow peaks now [inference].

**IRA to OBBBA.** The July 2025 law cut back much of the IRA but kept the 45X production credit; most phase-downs now begin in 2030, and products with "material assistance" from a prohibited foreign entity get nothing [src-20250608-08074e]. **Bonus depreciation is permanent:** a $2M machine can be deducted in year one [src-20260114-2075f7][src-20260101-08d847]. This helps profitable incumbents most and makes their robots cheaper, narrowing the startups' edge [inference].

| Tool | What it is | What it does for producers | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| NDAA | Yearly defense policy law | FY26: multiyear munitions (Sec. 804(b)); Industrial Base Fund purchase commitments up to 10 years with advance payments (Sec. 867); spare-parts review (Sec. 1842) | [src-20251218-f0d6ef] |
| DPA Title III | Lets the government pay to expand domestic capacity | 23 awards, $820.5M since Jan 2025, mostly rare earths and rocket motors; none to tracked startups | [src-20251215-98f650][src-20261019-68fd3d] |
| OSC | Pentagon lender (2022) for 34 critical technology areas | $620M to Vulcan; $97.8M FY26 funding can back up to $4.4B of loans | [src-20251103-ee9b27][src-20260908-f6558e] |
| CHIPS Act | $52.7B for chip fabs, research and workforce | Co-funded Vulcan with a $50M incentive | [src-20230928-c47558][src-20251103-ee9b27] |
| IRA 45X | Per-unit credit for US-made energy components; minerals earn 10% of production cost | Minerals credit phases out 2031-2034; bars Chinese-linked supply chains | [src-20251001-45a0c1] |
| Section 232 | National-security tariffs | 50/25/10% metals tiers | [src-20260402-1b5d6c] |
| Buy American / Specialty Metals | DoD must buy US-made; specialty metals melted in the US | Protects domestic demand | [src-20251215-98f650] |

**What this means [inference].** Policy is strongest for metals and minerals; for machined parts it works mainly through prime contracts and the new multiyear authorities.

### 2.4 The government as lender and investor: OSC, EDF and DPA Title III

- **OSC (Defense).** June 2026 conditional loans of $725M to Energy Fuels and $500M to Phoenix Tailings [src-20260618-184e0c]; earlier $150M to MP Materials [src-20260807-110c13]. A new program lends $500M-$1B per private fund; applications due November 1, 2026; the FY27 request includes about $20B for OSC [src-20260831-4dd3b7]. An OSC official: without germanium, gallium and rare earths the buildout "is a pipe dream" [src-20260618-184e0c]. OSC debt can replace much of a heavy producer's equity, and the fund-finance window is worth a look for SPC itself [inference].
- **EDF (Energy; formerly the Loan Programs Office).** More than $289B of loan authority [src-20260116-27c00a][src-20251217-51eb2f], going to nuclear, grid, fossil and minerals, e.g. $17.5B conditional for Westinghouse AP1000 components; it wants offtakes, proven technology and US content [src-20260804-05a6a5]. Fits late-stage plants, but a reactor buildout means years of demand for forgings, castings and precision parts [inference].
- **DPA Title III and the Industrial Base Fund.** Grants and purchase commitments, plus DLA stockpile orders for magnets; USA Rare Earth got a $277M grant plus a $1.3B loan [src-20260807-110c13].
- **Mineral executive orders.** March 2025 speeds permits and directs DPA and DFC financing to minerals [src-20250320-91988a]; April 2025 opens seabed minerals [src-20250424-a310b3]. A 3-year permit instead of 10 changes value more than any grant [inference].

### 2.5 Capex, construction, rates and how plants get financed

- **Construction is cooling.** Manufacturing construction spending was $202.4B annualized in December 2025, almost 16% below its August 2024 peak, and down 21.9% year on year by May 2026; data centers are about 35% of contractor backlogs and compete for electricians and switchgear [src-20260302-477c5f][src-20260708-da5010].
- **Activity is strong.** ISM PMI 54.5 in September 2026, the ninth month above 50; new orders 55.3, prices paid 77.9 [src-20261008-89df84].
- **Capex before contracts.** **Claim (Power):** "no one's going to give you a production contract unless you've already started executing"; capex runs about 2 years ahead of revenue, and only customer prepayment or long-term infrastructure credit has worked [src-20260921-876080].
- **Debt for machines, equity for software.** **Claim (Mehr, Machina):** "don't fund physical hardware with equity"; machine loans cost about 2-3% over prime, 8-9% good to 12-14% risky [src-20260220-0d5345][src-20260320-1361e2][src-20260430-30ea09]. **Claim (Belosic):** lenders fund machines, not the software team [src-20260519-2d6ceb], and custom lines (anodizing, casting) are "unfinanceable" [src-20260624-3dc6f7]. Who funded what: section 10.
- **What this means [inference].** Parts demand is good; new plants are costlier. This favors capital-light models (brownfield robotics, roll-ups like Amca) over greenfield megafactories.

### 2.6 Defense and munitions budgets

- **The FY27 request.** CNAS counts $49.5B for 10 key munitions, about 8.5x the FY16-26 average of $5.8B. But about 82% depends on reconciliation, lead times have not improved for 6 of 10 munitions, the Iran war used more than half of US high-end interceptors, and framework deals with primes (Lockheed up to $9B, Northrop over $2B) are mostly unconverted; CNAS calls it "necessary but insufficient" [src-20261007-c6f64e]. CRS/CBO estimate a $21.7B replacement bill [src-20261005-b7e08b].
- **Primes sign capacity deals with new producers.** Lockheed's 7-year deal puts Hadrian cells inside its missile plant from early 2027 [src-20261014-015327]; the Navy put $900M beside $1.5B+ of Hadrian money for submarine-part plants [src-20260320-67ba4c]; Divergent builds Tomahawk structures for RTX [src-20260601-976843]; the Army chose Vulcan magnets for a 1M-drones-a-year program [src-20260923-c53c99]. **Claim (Power):** labor hours per submarine rose from about 13M to over 70M [src-20260610-779f8b].
- **Navy on record.** "I do not believe we have a technology problem, we have an adoption problem"; the nuclear supplier base shrank from 17,000+ to about 5,000 [src-20250319-f21bec].
- **Demand is lumpy and orders lag budgets.** **Claim (Power):** the government "may not buy missiles next year" [src-20260908-65fab6]. Carpenter's defense bookings fell because customers "are not receiving funds" [src-20261022-de5e6c].
- **What this means [inference].** The biggest demand driver, but back-loaded and political. Ask candidates how much backlog is funded versus framework-only.

### 2.7 Labor: the binding constraint, and why cheap labor is not the point

- **The gap.** 3.8M manufacturing jobs to fill from 2024 to 2033, 1.9M possibly unfilled [src-20240416-b69154]; 522,000 open jobs in August 2026 versus 432,000 pre-pandemic [src-20261013-a7c995]; DoD needs at least 122,000 more critical workers by 2028 [src-20250101-af28c1]. 2.3M technician openings by 2030 are counted separately and exclude machinists, so do not add them [src-20260910-a2cd42]. Welders needed by 2029: 320,500 [src-20261023-57ad95].
- **Owner-operator claims.** Hadrian trains operators in 30-40 days [src-20260926-70087f]; SendCutSend hired 200 welders in a year, mostly with no factory experience [src-20260525-4a12ab].
- **The correction.** **Claim (Drew Baglino):** in a new automated plant "the labor differential is less than 10% of COGS"; China wins because its supply chain sits within a 3-hour drive [src-20260513-bdc044].
- **What this means [inference].** The pitch is "we deliver when your job shop can't hire", not cheaper labor.

### 2.8 Power, permits and build time

## 3. Raw Materials (Minerals & Metals)

> **Our view: Raw Materials** _(analyst judgment, not fact [inference])_
> - **Stance:** Selective. Back refining and magnets (Stage B) where a government or defense buyer is already paying, and watch exploration (Stage A).
> - **Conviction:** medium. Policy pull is the strongest of any industry (2.14), but profits depend on prices, which a startup cannot control.
> - **Why:** (1) only the owner of a non-Chinese plant can sign the offtake that OSC and EDF lenders want [src-20260804-05a6a5]; (2) the data loop scores 2 where the process runs continuously (Mariana); (3) startups win on small deposits, tailings and magnet steps that majors ignore (3.14).
> - **Strongest counter:** MP, the largest US name, still loses money [src-20260201-0b4d7a]. A Chinese price cut or a US–China deal could strand subsidized plants.
> - **What would change our mind:** price floors renewed without subsidy; Mariana hits nameplate on schedule; OSC fund-finance commitments close (expected by March 2027).
> - **Best placed / watch:** Vulcan (fully integrated), Mariana (fully integrated); watch Earth AI (hybrid) and Phoenix Tailings (context).
> - **Good SPC fit:** Series A–B; a plant financed mostly by government debt, with venture money paying for software and ramp; buyer is DoD, a prime or an automaker with an offtake.

_What this industry is._ Raw Materials covers everything from finding ore to delivering a finished metal product: **Stage A, finding and mining** (exploration, mining and concentrating ore) and **Stage B, refining and making metal** (refining to pure oxides, carbonates or cathode, then smelting, alloys, magnets and powders). It sits at the bottom of every supply chain in this primer. Metal distribution (service centers that buy mill metal and cut it to size) now sits in Manufacturing (chapter 4) as a supplier to parts makers.

### 3.1 Shared macro: China's refining grip, the critical-minerals orders and government lending

- **China owns the middle of the chain.** It leads refining for 19 of 20 strategic energy minerals (about 70% share) [src-20250521-c62d29], did about 99% of heavy rare-earth processing until 2023 [src-20250414-50f023], makes about 60% of refined germanium [src-20261027-f82d55] and over 90% of rare-earth magnets (US under 1%) [src-20250429-6f9155]. Rare earths go into the F-35, Virginia- and Columbia-class submarines, Tomahawk and JDAM [src-20250414-50f023]. **Claim (Power):** about 150 of 1,000 components in a Navy missile come from China [src-20260610-779f8b].
- **China uses the leverage.** Licensing on 7 heavy rare earths and magnets since April 2025 (still in force); broader October 2025 controls suspended to **November 10, 2026** (the US says the truce runs to January 10, 2027); the gallium, germanium and antimony ban on the US suspended only to **November 27, 2026** [src-20261019-854e22][src-20261027-f82d55]. Rare-earth exports fell 9.6% year on year in January–September 2026 [src-20261019-854e22]. Strature puts a 63% probability on a short roll-over [src-20261016-83adeb] (med).
- **Prices show stress.** Germanium hit a record ¥28,500/kg on October 26, 2026, up about 110% year to date, with a 78.6% US-warehouse premium [src-20261027-f82d55]. In 2025 antimony rose 144%, while lithium fell 24% and nickel 11% [src-20260130-ebdd5a].
- **Not enough capacity is coming.** About $6.5 trillion a year of output depends on concentrated minerals; planned non-Chinese magnet capacity covers about a third of 2035 demand [src-20261014-b9d647].
- **Government lending and orders.** OSC, EDF, DPA and equity stakes (2.3-2.4) are mostly minerals deals; $65B of public money was committed to minerals in 2025 [src-20260716-6a06e4]. The 2025 executive orders speed permits [src-20250320-91988a][src-20250424-a310b3]; a new president can reverse them, but granted permits stay granted [inference].
- **What it means [inference].** A US producer of rare-earth metals, magnets, gallium or germanium has the government as buyer of last resort. But if China floods the market after a deal, prices collapse, so producers need floor-price offtakes (the MP Materials–DoD template) [src-20260726-39275b]. "Designing out" scarce elements is the strongest commercial case for materials AI (chapter 6).

**The problem.** US nonfuel mines produced $112B in 2025, but metals were only $38.1B [src-20260130-ebdd5a]. The US is 100% import-reliant for 16 mineral commodities and over 50% for 54, including 67% of rare earths and 57% of refined copper [src-20260206-04fe3e]. **Claim (Earth AI):** industry discovery success is about 0.5% per project [src-20250127-c28430].

**Headwinds, at equal weight.**
1. **Commodity prices swing** (lithium −24%, nickel −11% in 2025) [src-20260130-ebdd5a].
2. **Permits and time.** About 5 years to build and 3–5 to ramp after permits (**Claim, Mariana CEO**) [src-20260513-bdc044]; detail in 3.14.
3. **Geology decides.** Ore grade, not software, sets most of the cost [inference]. **Claim (Earth AI's CEO):** about 40 deposits are found a year and about a fifth become mines; about 650 more mines are needed by 2040 for five battery/magnet minerals, yet only a handful opened in 2023-25; about $300M of industry spend per discovery [src-20260428-aa5557, 45:54–47:04][src-20251001-cf441c, 2:10–4:46].

6. **The state picks winners** [inference].

### 3.4 Why an AI-enabled, vertically integrated company is the answer here

- **Stage A:** AI narrows where to drill; owning the claims and rigs keeps a discovery's upside. **Where it breaks [inference]:** a find is worth little until drilled out, and most are sold to miners.
- **Stage B:** the scarce thing is a permitted, financed plant outside China. Only the owner of output can sign the offtake lenders want [src-20260804-05a6a5]. AI attacks build and ramp time: **Claim (Mariana):** software halves 5–10-year timelines, and its first plant runs about 50% autonomously with agents retuning every 15 minutes [src-20260803-30e6d5][src-20260623-d1790b]. **Claim (Vulcan):** its pilot opened March 31, 2025 and was qualifying magnets within months [src-20250811-e7cb9a].
- **Where it breaks [inference].** Ore grade and price cycles decide most of the economics; a refinery is one giant bet, so venture equity is the wrong money for most of it.

### 3.6 Macro in more depth

- **Protected versus open markets.** DFARS bars defense buyers from a "foreign entity of concern", so price against China does not decide the sale [src-20250429-6f9155]; ITAR keeps defense work onshore. **Claim (Mehr):** "if you go head-to-head with China ... you're losing", so Machina starts in defense [src-20260622-7846f9]. In open markets SendCutSend can "meet or beat China" on only about 20-25% of its work [src-20260519-2d6ceb], and Taiwan is 20-30% cheaper even with tariffs [src-20250709-43486e]. Commercial buyers do pay some insurance: a few hundred dollars of parts can stop an $80,000 car (**Claim, Vulcan CEO**) [src-20250429-6f9155].
- **Tariffs, net.** Section 232 helps metal makers and hurts metal buyers, including fabrication startups; the 10% vs 25% gap rewards suppliers who can prove where metal was melted [src-20260402-1b5d6c][inference]. ENR notes an energy-price spike tied to the Iran conflict [src-20260708-da5010].
- **Data-center demand.** A legacy fabricator building generator enclosures for hyperscalers has "backlogs working on 2027" (**Claim**) [src-20261022-4234b3]; most hyperscalers have near-zero free cash flow [src-20261015-c425a9].

### 3.7 How the industry and its supply chain work

- **Stage A:** explorers find and drill out a deposit, usually selling it to a miner, which mines and concentrates the ore.
- **Stage B:** concentrate is refined to an oxide, carbonate or cathode, then smelted into alloys, magnets, powders and mill products for service centers and parts makers (chapter 4) or chemical and materials plants (chapters 5-6).
- **Buyers and prices.** Miners, metal and magnet makers, battery makers, primes, the DoD stockpile and traders. Prices are set globally or in China, so a US producer is a price-taker without a floor-price offtake [inference]. Incumbents: Nucor, Carpenter, MP Materials. Candidates: Hertha, Cyclic, Mass Magnetics, Duranium.
- **Market size.** No free source sizes US refining or exploration; public money committed to minerals: $65B in 2025 [src-20260716-6a06e4].

### 3.8 Where AI and automation help

Stage A: AI targeting and ore-body modeling. Stage B: AI agents tuning a refinery, recipe tuning for magnets and alloys, faster engineering and permitting documents (MarianaOS) [src-20260803-30e6d5].

## 4. Manufacturing

> **Our view: Manufacturing** _(analyst judgment, not fact [inference])_
> - **Stance:** Selective. Back capital-light owner-operators where speed and qualification are already paid for, and use the large names as benchmarks.
> - **Conviction:** medium. Demand is real and policy now buys orders, but price is not the wedge and no one shows unit cost falling with volume.
> - **Why:** (1) defense buyers pay for speed and capacity (section 0, finding 1); (2) SendCutSend has been profitable for 8 years [src-20260519-38c7ed], so the model can work; (3) the FY26 NDAA adds multiyear munitions and advance payments [src-20251218-f0d6ef].
> - **Strongest counter:** at 1,000 parts an old shop is cheaper [src-20260908-ed94f0], and AI CAM tools are sold to every shop [src-20260908-f98d21].
> - **What would change our mind:** a startup above 20% of a prime's rate-production spend at a lower price; published scrap or cycle-time curves improving with volume.
> - **Best placed / watch:** Senra and Freeform (fully integrated), Isembard (hybrid); benchmarks Hadrian, Divergent, SendCutSend.
> - **Good SPC fit:** Series B; owns the shop floor; already paid by primes or defense for speed; debt funds the machines.

_What this industry is._ Manufacturing turns metal (and some plastics and composites) into finished parts made to a customer's drawing. We use three sub-types, in chain order, and tag every company with one:

- **Metal Supply:** buying standard metal from mills and cutting it to size for parts makers (Nox Metals). These are suppliers to the parts makers below, not raw-materials producers.
- **Fabrication (sheet/structures):** cutting, bending and welding sheet metal and plate into brackets, enclosures, frames and structures. Lower tolerance, high volume of simple jobs, fast turnaround; margin depends on machine use and material cost.
- **Precision & Defense Hardware (machining, 3D printing, castings, composites):** tight-tolerance parts for aerospace, defense and medical, made by CNC machining, additive manufacturing (3D printing), casting or composites layup. Slower, needs certifications (AS9100, NADCAP) and a first-article inspection; higher margin.

Between fabrication and precision, the difference matters because fabrication competes on speed and price for many small orders, while precision competes on qualification and trust for long-lived programs. The macro below covers both; where it differs, we say which.

### 4.1 The problem today

- **The supplier base is small, old and shrinking** (2.1), and buyers have consolidated.
- **Parts are late.** An average aerospace part takes 8–12 weeks, and about 40% arrive late or with quality issues (med, single memo) [src-20251024-2e5383]. Castings take 9–10 months at a Raytheon supplier [src-20250708-2aa3ad].
- **Yet existing shops are underused:** machines cut metal 34% of the time against a 70% target [src-20250120-419768]. The shortage is people, programming and process, not machines [inference].
- **Labor.** Machinists: 344,900 jobs, $59,620 median pay, 30,400 openings a year from retirements [src-20260901-d504f9]. Welders: 437,700 jobs, 40,300 openings a year [src-20260901-3b0073].
- **Moving a part is slow by design:** first-article inspection repeats whenever design, material, process, tool or location changes [src-20190801-e81d7b].

**The defense sub-tier, in numbers.** DoD relies on more than 200,000 suppliers and has country-of-origin data for fewer than 10% of F-35 sub-tier suppliers; F-35 production stopped for about six months in 2023 and 2024 over Chinese magnet content; DoD put about $6.5B into 828 industrial-base projects in FY20–24 [src-20250724-1c09ad]. **Claim (Power):** 60-80% of a prime's output comes from small suppliers facing about 300-day payment terms [src-20260908-65fab6]. Sub-tier shops run batches "of 10 or 20" and ask "will you keep buying them from me" (NDIA president) [src-20260901-a44eee]. The bottleneck is thousands of untracked sub-tier shops, which is where a new owner-operator enters [inference].

### 4.3 Headwinds, at equal weight

1. **Spare capacity exists** (34% utilization), so primes can push work to existing shops [src-20250120-419768].
2. **Imports are cheaper:** Taiwan 20–30%, Vietnam/Malaysia more [src-20250709-43486e].
3. **Qualification is slow** (9.1).
4. **Tariffs raise input costs** (3.3).
5. **Rates and construction** (2.5).
6. **Automation saves less than it sounds.** **Claim:** labor falls from 100 to 30–50 hours a part, but net saving after machine costs is 20–30% at best [src-20250712-e5e110]; robot-learning success in real tasks is 59–66% [src-20260904-267293].
7. **Machine tools are imported.** US machine-tool shipments fell from $5B (1981) to $2B (1983) and imports reached about 60% by the late 1990s [src-20240118-4e5ac1], so even AI-native factories buy foreign machines [src-20260310-f52a8d].

### 4.4 Why an AI-enabled, vertically integrated company is the answer here

- **Versus job shops:** they lack capital, software and successors (2.1). An owner-operator that automates quoting, CAM and inspection runs machines more hours with fewer machinists [inference].
- **Versus marketplaces:** owned factories earn more; Proto Labs makes 49% gross margin in its own factories versus 31% in its network [src-20260206-8bb619][src-20260206-2b13c7].
- **Versus software sold to shops:** shops buy slowly and the vendor never owns the margin [inference].
- **Fabrication vs precision:** fabrication wins on speed and price for many small orders (**Claim, SendCutSend:** 48-hour delivery [src-20260227-e92d45]); precision wins on qualification and long contracts [inference].
- **Where it breaks [inference].** If primes keep using existing shops, or qualification stays slow, a capital-heavy factory sits idle.

### 4.5 What would have to be true, and signals to watch

- Primes sign multi-year contracts with new suppliers. **Watch:** more Lockheed-style deals; Sec. 867 commitments.
- Plants run well above 34% utilization. **Watch:** disclosed utilization or gross margin from Hadrian, Isembard, Senra.
- Qualification gets faster. **Watch:** first articles passed per quarter; Navy approvals of printed parts.
- Debt, not equity, funds plants.

### 4.6 Macro in more depth

**The munitions gap.** **Claim (Power):** the US buys about 100 Tomahawks a year, versus about 850 fired in one day in the Iran conflict [src-20260908-65fab6]. Lockheed has $60B+ of deals to triple PAC-3 output to 2,000 a year (trade press) [src-20261015-86f700].

**How a buyer buys a part.** A new producer wins one part number at one buyer at a time:
1. **RFQ:** the buyer sends a drawing and asks for price and date.
2. **Qualification:** AS9100 quality system, NADCAP for special processes (9.1 for timelines).
3. **First-article inspection (AS9102):** every dimension checked; repeated on any change [src-20190801-e81d7b].
4. **Prototype and low-rate production,** often under OTAs that can roll into production without new competition [src-20241219-9f8fcc].
5. **Rate production:** price and consistency decide; at about 1,000 parts a traditional shop is cheaper than Hadrian [src-20260908-ed94f0].
6. **Repeat and multiyear orders:** the prize. Programs last about 50 years, and suppliers for a 2035 aircraft are being chosen now [src-20260818-9b2d5c].

Buyers also cap any one supplier (decision summary 5). The test is whether prototype wins become repeat rate orders; evidence is thin (section 9) [inference].

## 5. Specialty Chemicals

> **Our view: Specialty Chemicals** _(analyst judgment, not fact [inference])_
> - **Stance:** Selective. Pharma ingredients and fragrances now; agrochemicals and discovery labs to watch.
> - **Conviction:** medium. Margins are real (5.2) and reshoring policy helps, but new molecules face long reviews.
> - **Why:** (1) specialty players earn far more than commodity players (Lonza 31.6% vs losses at Dow) [src-20260128-af3b8f]; (2) China and India dependence gives US makers a policy-backed buyer (5.6); (3) Osmo shows that owning an AI-designed molecule and the plant can earn money [src-20260204-1ec8c6].
> - **Strongest counter:** generics are excluded from the tariff, US demand is flat, and new-chemical reviews took a median of 520 days in 2025 [src-20261027-f4abbf].
> - **What would change our mind:** Phlow's FDA approval in 2027; Osmo naming large fragrance-house customers; faster EPA reviews.
> - **Best placed / watch:** Solugen, Osmo, Phlow (fully integrated); Scimplify, Enko (hybrid); Albert (enabler).
> - **Good SPC fit:** Series A–B; owns the molecule or process and a small US plant; buyer is a pharma, consumer or industrial brand with repeat orders.

_What this industry is._ Specialty chemicals are molecules sold for what they do, not what they are, in smaller volumes and at higher prices than bulk chemicals. This chapter covers pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs, their intermediates and key starting materials), agrochemical actives, electronic and semiconductor chemicals, flavors and fragrances, coatings and additives, catalysts, performance polymers, and AI molecule discovery. Engineered materials (battery cathodes, cooling fluids, alloys) and the materials discovery labs sit in chapter 6. **Brooke's focus** runs through the chapter: the chemical supply chain (5.6, 5.7) and AI-designed molecules (5.8, 5.10, 5.14).

### 5.1 The problem today

- **US drug supply rests on China and India.** About 46% of US generic doses depend on Chinese source materials (detail 5.6).
- **Specialty demand is soft.** US specialty output is expected to fall 0.3% in 2026, except for chips, data centers and healthcare (detail 5.6).
- **New molecules take years to reach customers.** Discovery is getting faster, but qualification and regulatory review (TSCA, FDA, pesticide registration) are not (5.14).
- **The 'valley of death' killed the last wave.** See 5.11 (1).

#### How specialty economics differ from commodity

| | Commodity | Specialty |
|---|---|---|
| Sold on | Price per ton | Performance in the customer's product |
| Plants | Giant, continuous (millions of t/yr) | Small, often batch, multi-product (5.7) |
| Margins (2025) | Dow net loss $2.4B [src-20260129-338338]; LyondellBasell net loss $738M [src-20260130-12a9f3] | Lonza CORE EBITDA 31.6% [src-20260128-af3b8f]; Clariant 17.8% [src-20260226-f774af]; Evonik 13.3% [src-20260305-5c5e74] |
| Switching cost | Low | High: the customer must re-qualify. **Claim (Scimplify):** custom development usually takes 12–18 months [src-20261014-b218c5] |
| Where AI helps | Plant control | Finding the molecule, the route and the formulation, then speeding qualification |

_Meaning [inference]:_ specialty buyers pay for performance and pay again for reliability, so a supplier that is qualified tends to stay. That makes specialty the part of chemistry where a startup can earn margin without matching incumbents on scale.

### 5.4 Headwinds, at equal weight

1. **Specialty demand is flat.** US specialty output is expected to fall 0.3% in 2026 [src-20260615-0c9b7e]; in Europe, dyes and pigments fell 2.7% in the first half of 2026 [src-20260915-e74614].
2. **Qualification is slow and sticky.** Incumbents are already qualified; a customer switches only for a real gain (5.2).
3. **Regulation sets the clock, not AI.** A new US chemical's median TSCA review took 520 days in 2025; pesticides and drugs take longer (5.14).
4. **Generics are outside the 232 tariff.** See 5.11 (4).
5. **Pledges are not plants.** See 5.3.
6. **Rates.** Plants are capital-heavy and long-dated; Fed 3.75–4.00% [src-20260916-f0c016].
7. **AI discovery is not the bottleneck.** Discovery without scale-up does not create supply (see 5.8).

### 5.5 Why an AI-enabled, vertically integrated company wins here

- **Owning the plant captures the margin.** Specialty margins are earned by whoever is qualified to supply (5.2); pure AI software for chemistry is a tool, labeled **enabler**.
- **AI shortens the slow steps.** Finding the molecule (Enko, Osmo), planning the route and process (Scimplify, Chemify), and continuous-flow plants (Phlow, On Demand Pharmaceuticals). All are **claims** at this stage.
- **Each order feeds the data loop.** A producer that makes many custom molecules builds proprietary process data that a software vendor never sees [inference].
- **Versus imports:** BIOSECURE and the 232 tariff reward provable US supply.
- **Where it breaks [inference]:** regulators (FDA, EPA) set the timeline, and a customer's qualification cycle does not move just because the molecule was found faster.

**What would have to be true, and signals to watch.**
- Onshoring rules bite on generics. **Watch:** any 232 extension to generics; BIOSECURE's first OMB list (Dec 2026).
- Repeat orders from paying buyers. **Watch:** Phlow's FDA decision (2027); Osmo's ingredient sales to large brands; Scimplify's US plant with Teknor Apex.

### 5.6 Macro in more depth: the supply chain

**China and India own the pharma building blocks.** About 90% of US prescriptions are generics. India supplies 47.2% of US generic doses and China 13.0%; only 10.5% are US-made. Indian firms get 65–70% of their KSMs, intermediates and APIs from China, so after adjusting, about 46% of US generic doses depend on Chinese source materials [src-20250605-bde73f]. Estimates vary: direct Chinese API is about 8% of volume, and Brookings puts Chinese API in "perhaps a quarter" of US drug volume [src-20250501-c298cb]. CFR finds about 41% of KSMs in US-approved APIs come only from China, including the starting materials for 94% of amoxicillin, 74% of heparin and 70% of acetaminophen sold in the US; US amoxicillin production went from 100% domestic to zero in twelve years. Shortages would peak 4.5–9 months after a Chinese restriction [src-20260501-7f527d]. Only about 10% of APIs by volume are US-made [src-20260701-818dbe]. _Meaning [inference]:_ the case is strongest for a short list of old, cheap molecules, which need a price floor or a government buyer.

**Beyond pharma, Asia holds the volume.** Asia-Pacific is about 47% of specialty revenue [src-20260101-cab1ab]. Electronic chemicals are growing with Asian fab expansion and US subsidies [src-20260101-cab1ab]. India is emerging as an AI-enabled specialty supplier as buyers diversify away from China (Scimplify, 5.10) [src-20261014-b218c5].

**Europe is shrinking.** Announced European closures reached 37 Mt over 2022–25, about 9% of capacity, with energy cost the reason in 49% of cases [src-20260115-ec35af]. Plants need 80–85% utilization to pay; Europe averages about 74%, and more than 30 German chemical companies a year have gone insolvent since 2024 [src-20260401-e2adb0]. Specialty exports from the EU fell €1.5B in the first half of 2026 [src-20260915-e74614]. _Meaning [inference]:_ buyers of European intermediates need new suppliers, and US makers with cheap energy are the natural replacement.

**US demand.** US specialty output is expected down 0.3% in 2026 and up 2.7% in 2027; specialties for semiconductors, data centers and healthcare keep growing [src-20260615-0c9b7e].

**Policy is pushing reshoring, mainly in pharma.**
- **Section 232 pharma tariff (April 2026):** 100% on patented drugs and their ingredients; 20% with approved onshoring plans (rising to 100% in 2030); zero for pricing-and-onshoring deals; 15% for the EU, Japan, Korea and Switzerland. Generics are excluded for now. Only 15% of patented API volume is US-made [src-20260402-ecae19]. Deadlines run to July 31 and September 29, 2026; pricing-deal firms get 0% until 2029 [src-20260601-17c2d4].
- **BIOSECURE Act:** enacted Dec 18, 2025. It bars federal contracts, grants and loans involving "biotechnology companies of concern"; the first OMB list is due by Dec 2026 [src-20260110-4db409]. The Pentagon added WuXi AppTec to its 1260H list in June 2026 [src-20260818-489fdf].
- **Strategic API Reserve:** an August 2025 order directs HHS to hold six months of API for about 26 critical drugs, favoring domestic sources [src-20260701-818dbe].
- **Executive Order 14293 and FDA PreCheck** speed approval of new US drug and API plants (section 11).

### 5.7 How the industry and its supply chain work

Think of chemistry as a five-step ladder. Each step takes the output of the step below and makes it more specific, smaller in volume and higher in price per kilogram.

1. **Feedstocks.** The raw inputs: natural gas and natural-gas liquids (ethane, propane), crude-oil fractions (naphtha), salt, sulfur, phosphate rock and, increasingly, biomass such as corn sugar.
2. **Commodity (basic) chemicals.** Made in giant, continuous plants measured in millions of tons a year. A **steam cracker** heats ethane or naphtha to "crack" it into ethylene and propylene, the building blocks of most plastics. Others: chlorine, sulfuric acid, methanol. Price is set by feedstock and energy cost; margins are thin and cyclical.
3. **Intermediates.** Molecules one or several reaction steps further on (for example acetic acid, adipic acid, solvents and monomers). In pharma, the key ones are **key starting materials (KSMs)**, the purchased building blocks an approved drug process starts from.
4. **Specialty and fine chemicals.** Smaller volumes sold for what they do, not what they are: coatings, catalysts, water-treatment chemicals, electronic chemicals, flavors, crop chemicals, energetics. Higher margins; buyers qualify a supplier and then rarely switch.
5. **Active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs).** The molecule in a drug that does the work. A **finished dose form (FDF)** is the pill or injection. A **CRO** (contract research organization) makes molecules for research; a **CDMO** (contract development and manufacturing organization) makes them at commercial scale under FDA rules.

Two engineering terms matter for startups. **Batch** chemistry runs one tank at a time; **continuous flow** pumps reactants through pipes or small reactors nonstop, which is safer for hazardous steps and easier to automate (FDA guidance Q13 covers continuous manufacturing). **Biomanufacturing** uses enzymes or engineered microbes (fermentation) instead of heat and pressure to make molecules.

Who buys: ACC says more than 80% of basic and specialty chemicals are bought by other industries, not consumers [src-20260615-0c9b7e]. So chemical demand follows manufacturing, housing, autos, data centers and pharma.

**Market size (this industry).** No clean free revenue number for US specialty chemicals in the repo [gap]. Global specialty estimates: 5.2. Europe announced 37 Mt of closures in 2022–25 [src-20260115-ec35af]; only about 10% of APIs by volume are made in the US [src-20260701-818dbe].

### 5.8 Where AI and automation help

| Stage | What AI does | Maturity | Molecule-to-revenue time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Discovery** (what molecule?) | Generative design, property prediction, screening billions of candidates | Real but crowded; models are commoditizing (4A) | Drugs: 10+ years to market; specialty chemicals: 2–5 years to qualify |
| **Synthesis planning** (how to make it?) | Retrosynthesis software suggests reaction routes (Synthia, IBM RXN, PostEra) | Decision support only; chemists still filter routes [inference from literature review] | Months saved per route |
| **Lab execution** (actually make it) | Robots plus AI run reactions and learn from results ("self-driving labs") | Early; claims of autonomous discovery have been disputed (the 2023 A-Lab row) | Faster make-test loops for research compounds |
| **Process and plant** (make it cheaply at scale) | New catalysts and enzymes, continuous-flow control, yield optimization | Where the money is, and where startups own plants | 3–7 years for a new plant [inference] |

The key point for SPC: **discovering a molecule is not the bottleneck; making it reliably at scale and getting buyers to qualify it is.** This matches the materials finding in 4A.4.

### 5.11 Bear case, at equal weight

1. **Biomanufacturing has a terrible record.** Ginkgo, Zymergen and Amyris all collapsed in 2021–23. For simple molecules, making them with organisms usually costs more than conventional chemical synthesis, so bio works mainly for complex, high-value compounds [src-20240101-499ec8]. Zymergen had no in-house commercial-scale manufacturing, its contract manufacturers missed production goals, and customers could not get its Hyaline film to work in their own production lines [src-20220101-9c58e3].
2. **AI-for-chemistry software alone fails.** Zapata is the example [src-20241011-721c93]. Retrosynthesis tools are still decision support for chemists [inference].
3. **Commodity economics punish newcomers.** Europe's plants are closing because of energy cost and Chinese overcapacity [src-20260115-ec35af]. A US startup selling a commodity competes with shale-fed incumbents and Chinese surplus, so it needs a real cost edge, not a green premium.
4. **Pharma policy can reverse or be diluted.** Section 232 exempts generics (where China dependence is deepest) and gives 0–20% rates to companies that do deals [src-20260402-ecae19]. A federal court enjoined WuXi's 1260H designation (search summary; low). The cheaper Indian and Chinese supply still exists.
5. **Capex before contracts again.** Chemical plants take years and hundreds of millions. Solugen's DOE loan is still conditional [src-20240613-2df5d6].
6. **Labor cost of US CROs.** Warp and Chemify must automate faster than offshore labs cut prices [inference].

- **FDA (APIs):** a standard generic application has a 10-month review goal [src-20261027-711e48], plus cGMP plant inspection (Phlow's 2027 timing [src-20260209-16223c]).
- **Pesticides:** new actives typically take 10–15 years and about $300M from discovery to market (**Claim, Enko CEO**) [src-20250601-f7467b].
- **EU REACH:** registration applies before selling into Europe [src-20251110-84b67e][src-20251218-f0d6ef].

**5-year outlook (to 2031).** _Pharma APIs and intermediates:_ **possible**. Phlow could supply in 2027 if FDA approves; policy creates buyers. _Fragrances and custom specialty:_ **likely** to be selling at meaningful scale (Osmo, Scimplify already sell). _Agrochemical actives:_ **unlikely** (registration timelines). _AI-designed new molecules at volume generally:_ **unlikely** outside fragrance, given TSCA and qualification. **Bear case, equal weight:** see 5.11. US specialty demand is flat, generics are outside the tariff, incumbents are qualified and buying AI tools, and the first AI-designed molecules sell mainly to small customers.

## 6. Advanced Materials

> **Our view: Advanced Materials** _(analyst judgment, not fact [inference])_
> - **Stance:** Selective. Back producers with an urgent buyer; watch discovery labs.
> - **Conviction:** low to medium. The needs are new (PFAS-free cooling, hypersonic alloys) but no AI-designed material sells at scale yet.
> - **Why:** (1) only the plant owner captures the margin at the supply step (6.4); (2) startups win where no incumbent is qualified (6.14); (3) liquid cooling is expected to reach 53% share in 2026 [src-20260817-13658d].
> - **Strongest counter:** qualification takes 1–5 years and can outlast runway; Chinese cathode prices cap margins; investors say there is "no AlphaFold moment" for materials yet [src-20260528-2c20ee].
> - **What would change our mind:** Orbital's fluid shipping with 2027 GPUs; a discovery lab selling material, not papers.
> - **Best placed / watch:** Orbital, Mitra (fully integrated); Radical (hybrid); CuspAI, Periodic, Lila (enablers).
> - **Good SPC fit:** Series A–B; makes its own product, or uses a contract maker while keeping the margin; buyer is a data-center, defense or battery customer already in qualification.

_What this industry is._ Advanced Materials covers companies that make and sell engineered materials rather than commodity molecules: battery cathodes, cooling fluids and hardware for data centers, sorbents, alloys and catalysts. Two producers sit here (Mitra Chem, Orbital Industries). AI discovery labs (Radical AI, CuspAI, Periodic Labs, Lila Sciences) are kept in a clearly labeled sub-group, **Materials Discovery Labs**: they invent materials with AI and robotic labs but mostly do not yet sell product at scale.

### 6.1 The problem today

- **China makes almost all battery materials.** In 2025 China made 70% of the world's electric vehicles, more than 80% of battery cells, about 85% of cathode active material and more than 90% of anode material [src-20260501-79ef76]. For the cheaper LFP (lithium iron phosphate) chemistry, more than 98% of cathode material and cells are made in China [src-20250521-9be25a].
- **The US is switching to the chemistry it makes least.** LFP was more than 55% of EV batteries worldwide in 2025. In the US, more than 50 GWh of cell capacity (LG Energy Solution, Ford) has been reassigned to LFP, much of it for grid storage, which was a third of US battery deployment; LFP cathode and its precursors are almost entirely made in China [src-20260501-600ef9].
- **Data centers need new cooling materials.** Data centers used 415 TWh of electricity in 2024 (1.5% of the world total), heading to about 945 TWh in 2030, growing about 15% a year; cooling takes from 7% (hyperscale) to over 30% (enterprise) of a data center's power [src-20250410-22e27a]. Liquid cooling is expected to reach 53% of AI chips in 2026 (med) [src-20260817-13658d].
- **The old fluorine chemistry is being regulated out.** Many cooling fluids, seals and coatings use PFAS ("forever chemicals"). EU scientific committees backed a broad PFAS restriction in March 2026, with targeted exemptions; fluoropolymers and fluorinated gases are in scope [src-20260326-379e42].
- **Discovery is cheap, scale-up is not.** AI shortens the first two steps of making a new material (weeks to months), but scale-up and customer qualification take one to five years each (6.6).

_What this means [inference]:_ there is real, policy-backed demand for a non-Chinese cathode and for new cooling materials, but the winner is whoever can make tons at a qualified spec, not whoever finds the molecule first.

**Battery chain, in more depth.** The IEA expects China still to hold about three-quarters of cathode and about 90% of anode production in 2035; the EU and US together reach only about 10% of cathode [src-20260501-79ef76]. China's edge in LFP comes from cheap inputs, equipment and patents [src-20250521-9be25a]. _Meaning [inference]:_ a US cathode maker sells mostly security of supply, so its customers are those who must or will pay for it (automakers with tax-credit rules, defense, utilities buying storage).

**Cooling, in more depth.** Accelerated (AI) servers are the fastest-growing slice of data-center load, and the US alone accounts for almost half of its electricity demand growth to 2030 from data centers [src-20250410-22e27a]. ### 6.3 Headwinds, at equal weight

1. **Chinese price.** China's scale sets cathode prices; a US plant is a price-taker without offtake or subsidy [src-20250521-9be25a] [inference].
2. **EV slowdown in the US.** US LFP share in EVs almost halved; capacity is being re-pointed to storage [src-20260501-600ef9].
3. **Scale-up and qualification take years** (6.6) and are where most money is spent.
4. **Rules can soften.** The US PFAS rollback shows regulation-driven demand can shrink [src-20260520-caa33e].
5. **Discovery-lab hype.** Large rounds before revenue (6.10); Plural, an Orbital investor, says there is "no AlphaFold moment" in materials [src-20260528-2c20ee].

### 6.4 Why an AI-enabled, vertically integrated company is the answer here

Owning the plant is the only way to capture the margin in step 5 (supply) of the materials chain; a lab that licenses an invention captures little [inference]. AI helps most where a producer can run many experiments on its own line: Mitra uses AI from lab scale (1 kg/day) to production (1,000 kg/day) [src-20261023-6e0642]. **Where it breaks:** discovery speed does not shorten customer qualification, and Chinese cost still sets price.

### 6.5 What would have to be true, and signals to watch

- A US cathode plant reaches steady output at a price buyers accept. **Watch:** Mitra's Muskegon plant start-up and offtakes [src-20261023-6e0642].
- Liquid cooling stays standard as AI chips grow. **Watch:** liquid-cooling share (53% expected in 2026) [src-20260817-13658d].
- The EU PFAS restriction is adopted. **Watch:** the final SEAC opinion and Commission proposal [src-20260326-379e42].
- A discovery lab sells product, not just papers. **Watch:** Periodic, Lila, CuspAI revenue from materials.

### 6.6 Macro in more depth

The steps, with typical time in brackets [inference, built from sources cited]:
1. **Compute screen** (weeks): the model narrows millions of candidates to hundreds. Now cheap.
2. **Synthesis and first tests** (months): grams to 500 g in an SDL. This is the step labs speed up 6–17 times. An RSC benchmark finds a median speedup of about 6 times over random sampling (search; low), and Lila claims 17 times [src-20260925-679418].
3. **Scale-up** (1–3 years): kilograms to tons. It needs process chemistry or metallurgy, and a pilot line. Radical's RAI-939 is at this step now [src-20260807-52b690].
4. **Qualification** (1–5 years): the customer tests the material in its own product against specs. Aerospace alloys need allowables databases. Chip fluids need qualification by the fab or GPU maker (Orbital, 2027 target [src-20260528-375aec]). Water sorbents need regulatory approval (Kemira).
5. **Supply** (ongoing): someone has to make it reliably at cost. This is where the margin is, and where owner-producers sit.

**Where AI helps:** step 1 a lot, step 2 a lot, steps 3–5 little. Steps 3–5 are most of the time and money. That is why Plural says there is "no AlphaFold moment" [src-20260528-2c20ee], and why the Lila CTO says only about 5% of science is cheap to verify [src-20260917-29aa29].

**Materials discovery, step by step.**

A new material (an alloy, a battery cathode, a catalyst, a coolant, a magnet) normally takes 10–20 years and tens of millions of dollars to go from idea to qualified product. Discovery labs try to compress that with three tools:
1. **Generative and predictive models.** Software proposes candidate structures and predicts their properties, so you test hundreds instead of millions. Examples:
   - Microsoft's MatterGen is a diffusion model trained on about 608k stable materials. It generates crystal structures to a target property [src-20250116-63e766][src-20261022-3e2a9d].
   - Google DeepMind's GNoME predicted 2.2M crystals, about 380k of them stable. Outside labs have since made 736 of them [src-20231129-1dc706].
   - Meta's open OMat24 dataset holds more than 110M quantum-mechanics (DFT) calculations [src-20130101-5e5543].
2. **Self-driving labs (SDLs).** Robots mix, synthesize and test the candidates, and the results feed back into the model. Examples are Berkeley's A-Lab, Argonne's Polybot [src-20250217-059784] and Radical AI's alloy lab.
3. **The closed loop.** Model proposes, robot makes and measures, model updates. The claim is that each turn makes the next guess better, so the lab gets faster over time. This is the materials version of the "data loop" in section 6.

**The key point [inference]:** models are becoming free. GNoME, MatterGen, OMat24 and the Genesis Mission platform are all public or close to it. The scarce assets are (a) physical throughput, meaning how many real samples you can make and test, (b) data that is proprietary and tied to real-world performance, and (c) the ability to scale up and qualify a material with a customer. That is where the right to win sits.

**Regulation and qualification.** Customer qualification takes 1–5 years (6.6). Battery products also need safety certification; full UL compliance typically takes 12–20+ weeks (low; vendor source) [src-20250101-040756]. New chemistries also face TSCA review (5.14).

**5-year outlook (to 2031).** _Producers:_ **possible** for one or two names (Orbital if its fluid qualifies in 2027; Mitra if its plant is funded). _Discovery labs:_ **unlikely** to be selling material at scale. **Bear case, equal weight:** see 6.11. Qualification can outlast a startup's runway, Chinese cathode pricing caps margins, and discovery labs may end up as royalty businesses, not producers.

## 7. Enabling Software & Robotics (tracked, outside the core)

> **Our view: Enabling Software & Robotics** _(analyst judgment, not fact [inference])_
> - **Stance:** Watch, not invest yet; track as benchmarks and as tools inside producers.
> - **Conviction:** medium. The tools matter, but selling them alone is a smaller, more competitive business (7.4).
> - **Why:** (1) traction is first-customer stage (Arda: Anduril only [src-20261020-a1e174]; Mind: Rivian [src-20261013-cc78c0]); (2) GrayMatter pivoted to robots as a service after software-only sales did not work [src-20261022-3335bd]; (3) incumbents own the installed base.
> - **Strongest counter:** an enabler that becomes the standard for thousands of small factories could beat owning plants (Arda's bet).
> - **What would change our mind:** retention data from Arda; robot success above ~90% in real work; producers licensing their software (MasonOS, MarianaOS).
> - **Best placed / watch:** Arda, Mind, GrayMatter, Lumaril (enablers).
> - **Good SPC fit:** only where it is tied to owned capacity, e.g. robot cells as a service with usage pricing.

_What this industry is._ Enabling Software & Robotics covers companies that sell tools to factories rather than making the product themselves: factory operating systems, inventory and ordering software, inspection AI, CAM automation and robots. We track it because it sets the pace for the producers in chapters 3–6, but it sits **outside the core thesis** (it is an enabler, not an owner of output).

### 7.1 The problem today

- **Factory workflows are slow and manual.** **Claim (Accel, on Arda):** assembly instructions drop from hours to minutes [src-20261020-8d7abc].
- **The US is under-automated.** A record 603,307 robots were installed worldwide in 2025; China installed 354,226 and Chinese brands supply over half of China's installs; the US installed 38,428 [src-20260924-28b59b], only about 3,000 in metal and machinery, at a density of 307 per 10,000 workers versus 1,220 in South Korea [src-20260618-c903cc].
- **Robots still fail often:** robot-learning success is 59–66% on short tasks versus 99.9%+ needed [src-20260904-267293].

### 7.3 Headwinds, at equal weight

1. **Small factories buy slowly** and churn [inference].
2. **Incumbents bundle software** with hardware (Siemens, Rockwell) [src-20250326-cc1810][src-20260804-0e8130].
3. **Reliability gap** in robot learning [src-20260904-267293]; **Claim (Mehr):** physical AI is at "2007-2008 of LLMs" [src-20260220-0d5345].
4. **Rates and capex caution** (2.5).
5. **Value capture:** a vendor never owns the output margin, and owner-operators may build their own software [inference].

### 7.4 Why vertical integration matters here, and where the logic breaks

- Software and robots are the **means** by which the producers in chapters 3–6 win; selling them separately is a smaller, more competitive business [inference].
- An enabler can still win as the standard for thousands of small factories (Arda's bet).
- **Where it breaks:** if owner-operators build in-house, the enabler market shrinks to slow-buying job shops.

### 7.5 What would have to be true, and signals to watch

- Usage- or outcome-based pricing sticks. **Watch:** Arda and Lumaril retention (claims only so far).
- Robot success rates rise above ~90% in real work.
- Producers license out their software (MasonOS, MarianaOS).

**Who buys.** Primes (Lockheed, RTX, Northrop, Boeing, L3Harris, General Atomics), new primes (Anduril), depots and programs (Army, Navy, DIU), and commercial hardware firms (SendCutSend serves 60% of the Fortune 500) [src-20261020-487a39].

**What buyers told expert calls (med).** Price evidence is in the decision summary; the rest:
- Willingness to pay for speed: a Northrop engineer would pay 20-40% more to avoid a delay [src-20260802-36d46c]; ex-customer FlyX says "10%-15% is tops" and left over cost when work became "less stringent" [src-20260806-3a2ec7].
- Share today: Hadrian holds 2-3% of one Boeing unit's precision spend and under 10-20% of a Northrop unit's outsourced work [src-20260802-ef6506][src-20260802-36d46c]. Big buyers move work in-house after a "honeymoon period" [src-20250729-a0a78c].
- Raytheon reconciles "cheaper" with Boeing's "dearer at volume" if Hadrian prices low on small urgent jobs [inference].

**Qualification: a tax at entry, a moat once paid.** 9-12 months at Raytheon [src-20250708-2aa3ad], 1-2 years at Northrop [src-20260802-36d46c]; **Claim (Power):** full aerospace trust takes 7-8 years [src-20260908-65fab6]. Amca buys qualification by acquiring shops [src-20260520-af3e6c][inference].

**Repeat orders in federal data.** Firestorm alone climbed the full ladder (SBIR, then Phase III IDIQ, then 19 task orders, $53.8M) [src-20251022-629fc0][src-20251219-bd5788]. Hadrian's Army award ($39.2M obligated of $80M, one bid) is recorded as "DATA RIGHTS AND INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY", not plainly parts [src-20260317-5585be][src-20260317-77e798]. Mariana, Arda, 1872 and Nox: no prime awards found.

### 9.1 How buyers qualify suppliers and reorder, step by step

- **The ladder.** "A PO for six months ... If they can do it well, giving another PO for two years"; prototypes are bought on lead time, production on price [src-20250623-5bc67c]. Any change of design, material, process or site repeats first-article inspection [src-20190801-e81d7b].
- **Probation is small.** Raytheon spend on Hadrian is "in the tens of thousands ... on purpose", with "tens of millions" possible later [src-20260823-b3713f].
- **Buyers cap each supplier** at about 25-30% and want about 25% spare capacity [src-20250623-5bc67c].
- **"Field of dreams."** The Pentagon "cannot actually sign a contract until they have that physical facility built" [src-20260325-7ba845]; Lockheed: the government does not "want to buy factories. We want to buy weapons" [src-20261014-47f64b].
- **Base rates.** About 5% of companies go from SBIR Phase II to Phase III [src-20240129-b46554]; Castelion calls the roughly 3-year gap the "valley of death" [src-20251010-d50ea5]. Ursa Major's $137M and Divergent's ~$3.64M Air Force awards are development-scale [src-20261014-08000a][src-20250924-22c5ee].
- **What this means [inference].** Most new producers are on the lower rungs: probation and small repeat POs.

## 13. Bottom line

**Five conclusions.**
1. The category is real, but proven demand is narrow: speed on prototypes and low-rate work, at 2-10% of spend at the primes we spoke to [src-20260802-ef6506][src-20260802-36d46c].
2. The data loop has partial evidence (score 2) only where the process is continuous (Freeform, Mariana, Radical). Nobody shows improvement over time.
3. Capital falls into three tiers: capital-light owner-operators (SendCutSend, Senra), debt- and government-funded heavy builders (Hadrian, Vulcan, Mariana), and software vendors that need someone else's plant (Arda, Mind) [inference].
4. Precision & Defense Hardware is priced out at the top (Hadrian $7.87B [src-20260807-b0dc5b], Amca over $1B [src-20260520-af3e6c]); SPC's entry is Series B in fabrication or a hybrid/franchise model [inference].
5. Policy money lands mostly on metals supply (OSC, tariffs, DPA) [src-20251103-ee9b27][src-20261019-68fd3d]; precision parts get their pull through prime contracts [inference].

**Picks [inference].** Fabrication: Senra. Precision: Freeform; Isembard (hybrid). Metals: Vulcan and Mariana as benchmarks. Materials R&D: Radical and Periodic. Factory software: Arda and Mind as benchmarks.

**Bias check.** Senra (10 sources), Freeform (14) and Isembard (13) are among the least-covered names (Hadrian 76). Each pick rests mostly on company claims; treat all three as diligence hypotheses.

**What would change our mind.** A prime shows a new producer at over 20% of rate-production spend at a lower unit price (bull); a company publishes scrap, cycle time or quote accuracy improving with volume (bull); Section 232 tariffs are rolled back (bear); a tracked owner-operator raises down or stalls a plant (bear).

### 13.1 The bear case in one list

Points already made above are listed by section.
- Buyers pay parity or more, and speed matters to few (decision summary 1).
- The model has failed recently: Daedalus, Bright Machines, Markforged (section 8).
- Quality on complex parts is unproven and regulators tighten after Boeing's failures: "Innovation ... is the first thing that gets sacrificed" [src-20260818-9b2d5c].
- The AI is thinner than the pitch: Opus relies on outside CAM [src-20261004-9b47f5]; robot learning succeeds about 60% of the time versus 99.9% needed [src-20260904-267293]; A-Lab's 41 new compounds were challenged [src-20240124-513fd9]; CloudNC sells AI CAM to 1,000+ shops including Lockheed's [src-20260908-f98d21].
- Valuations run ahead of audited revenue (Hadrian $7.87B, Mind $3.4B, Periodic about $7.5B reported); plants built ahead of contracts are exposed to a defense or tariff reversal [src-20260921-876080].
- Spare capacity exists: existing spindles cut metal only 34% of the time [src-20250120-419768].
- Share per customer is capped and repeat orders are tiny (sections 9 and 9.1).
- Defense demand is lumpy and budget-gated: Carpenter's defense bookings are down because funds haven't reached customers [src-20261022-de5e6c]; 82% of the FY27 munitions request rests on reconciliation [src-20261007-c6f64e].
- Incumbents are booming: Carpenter's specialty-alloy margin hit 37.7% [src-20261022-679fcc]; Reliance is taking share with no AI [src-20261022-1e6872].
- Teams churn: 90% of Hadrian's first 10 employees left by year 5 [src-20260817-8867f2]. Some see a debt business, not a venture one [src-20250709-43486e].
- Commodity risk: Ganfeng's market value fell more than 75% with lithium prices [src-20250529-3a588b].

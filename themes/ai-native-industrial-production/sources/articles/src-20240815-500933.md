Now he’s got plans to build a network of them across the country to make more parts better, faster and cheaper. By Amy Feldman , Forbes Staff A t Hadrian’s Torrance, California factory, the machines keep working after their operators go home. Swing by after hours and you’ll see them, displays aglow as they crank out parts in the dark. “We’re running 24-7. There’s a crew of people during the day and then it just runs itself,” said Chris Power, Hadrian’s 33-year-old founder and CEO. “That’s what our software does for manufacturing.” Power’s vision is somewhat different from other startups building tech to make other companies’ factories more efficient: He’s developed proprietary software to run Hadrian’s own factory, which allows it to churn out all types of precision metal parts for aerospace, space and defense companies faster, more efficiently and with fewer people. Now he wants to build out a network of cookie-cutter, high-tech machine shops across the country to shake up a giant and fragmented industry. That’s a difficult and capital-intensive project, but one that Power argues is crucial for America’s industrial base. Investors, including Andreessen Horowitz, Founders Fund and Lux Capital, have bet $180 million on it, and the company is now worth roughly $500 million. While the three-year-old business is still small, having just launched commercially in 2023, it’s growing fast. It brought in $3 million in revenue last year, and Power expects to increase that number 10-fold this year, reaching at least $30 million, as it ramps up production from its factory near Los Angeles. “I get bored of things going too slowly,” he said. Those numbers helped Hadrian make the cut for this year’s Next Billion-Dollar Startups list, Forbes’ annual showcase of the 25 companies we think most likely to reach a $1 billion valuation. There are huge risks to building a new high-tech manufacturing business. Metal parts that go in rockets or satellites need to be extremely precise, yet production quantities of each part are very limited. That’s very different from automotive factories, where manufacturers churn out standardized parts in vast quantities. As a result, many machine shops for such specialized parts have stayed small, despite the industry being as large as $50 billion. Hadrian currently produces thousands of parts each month, comprising hundreds of unique ones, Power said. “There is certainly an opportunity to improve the speed and efficiency with which parts are made,” said John Hart, a professor of mechanical engineering at MIT and director of its Laboratory for Manufacturing and Productivity. “That said, machining is still really complicated.” Hadrian’s software is what enables its existing factory to make spaceflight-grade parts 10 times faster and more than 40% more efficiently than legacy manufacturers. That software allows Hadrian to take legacy designs or CAD files from customers and turn them into digital instructions to make finished parts. It also operates as a brain for the factory, letting it track and schedule jobs, and allows the machines to run on autopilot and be inspected autonomously. That permits Hadrian to produce parts at a speed that has its customers excited — this is a market where the delay in one small part can mess up an entire rocket launch or postpone delivery of a needed fighter jet. “I think we’re just at the early innings of how to change the whole industrials market into a technology market,” said Dayna Grayson, cofounder of Construct Capital, an investor in Hadrian and other manufacturing startups. “I’m a big student of history. I realized there was a huge geopolitical risk about to hit the U.S.” Power said that the need for a more efficient machine shop is so great that Hadrian has been growing at rates of 20% or more each month, despite not having set up a sales team until this spring. Now he wants to build out additional factories, speeding towards his vision of a network of automated plants across the country that can, as he sees it, help the U.S. maintain its industrial power against China. “If you want something made, we’ll make it for you, higher-quality, faster and cheaper,” Power said. P ower grew up in Melbourne, Australia, a world away from Silicon Valley. A dropout of Monash University, he took a job with a retail firm called Retail Splash, worked at a few other startups and then joined employee-engagement software firm Ento as head of growth in 2015. He came to the United States in 2019, drawn by the bigger opportunities for someone with an entrepreneurial bent. By then, he’d developed some strong opinions about America’s declining industrial base – and how critical reversing that trend was with China rising. “I’m a big student of history. I realized there was a huge geopolitical risk about to hit the U.S.,” said Power, whose bio on X identifies him as “industrialist, technologist, believer in hard power and Pax Americana.” After visiting a bunch of small, old-school machine shops run by workers in their 50s and 60s, he realized his fear of a decline of American industry was “horribly right.” He set up a small San Francisco-based investment firm called ADSC in July 2019 with the idea of buying up existing mid-sized firms in the aerospace and defense supply chain. ADSC raised approximately $650,000 from institutional funds-of-funds and wealthy individuals for acquisitions. But as the pandemic hit in 2020 and he continued to visit machine shops and small manufacturers, Power became convinced that trying to change existing companies wouldn’t solve the problem he saw. So in 2021, Power started Hadrian, named for the Roman emperor’s efforts to fix his civilization’s crumbling infrastructure. The vast majority of investors in ADSC rolled their equity over to Hadrian, he said. “He’s an outsider,” said Brandon Reeves, a partner at Lux Capital who’s been invested in Hadrian since its seed funding. “He networked himself into Silicon Valley and became very plugged in.” To bring his idea for an automated factory to life, Power reached out to Chris Baker, who previously managed machine shops for SpaceX. “I thought it was truly impossible to get this thing and all the parameters figured out,” Baker said. “He was extremely tenacious, very pushy and continued to engage with me even while I was telling him the thing he was trying to do was going to fail.” After six months of talking, Power convinced Baker to join his startup as VP of operations. “He’s an outsider. He networked himself into Silicon Valley and became very plugged in.” Hadrian first set up a small, 20,000-square-foot plant, then Power realized he needed a larger factory and built out the existing 100,000-square-foot factory in Torrance. The factory combines robots he bought from other companies, like Kuka robotic arms, with Hadrian’s proprietary software system to manage operations. “Imagine if you put Tesla autopilot on an F1 car so you could drive it with 15 minutes of training,” Power said. “That’s what our software does for manufacturing.” To convince its first customers to trust a small, VC-backed startup, Power lured two companies that are now major customers (that he declines to name) with a no-risk pitch. “We basically said, Hey, we’re going to make a bunch of parts, but we’re in beta test,” he said. “It may not work, but if it does work, we’re going to be your best supplier—the fastest, cheapest and best quality. Will you give us the data to run the factory in beta mode undercover for 2022 and 2023?” The companies agreed to give them plans for parts so Hadrian could test out its systems, and the startup spent the next nine months making parts on its own dime. “It took nine months of running the factory and fighting fires every day to go from, ‘We think the system works’ to ‘It actually works,’” Power said. “We made probably $1 million worth of parts and destroyed them just to test the systems ... .In our industry you cannot fail so we had to test the factory running at full tilt.” The ploy worked and the beta customers signed on. Since then, Hadrian has signed up some 30 customers, including top space companies, defense startups and major defense companies, Power said. He declined to name any customers, citing confidentiality agreements. “It’s a very clandestine industry,” he said. RTX Ventures, the venture arm of RTX, the aerospace and defense giant formerly known as Raytheon Technologies, invested in Hadrian early this year. Daniel Ateya, managing director of RTX Ventures, said that RTX is evaluating a number of parts that Hadrian could supply to it. “From a national security perspective, these supply chain bottlenecks are really constraining,” he said. “We feel there is a lot of opportunity for Hadrian to eliminate the bottlenecks and constraints that currently exist.” He declined to comment on which specific parts Hadrian might supply or which RTX products they might go into, but noted there’s potential across RTX’s business and in aerospace and defense more broadly. Katherine Boyle, a partner at Andreessen Horowitz, who is invested in the company, said that Hadrian is on the radar of top Defense Department officials, many of whom have visited its factory. “They understand that the defense industrial base is crucial,” she said. “This was a focal point for DoD long before it was talked about in Silicon Valley and in Torrance and in Los Angeles.” “From a national security perspective, these supply chain bottlenecks are really constraining.” The company employs just 170 workers, who it can train in 30 to 60 days to run its machines. Some of them run 10 machines at a time. All receive equity in the firm. Many of them have switched from other careers, including nursing and retail. That’s a potential advantage at a time when manufacturers have struggled to find workers. “We want to bring people into manufacturing who’ve never set foot inside a factory before,” Power said. He has big plans for the future, like building out a network of automated factories across the country as fast as possible — a must, he believes, to position America to win in the competition with China. He’s already looking for a location for the next factory – perhaps in Texas, Arizona or Virginia – which he expects to open in 2025. That facility will be larger than the Torrance one, potentially three times the size, with the capacity to produce $200 million in revenue, he said. “Chris is pushing for an insane amount of speed,” VP of operations Baker said. The rollout accelerates after next year. Starting in 2026, Power is aiming for two or three new factories a year, a pace he figures is doable because the software and factory setup will be the same. “Factories two through N are rinse-and-repeat,” said investor Reeves. Speed is hard in a complex, capital-intensive business like manufacturing. But Power sees a window now. He figures that either the U.S. continues to outsource its manufacturing to low-cost countries or it figures out how to use automation to enable factories in the U.S. to compete.

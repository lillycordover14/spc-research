When Silicon Valley Meets the Shop Floor in Michigan
            A surprising collaboration between Silicon Valley tech and Michigan moldmakers uses AI to improve traditional moldmaking processes.

Who would have thought? A Silicon Valley software engineer with no manufacturing experience teams up with seasoned moldmakers in Michigan to rethink an industry built on decades of hands-on skill. This unlikely partnership is at the heart of Atomic Industries, a Macomb County company proving that technology and tradition can work together; not against each other.
It started when the engineer (and now Atomic CEO Aaron Slodov), trying to bring a consumer product to market, ran into the familiar roadblocks of long lead times, high costs and inconsistent results. Instead of walking away, he saw an opportunity to apply AI — physics simulations, algorithmic design and machine learning — to make tooling as flexible as code.

By pairing his background in AI and software with the deep expertise of veteran toolmakers like Lou Young Jr., Atomic’s Co-Founder and Head of Manufacturing, they set out to capture decades of moldmaking knowledge in code. Thinking outside the box, they turned a personal setback into an exciting experiment: what if a coder and a master craftsman could train software to think like both?
The partnership began with Slodov’s idea to use real manufacturing data in AI models, which needed Young’s expertise to train the systems properly. This combination of technology and manufacturing began remotely in LA, grew with the help of Y Combinator, received funding from Toyota Ventures and Porsche Ventures, and eventually moved to Warren, MI, to be near suppliers, labor and customers.
The result is autonomous mold design. Using physics-based simulations and machine learning with real mold data, the system makes complex decisions — like where to place waterlines, how to optimize cooling and where to put ejectors — in seconds instead of days. Human workers still make important decisions, such as where to place gates and parting lines, but routine tasks are automated, allowing people to focus on more valuable work.
This approach addresses some of the biggest challenges in moldmaking: fewer available skilled workers, flat wages despite high demand and baby boomers retiring. Shops need to find ways to be more efficient, and Atomic’s technology is an option for speeding up production, improving quality and making complicated tasks easier for smaller teams to handle.
The real breakthrough isn't just about speed or accuracy; it's about the mindset. By bringing together different areas that don’t usually connect, Atomic is demonstrating how these surprising combinations can lead to new ideas, even in traditional methods. Atomic says this isn't about taking away skilled work; it's about expanding it, keeping it alive and ensuring it lasts for future generations.

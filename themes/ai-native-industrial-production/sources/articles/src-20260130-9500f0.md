Meet 
Atomic Industries
, where Silicon Valley intersects with advanced manufacturing. Atomic CEO Aaron Slodov came to the manufacturing world from a software and startup background. His journey began when he wanted to create his own plastic consumer product and discovered the complex process of finding a mold shop, building a mold and producing parts. This experience revealed the significant inefficiencies in traditional manufacturing processes. Atomic was born.
However, startups in hard tech face significant challenges. “Venture capitalists are more inclined to invest in software-as-a-service companies with rapid scaling potential,” Slodov says. The team pushed ahead connecting venture capital, startups and technology providers to promote innovation in hard tech and manufacturing. One of the company's key initiatives is focused on reindustrialization. They are actively working to bring manufacturing capabilities back to the U.S. by leveraging advanced technology.

Atomic is building technology to autonomously design and build plastic injection molds, representing a fundamental shift in manufacturing methodology. “The approach goes beyond traditional toolmaking, focusing on creating injection molds that operate 50% more efficiently, are built 50% faster and cost 50% less,” Lou Young, co-founder and head of manufacturing, says.
Unlike conventional manufacturers, they aim to use this technology to scale
Developing physics-driven mold design technology, the company streamlines high-volume production — eliminating complex kick-offs and lengthy design reviews — while enabling software that learns from fabrication to design molds optimized for the exact strategies used on the shop floor.
manufacturing capabilities and bring more production in-house. Building manufacturing infrastructure is costly — Atomic has spent $8 million setting up its test bed, which is more about R&D than direct revenue generation.
The team currently employs about 45 people, with approximately 16 being software engineers. This includes PhD-level mathematicians and engineers creating new methodologies for optimizing waterlines, gate locations, ejection layouts and parting lines. Their approach blends software expertise with deep manufacturing knowledge.
By merging advanced software, AI and traditional manufacturing techniques, Atomic is creating a new approach that could dramatically improve efficiency, reduce costs and transform manufacturing.
The Goal
The company is working to build technology that leverages actual “physics” in the tool design decisions.  They aim to simplify high-volume part production by eliminating the need for every tool to undergo complex kick-off and lengthy design review meetings before fabrication can begin.  “This approach is particularly challenging in industries like automotive, where business practices are deeply protective and resistant to change,” Young says.
On top of that, global manufacturing presents significant challenges. “For example, Chinese tool shops can deploy 200 designers simultaneously, completing 60 mold designs in a matter of days. In contrast, the largest tool shop in North America has fewer than 30 mold designers,” Young says. Atomic sees technology as the key to competing, developing software that can automate and optimize design processes that typically require large teams.
The company's goal involves vertically integrating mold building into high-volume part production, transforming tooling from a quoted cost to an operating expense. This approach allows Atomic to quote customers directly for parts without including traditional tooling costs.
The Software
At the core of their process is an advanced AI system that designs tools with perfectly optimized gating, waterlines and ejector systems.
“By automating and optimizing tool design, we enable tools to run 30-50% more efficiently in terms of cycle time, quality and scrap rate. The AI not only designs tools but also determines the manufacturing strategy, ensuring automated work cells can produce components with minimal human intervention,” Young says. This approach eliminates the significant manual efforts traditionally required in toolmaking, from design to programming cutter paths and managing work cells.
A critical component of the Atomic approach is comprehensive data collection and optimization. They have set up testbed facilities with embedded sensors in injection molds, collecting real-world data to validate and refine their algorithms. These sensors measure everything from plastic flow and ejection forces to internal mold temperatures, creating a sophisticated feedback loop that continuously improves their design processes.
While much of today's AI in manufacturing relies on training models from existing data, this approach creates its own optimized datasets. When training machine learning models for waterline design, for example, the team doesn't use existing waterline designs from the industry — because those aren't perfectly optimized. Instead, they generate perfect optimization data using physics solvers, then train machine learning models on that idealized data, according to Young.
Automating tool design enables 30–50% greater efficiency, improving cycle times, quality and reducing scrap rates significantly.
This approach tackles different aspects of mold design with specialized algorithms. Cooling system optimization uses heat transfer and fluid flow equations while considering manufacturability constraints — can this hole be drilled, can this geometry be 3D printed, am I getting too close to a part surface? Ejector pin placement runs what they call a "sticky map algorithm" that predicts where a part will stick in the mold, then uses machine learning to determine optimal pin placement underneath ribs or screw bosses rather than in dead spots.
The software doesn't just design — it simultaneously develops manufacturing strategies. “As it designs a slide with a waterline system, it understands the machining requirements, the setup procedures and the sequence of operations needed to manufacture that component. This eliminates the traditional bottleneck where CAM programmers must analyze each tool design and develop individual manufacturing strategies,” Young says.
This obviously represents a huge shift. "Of course, humans can optimize a mold design. It just takes weeks and weeks and weeks. The throughput of mold design rooms would drop to near zero if they perfectly optimized every tool because they'd have to go back to simulation and move a waterline, then go back to simulation and move the waterline again,” Young says.
The Strategy
Rather than selling software or molds to customers, their strategy focuses on vertical integration. "Our business model is to build all of this technology around tool design and tool building, build these automated work cells and then vertically integrate that tool building capability into producing parts. Every tool that we can put into production grows our business. The faster we can build them, the quicker we grow," Young explains.
This approach eliminates the traditional separation between moldmaker and molder. A new 71,000-square-foot production facility houses 14 injection molding machines ranging from 25 tons to 2,000 tons, all fed by tools designed and built using AI-optimized processes. “We want to quote piece prices without separate tooling charges, rolling mold costs into production pricing because the tools can be manufactured so efficiently,” Young says.

“When manufacturing strategies are automatically generated, there's no bottleneck waiting for CAM programming. When cooling systems are perfectly optimized, cycle times drop by 20-40%,” Young says.
This vertical integration model also addresses a fundamental industry frustration. "Everybody hates the tooling part of manufacturing. You're like, 'Oh my God, I got to build a tool for this? It's going to take 20 weeks, and the mold shop's going to rake me over the coals.' We ultimately want to take the pain and the magic out of the tool building and just make people's parts," Young says.
The Implications
The downstream effects of this technology extend throughout the manufacturing supply chain. Young suggests, for example, that traditional injection molders will compete against companies that must quote both piece prices and tooling costs versus those who can quote piece prices alone because their tooling costs approach zero.
"If I'm bidding against somebody who has to also turn in a quote for a tool, I could quote someone 250 for a piece price, and Company B quotes 250 piece price plus a $300,000 mold. That's disruptive to the whole industry," Young says.
The technology doesn't replace human expertise entirely. Mold designers remain involved as the software develops, providing oversight and handling aspects not yet fully automated, like automatic parting line generation and die draw determination. However, advancements point to increasing automation of these functions. Mold builders willing to evolve may find opportunities in serving the technology companies developing these systems or specializing in complex applications where human expertise remains necessary.

The Future
By merging advanced software, AI and traditional manufacturing techniques, Atomic is creating a new approach that could dramatically improve efficiency, reduce costs and transform manufacturing.
Atomic’s approach centers on data: testbed facilities with sensor-equipped molds collect real-world insights to validate and refine optimization algorithms.
“The potential market is enormous. The injection molding industry alone is nearly a trillion dollars, with similar markets in high-pressure die casting and sheet metal. Atomic software could potentially work across multiple industries, offering a versatile solution to manufacturing challenges,” Young says.
However, success will be measured not by software capabilities but by results. Can these perfectly optimized molds actually deliver 20-40% cycle time improvements? Do they really produce good parts on first shots? Can manufacturing strategies generated by software actually drive lights-out manufacturing? The answers to these questions will determine success.
As one observer noted after seeing the technology demonstrated: "It seems like we've always solved one problem at a time and created these products and industries, and it looks like you're taking the time to solve it all with one." The message is clear: the future of manufacturing lies in embracing technological innovation that can dramatically improve efficiency, reduce costs and reshape entire industries.

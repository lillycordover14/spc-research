Two researchers who helped build ChatGPT and discover 2.2 million new crystal structures have left Silicon Valley's biggest labs to pursue a different kind of AI — one that doesn't just read what humans already know, but runs experiments to find what no one has found yet. Sources: NYT · TechCrunch · a16z Published: October 2025 Category: AI & Scientific Discovery $605M Total Raised — $300M Seed + Series A Now Closed $7.5B Reported Valuation 8 Months Later — Up from $1.3B 2.2M New Crystal Structures Found by Co-founder's Prior AI Ekin Dogus Cubuk Co-founder & CEO Former Google DeepMind GNoME · 2.2M Crystal Structures Liam Fedus Co-founder Former VP Research, OpenAI Co-creator of ChatGPT The Bet What Two Researchers Walked Away From — and Why Liam Fedus was, by any measure, at the top of his field. As VP of Research at OpenAI, he was part of the small team that built ChatGPT — the product that arguably launched the modern era of public AI. He left in early 2025. Around the same time, Ekin Dogus Cubuk departed Google DeepMind, where he had led the materials and chemistry research team that used AI to discover more than 2.2 million previously unknown inorganic crystal structures — roughly 800 years' worth of human scientific output compressed into a single project. The two had met while both were at Google, and what drew them together was a shared conviction: that the AI industry, for all its momentum, had taken a wrong turn. The dominant strategy at every major lab was to feed larger models more text from the internet and measure how well the systems could predict what came next. That approach had produced tools of genuine usefulness. But Fedus and Cubuk did not believe it would produce scientific discovery in any deep sense. They started Periodic Labs. What They Turned Down Mark Zuckerberg personally invited one of the researchers who eventually joined Periodic — Rishabh Agarwal, already a Meta employee — to join Meta's new superintelligence lab, offering millions in stock and salary. Agarwal declined, along with more than 20 other researchers from OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Meta who gave up tens of millions, in some cases hundreds of millions, in unvested compensation to join a startup that had not yet shipped a single product. The exodus was striking enough to draw wide attention in the industry. It reflected something beyond compensation math. The researchers who left were making a statement about what they thought mattered — and what they thought the largest AI labs were getting wrong. · · · The Core Argument Why Chatbots Alone Cannot Do Real Science The argument that Fedus and Cubuk are making is not that large language models are useless. It is that they have a structural limitation when it comes to scientific discovery specifically. Every major AI system today was trained on text — on books, papers, websites, and code that humans had already produced. That corpus, vast as it is, has a ceiling. The internet contains roughly ten trillion tokens of data. Once a model has learned what can be learned from that material, it is essentially reciting and recombining what humanity already knows. Science, as a practice, does not work by recombination. It works by confronting the unknown. A chemist does not discover a new superconductor by reading papers about superconductors. She forms a hypothesis, mixes specific compounds, heats them to specific temperatures, measures the results, and iterates — perhaps thousands of times — until something unexpected happens. The discovery lives in the gap between what the literature predicted and what the experiment actually produced. "The main objective of AI is not to automate white-collar work. The main objective is to accelerate science." — Liam Fedus, Co-founder, Periodic Labs A chatbot, Cubuk has argued, cannot make that kind of discovery by reasoning alone. It cannot sit and think its way to an unknown crystal structure any more than a human could. "Humans can't do that, either," he said. "They run many trial experiments before they find something incredible — if they even do." The implication is that any AI system meant to genuinely expand scientific knowledge has to be embedded in a cycle of physical experimentation, not just inference over existing text. Fedus was pointed in his assessment of how Silicon Valley's leading labs have described their scientific ambitions. The claim that large language models are already pushing toward breakthroughs in drug discovery, physics, and mathematics is, in his view, intellectually lazy. He invoked the memory of Bell Labs and IBM Research — institutions that treated the physical sciences not as an application layer for computing, but as the core mission. Periodic Labs is, in his framing, an attempt to revive that model. · · · The Method A Closed Loop: AI Conjectures, Robots Test, Models Learn The Periodic Labs method: an AI model generates hypotheses, a robotic system runs physical experiments, and the results — novel data not found anywhere on the internet — feed back into the model. The cycle repeats continuously. The architecture Periodic Labs is building can be described as a closed experimental loop. An AI system surveys the existing scientific literature and forms a hypothesis — a specific prediction about which combination of materials might produce a desired property, such as high-temperature superconductivity. That hypothesis is then handed to a robotic system in a physical laboratory, which carries out the actual experiment: selecting powders, mixing compounds, heating them in a furnace, and measuring the results with analytical instruments. The outcome — whether the experiment succeeded, partially succeeded, or failed — is fed back to the AI, which updates its model of the problem and generates the next hypothesis. The key insight is that this loop generates data that does not exist anywhere else. Unlike the internet, which is finite and increasingly well-scraped, physical experiments produce genuinely novel signal. Every run of every experiment is new information about how the physical world actually behaves. An AI trained on thousands of these experimental cycles is learning from a source that no prior model has had access to. First Target: Superconductors Periodic Labs has identified superconducting materials as its initial focus. A room-temperature superconductor — a material that conducts electricity with zero resistance at ordinary temperatures — would be among the most consequential materials discoveries in modern history. It would have applications in electrical grids, transportation, medical imaging, and next-generation computing. Decades of research have not produced one. Periodic is betting that automated experimentation at scale, guided by AI models, can find combinations and conditions that human-paced research has missed. The company's target customers are engineers and researchers in advanced industries — semiconductors, space, and defense — who, as Fedus put it, "don't really have particularly good tools" for materials discovery at speed. Robotics, in this model, are not a product. They are an instrument. The physical robots in Periodic's planned Menlo Park laboratory will not be sold or licensed; they will run experiments on behalf of the AI systems the company is building, generating proprietary data that forms the basis for the models' scientific learning. The distinction matters: Periodic is not a robotics company. It is an AI company that needs robots in order to do AI that is worth doing. The company began its work at a research laboratory in San Francisco with a team of roughly 32 people, a mix of AI researchers and experimental scientists. The Menlo Park facility, which will operate at larger scale, is planned for future construction. · · · Who Is Betting On It A $605 Million Wager on the Limits of Language Models Periodic Labs emerged from stealth in late September 2025 with a $300 million seed round — an amount that, at seed stage, has no precedent in the history of AI startup financing. Most seed rounds top out in the range of ten to twenty million dollars; a $300 million raise at the pre-product stage is more comparable in scale to late-stage growth investment. The round was led by Andreessen Horowitz and included participation from Felicis, DST Global, Accel, General Catalyst, and Nvidia's venture arm NVentures. The pre-money valuation was reported at $1.3 billion. The individual backers are notable on their own. Jeff Bezos, former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, and Google's Jeff Dean — the researcher credited with creating the foundational architecture of modern deep learning — all participated personally. The combination of institutional and individual backing reflects an unusual convergence of conviction about the thesis.

A consolidation cycle is about to begin and if you’re not in production already, it’s probably too late

Understanding the Current State of Play
We are entering the decisive phase of the defense reindustrialization cycle…and it is beginning to look like every other venture cycle. The field of early seed-stage entrants is being thinned, kingmakers are placing enormous bets on a small number of companies, and the anointed are using that capital to blitzscale, consolidate the market, and absorb or bury those left behind. The Department of War is reinforcing this dynamic, both implicitly and explicitly, through its contracting behavior. Innovation theater is giving way to real procurement, and the customer is not choosing 200 clever point solutions. It is choosing a handful of companies that can produce, deliver, field, and sustain capability at scale. Perfect tomorrow is the enemy of good enough today.
This weekend, I laid out how I think this will unfold—and the political, economic, and managerial realities driving it—in a long

 This Substack goes deeper on the first half of that argument: the investment environment, the defense-industry kingmaking now underway, and the Department’s desire to emerge from this recapitalization cycle with something closer to 20 prize fighters than 200 midgets. Part two will examine the structural forces making that outcome not just increasingly inevitable but a requirement from the standpoint of building a sustainable industrial base.
Defense Fundraising Cycle Peaking - changing from seeding starters to kingmaking
My favorite game in the casino is craps because the odds are dynamic and the menu of bets is almost endless. Depending on what has already been rolled (and your risk appetite) you can play everything from long-shot hardways and horn bets to the relatively conservative pass line. Investing works much the same way: different categories of investors offer different combinations of risk and return to satisfy the appetites of their Limited Partners (LPs). 
The graphic below is roughly how I picture that investing casino floor.
I don’t have a whole set of analysts to help me build graphics, so of course I used ChatGPT. If it’s screwed up, it’s Sam Altman’s fault. 
Defense is following the familiar pattern of an investment heat cycle. A handful of institutions lead the market, initially making the high-risk bets (the hardways and horn bets) and attracting the rest of the pack. As the table gets crowded, they pull back from the riskiest early-stage wagers and move toward the numbered bets. Eventually, many cash out and move on to the next hot table. Meanwhile, PE and public-market investors arrive later with much larger checks, concentrating on the safer parts of the table—the field and pass line—where returns may be lower, but the odds are considerably more dependable.
In 2022–23 the megafunds discovered defense optionality 
and

the broader venture trend of large funds moving into early stage reached defense and dual-use. As Anduril and Shield AI demonstrated venture-scale potential in 2020-2021, institutional investors increasingly placed smaller early bets to gain access to emerging companies—and preserve the ability to put substantially more capital behind the winners. For the non-VCs out there, these checks came with what’s called 
pro rata rights
 - essentially a call option to put more money in later if the company takes off. By 2023, 
Point72 was observing new “tier one venture investors” entering a category previously dominated by a relatively small group of defense-focused VCs
. Sequoia’s first hardware defense investment, 
a $5.7M seed in Mach Industries that was followed only four months later by a $79M Series A
, is a useful example of the shift.
The graphic below shows the “heat map” of early stage bets by these megafunds, along with some early shot callers like 8VC and Founders Fund who consistently lead the VC pack into new markets. Note how the wave of early investment really came in for these first movers about two years ago and now that others are coming in heavy as they are tapering off in early stage. 
This graphic is not precise but it’s directionally correct (any errors, once again blame Sam). Note that the time increments shrink from 2019 to today: there were as many bets placed in last 18 months as the five years before it combined.
Let’s look at this from a different angle: 
the amount of capital being deployed.
 The high rollers are now reaping the benefits of the 
pro rata
 bets they made at the early stage, which guaranteed them the ability to deploy serious capital behind the eventual winners. What we’re seeing now is pre-seed and seed activity beginning to taper off, with Series A through C following suit. Meanwhile, those same high rollers are writing massive checks into the handful of companies they believe will ultimately own the category.
The wave is starting to crest as growth bets pile up.
This is classic 
blitzscaling
 behavior, something we’ve seen repeatedly since Masa played kingmaker in the ’90s with SoftBank’s $100 million bet on Yahoo!. Capital stops being spread across the field and starts concentrating behind the presumed winners. 
The fact that we’re seeing that shift now is a pretty good indication that we’re near the top of the wave.
Enter the next wave: Reindustrialization
Meanwhile, a second wave of seed-stage bets was beginning to form. Coming out of COVID in 2021–22, companies like Hadrian, Bright Machines, Vulcan Forms and Varda (factories in space!) were attacking a problem the defense wave was beginning to expose: 
America doesn’t build stuff anymore.
This wave started roughly 12–18 months behind defense, but the playbook was fundamentally different from the one private equity firms like Arlington, Greenbriar, Tinicum and Warburg Pincus had been running for decades. PE bought Tier I and II aerospace and component suppliers, modernized them, consolidated them and eventually flipped them. The new venture-backed companies weren’t trying to fix Cold War-era industrial companies being sold by the Trustafarian children of the Greatest Generation who built them. 
They were starting with a blank sheet of paper and asking what the American industrial base should look like in 2026.
Reindustrialize wave is about 12-18 months behind the defense wave. Same graph shown for scale. Again, this is meant to be directionally correct rather than precise. 
That lag makes sense. Tier I suppliers and component manufacturers sit downstream from defense spending. The primes and neo-primes win the big contracts first; only then do they turn around and place orders for the components, subsystems and manufacturing capacity required to deliver them. 
Demand works its way down the bill of materials.
 The reindustrialization wave should therefore trail the defense wave—and that is roughly what the investment activity appears to show. The same investment heat map graph is shown above on the same scale.
Policy has amplified the demand signal. Tariffs, the Blue UAS/NDAA-compliance regime, contracting reforms intended to provide longer and more dependable demand to lower-tier suppliers, and restrictions on Chinese components (FCC covered list) have all made domestic production more valuable. In some categories—drone motors, controllers and batteries, for example—restrictions on Chinese suppliers have effectively created a temporary walled garden in which American manufacturers have a better chance to compete.
So just as the defense investment wave appears to be cresting, 
the reindustrialization wave is following close behind it.
 And you can increasingly see that shift at the earliest stages of the market: in what companies are getting formed, which founders are getting term sheets—and which ones aren’t.
A view of the future of the venture market in 2027. 
How is DoW accelerating this by picking winners and losers
There is universal consensus that six defense primes with 80%+ of the major acquisition programs is too few and that number has to grow. However, DoW has also made the command intent pretty clear: they don’t ultimately need 200 midgets—they need 20 prize fighters.
 The experimentation phase requires hundreds of startups competing for SBIRs, OTAs and prototype contracts, but wars aren’t fought with prototypes. Eventually somebody has to manufacture at scale, integrate increasingly complex systems, carry inventory, manage thousands of employees and suppliers, and deliver against multiyear programs. 
Depending on how you draw the line, there are probably only six to ten companies outside the traditional primes that have already demonstrated some combination of that capability—General Atomics, AeroVironment, Sierra Nevada, CACI, HII, SpaceX, Palantir, Leidos and Anduril are all potential candidates. The exact list is debatable; the larger point isn’t. 
If the destination is roughly 20 scaled competitors, there may only be room for another five to ten prize fighters to emerge from the hundreds of companies currently fighting their way up from the bottom.
 The Department’s acquisition reforms increasingly emphasize getting capability to the field at 
speed and scale
, while using multiyear contracts and other mechanisms that give industry enough demand certainty to build capacity.
What’s strange is how little the startup-defense conversation acknowledges 
private equity
, because PE has been placing billions of dollars of bets on essentially the same outcome from the opposite direction - and for longer. Furthermore, it has a number of $1 billion + exits in the last couple years to point to-  such as Aevex, StandardAero, Silvus and Blue Halo -  whereas most of the venture bets are years away from exit. 
A notional electronic warfare market map including examples from Private Equity owned, public companies and founder led. The venture backed companies are getting all the attention but they are merely the tip of the iceberg. 
 Venture investors are trying to turn startups into scaled defense companies; firms like Arlington and Greenbriar have spent years assembling existing aerospace, defense and government-technology businesses into larger platforms. Those two worlds increasingly look like they are headed toward each other. 
Not every promising startup can—or should—raise another $500 million venture round and attempt to become the next Anduril.
 For many of the other 190, the logical path may be consolidation: combine complementary products, contracts, manufacturing capacity and customer relationships until several smaller fighters collectively become one prize fighter.
That creates what I think is the overlooked 
second half of the defense-tech trade
. Venture capital finances the new technology and discovers the winners; private equity can provide the balance sheets and M&A machinery to consolidate the next tier. 
Eventually the distinction between a “venture-backed defense startup” and a “PE-backed defense platform” may matter a lot less to DoW than whether the company can reliably deliver thousands of systems on schedule.
 The other thing we are seeing is the emergence of publicly traded holding companies such as 
Ondas Inc
 who are also executing roll up strategies. These companies are hoping that they can become one of those prize fighters by leveraging public markets starved of exposure to “new defense” because of the lack of exits. 
 And importantly, the government doesn’t want consolidation everywhere: its recent opposition to 
TransDigm acquiring Stellant systems
 (which would have left the DoW with only one supplier for many categories of traveling wave tubes) shows that it still cares about maintaining competition and avoiding single-source component bottlenecks.
So there is some cognitive dissonance in the system: 
DoW wants fewer companies capable of operating at enormous scale, but enough of them to preserve real competition, 
i.e. more than the “big six” they have today. 
There’s no time in the Davidson Window to figure out production
While the DoW is trying to rebuild its industrial base around a broader set of scaled competitors (leveraging tens of billions of dollars of VC and PE capital to effectively unwind the consolidation megacycle that followed the end of the Cold War) the immediate problem hasn’t gone away. It still has the wrong mix of systems for many of today’s wars, and the clock on the Davidson Window - the period around 2027 when China could be capable of moving on Taiwan - is ticking louder and louder.

DoW simply cannot wait another 12–24 months for industry to sort itself out and grow into the demand.
 It has to place bets now on the companies that can manufacture at scale today and move out. Realistically, if you aren’t already in production (or very close to it) it may already be too late to compete for many of the programs of record being stood up between now and FY2027–29.
If you aren’t in production already, it’s probably too late
Even the new organizations created to break the old procurement model are beginning to exhibit this behavior.
 JIATF-401, the DoW organization charged with leading the counter-UAS mission, spent its first months aggressively testing and evaluating the ecosystem, and then began concentrating serious dollars behind the systems that worked. 
It selected Anduril’s Lattice as its enterprise tactical C2 platform
, placing an initial order worth up to roughly $103 million, and after operationally evaluating 
CACI’s SkyValor, put it into full-rate production under a three-year IDIQ with a $500 million ceiling
. 
Why did they do this?
 Because once you move from experimentation to deployment, the objective changes. The government may want hundreds of companies competing to discover the best technology, but it does not want to integrate, train, sustain and operate hundreds of bespoke systems at scale. Once winners emerge from operational testing, procurement naturally begins concentrating around the handful of platforms that can actually be fielded, integrated and supported. 
The funnel starts wide; the checks get written narrow.
In Conclusion
This gets to the fundamental lesson of the reindustrialization wave: 
the factory is the product.
 Anduril is ten years old now—it isn’t really a startup anymore—and the reason DoW is increasingly comfortable writing it enormous checks is not simply that its technology works; it is because the government increasingly believes 
Anduril can actually deliver it at scale and on time.
 For all the rhetoric around “commercial procurement practices,” lowering barriers to entry and bringing more startups into the industrial base, the ultimate selection criterion hasn’t changed much: 
Can these guys actually build what we need, in the quantities we need, when we need it?
 Commercial procurement may make it easier for a startup to get onto the field, but it doesn’t eliminate the requirement to perform once it gets there. 
The government can experiment with 200 companies; it can only depend on the handful that prove they can manufacture.
 That is why manufacturing capacity isn’t merely an operational advantage in defense—it increasingly becomes the moat.
What we are seeing in defense—and now, 12–18 months behind it, in reindustrialization—is not some new form of venture behavior. 
It is what venture capital always does.
 Capital initially spreads across a wide field of companies while investors search for product-market fit and category winners. Once those winners begin to separate from the pack, the game changes: fewer new seed bets, larger follow-on rounds, and enormous amounts of capital concentrated behind the companies investors believe can win. In other words, the defense VC cycle is no longer principally about 
finding
 the winners; it is increasingly about 
picking them and making sure they have enough capital to scale.
 Reindustrialization appears to be following the same curve, just a little later.
The government is sending much the same signal from the demand side. It wants more startups entering the funnel and more competition at the experimental stage, but ultimately it needs a much smaller number of companies capable of delivering meaningful quantities of equipment on operational timelines. DoW’s own acquisition reform language captures both sides of this apparent contradiction: broaden access to startups and nontraditional vendors, 
but ultimately deliver capability at 
“speed and scale.”
  Again, 
DoW may want 200 midgets competing in the preliminaries, but what it ultimately needs are 20 prize fighters it can put in the ring.
 JIATF-401 is already institutionalizing this process in counter-UAS: 
common testing standards create a broad competitive funnel
, while procurement mechanisms are designed to take technologies that prove themselves and rapidly field them at scale.
That is also why focusing exclusively on venture capital backed companies misses a large part of the opportunity. Not every one of those prize fighters has to be built organically from a VC-backed startup. 
Private equity can assemble one. Existing public companies can reinvent themselves into one. A traditional Tier II supplier can acquire its way up the stack.
 And the government itself can help create one through multiyear contracts, production investments and sufficiently durable demand signals. In fact, DoW simultaneously 
says it needs more entrants into the industrial bas
e 
and

more manufacturing capacity
—two objectives that make sense together if the broad funnel ultimately feeds a smaller group of scaled suppliers.
Because at some point procurement stops being a venture-capital exercise. The question is no longer 
who has the coolest technology?
 It becomes: 
Who can give me 5,000 (or 5 million!) of these, at this price, by this date, and who am I willing to bet the mission on?
 At that point factories, supply chains, quality systems, working capital, integration and past performance matter at least as much as the underlying technology. Commercial procurement practices make it easier for new companies to get into the tournament; they don’t make the government ignore execution risk. 
At scale, sometimes you simply have to pick the guy who has shown he can deliver.

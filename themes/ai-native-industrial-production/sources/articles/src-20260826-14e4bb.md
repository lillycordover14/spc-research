The robots are ready. Are the assemblers?
Why the next decade of robotics belongs to the builders
Emergence Team
Aug 26, 2026
4
Share
For sixty years, the promise of robotics was always just out of reach. The hardware was too expensive relative to the labor it aimed to replace. A welding robot in 2015 cost 
roughly half a year’s salary
 for the welder it was replacing, before integration and tooling were factored in. The average industrial robot cost 
about $46,000 in 2010
. By 2022, it was $23,000.
Foundation models have since accelerated deployment dramatically. Deployments powered by Physical Intelligence’s models, for example, take days to set up rather than months. Meanwhile, U.S. manufacturing labor costs 
$46.30 an hour fully loaded
 and 
compounds at 3-4% a year
. For the first time, deploying a robot costs roughly the same as the worker it replaces.
But cheaper, smarter hardware doesn’t automatically become a product. Someone has to take the components and turn them into something a plant manager will actually sign for; something that fits the workflow already running, passes the safety requirements already in place, and works reliably enough that the team standing next to it doesn’t feel like beta testers. That work is what General Partner Kevin Spain calls assembly. It’s the hardest, most underappreciated challenge in robotics right now.
Pick a task narrow enough to master. Win over the foreman. Answer the phone when it fails at 2am. It’s the work that turns a compelling technology into a market. Historically, it’s the work that captures most of the economic value. DroneDeploy spent a decade putting in this work. Last month, Procore paid $845 million for the result. That outcome is a proof of concept for the playbook we lay out in Assembly Required, and a signal of what’s possible for the assemblers being built on today’s robotics stack.

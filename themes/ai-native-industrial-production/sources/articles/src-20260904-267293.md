The Robotics Demo to Production Gap
Wider than people think, and also closing faster than people think.
Selina Wang
Sep 04, 2026
6
1
Share
After speaking to robotics developers, buyers, and researchers, one thing has become increasingly obvious to me: the gap between a working demo and a working deployment is wide. Three reasons stand out.
Data Collection and Curation
Production systems need specific data for post-training and fine-tuning robot models, and collecting it can be slow, unglamorous work. Synthetic data still leaves a lot to be desired. Model intelligence is advancing quickly, but nobody I’ve spoken with is deploying anything off the shelf. The good news is the quantity of post-training data needed for deployments is declining as base models get smarter. Generalist’s 
GEN-1.5
 released in August claims to learn a new task from a 3
–
12 second demonstration
, with
 59% average success across ten manipulation tasks one-shot. This rises to 83% with ten gradient steps on 5 minutes of data per task. Skild’s 
S1
 announcement claims 66% step-success on unseen long-horizon tasks against 9% for a language-prompted baseline.
Hardware Constraints
A demo runs for a few minutes, but a production robot has to run for days, months, and years. That means getting cooling to work, actuators to survive millions of cycles, etc. For example, a harmonic drive that makes a compact demo arm possible has flex-splines that fatigue under the loading production imposes. Production units may need to switch to cycloidal drives that are more durable, but bigger and heavier. This switch would require an entire joint redesign, not just a parts swap. Hardware that worked for a demo may not work in production, which drives redesigns, adds cost, and pushes back deployment timelines. Accelerated life testing and failure contingency planning are things you never think about for a demo but cannot avoid for a successful deployment.
Accuracy Bar
A demo needs to work once. A deployment needs to work 99.9%+ of the time, and failures can be very costly. For example, if an automotive line goes down, the manufacturer loses output, which slows down revenue. Simultaneously, it loses operating leverage, so per-unit cost goes up on everything that did get built. The failure is expensive twice. So it’s worth sitting with the numbers here. The frontier of one-shot learning is roughly 59% on short, simple tasks. Production needs 99.9%+ on longer duration tasks.
Conclusion
Despite all these challenges, I am optimistic about robotics because the slope of improvement is accelerating. The gap between demo and deployment is narrowing, particularly for companies focused on vertical 
or task-
specific applications, from strawberry picking to drone assembly. And every deployment that survives a year in production produces the data, the failure modes, and the reliability playbook that makes the next one better. This is a different thing from general intelligence, and I think the market will keep confusing the two for a while. But the useful version of this technology doesn’t need to wait for general intelligence.

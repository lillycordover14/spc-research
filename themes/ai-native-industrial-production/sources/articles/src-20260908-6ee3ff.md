Benjamin Kompa
He’s creating tools for fully autonomous science.
As a cofounder of Lila Sciences, a 2023 startup now valued at more than $1.3 billion, Ben Kompa helped formulate the company’s audacious goal of achieving “scientific superintelligence.” His vision, at the core of Lila’s mission, is to use AI to run every step of the scientific method, from creating hypotheses to designing experiments and running them in automated labs, and then using the results to guide the next series of experiments.
Now, as head of AI lab innovation at Lila, Kompa’s job is to build the connections between AI and the lab that will make this vision possible for materials science, chemistry, and life sciences. Much of that responsibility rests on Lila’s software operating system. It’s built on top of large language models (LLMs) and allows the company’s AI to interact with a wide variety of analytical and lab instruments.
But AI-powered software is only one step in the company’s mission of autonomous scientific discovery. Kompa, 31, and his colleagues are also incorporating new robotic hardware and other tools to expand lab automation capabilities, and improving the AI models and their ability to make sense of data and learn from it.
Kompa’s educational background and training well prepared him to take advantage of the recent explosion of interest in AI and science. As a PhD student at Harvard—he finished his degree in August 2022—he began researching how LLMs could be applied in science and health care. When he joined the life sciences venture company Flagship Pioneering, the conviction that LLMs could design, run, and learn from experiments led him and his cofounders to create Lila Sciences.
Fueled by $550 million in venture funding, Lila Sciences is now rapidly expanding its operations and moving into a new 250,000-square-foot laboratory and office space in Cambridge. It’s large enough to accommodate the vast array of lab equipment, robotics, and instruments for Kompa and his colleagues to build what they like to call an AI science factory.
Myra Cheng
She’s helping us understand how we relate to AI.
When Myra Cheng started studying LLM sycophancy in early 2025, few outside of AI research had heard of the concept. By May of that year, when Cheng posted her first paper on the subject, the term was everywhere. OpenAI had recently come under fire after its GPT-4o model had started outrageously flattering its human interlocutors and agreeing with everything they said, no matter how absurd. At best, the sycophancy was annoying; at worst, it may have dragged vulnerable people into spirals of delusion. And, like so much to do with AI, we barely understood it.
Cheng, a PhD student in computer science at Stanford University, brought much-needed rigor to the public discussion with her paper. By comparing human and LLM responses to questions posed on Reddit’s “Am I the Asshole?” forum, in which users describe personal anecdotes and ask other Redditors to judge whether or not they behaved badly, Cheng demonstrated that LLMs are consistently far more sycophantic than people. She also elucidated the ways in which that sycophancy expresses itself, such as unquestioningly accepting the user’s framing or validating their emotions.
Since then, Cheng, 25, has begun to map out the consequences: Earlier this year, she published a study in the journal Science on how sycophantic models change the way users think about their interpersonal conflicts, convincing them that they are in the right and making them less inclined to take responsibility for their actions. She’s also investigated other aspects of human-AI relationships, such as people’s tendency to overestimate efficiency gains from AI. In her quest to understand this totally new type of relationship, she deploys tools ranging from LLM prompting strategies to midcentury sociological theories.
Cheng has already shown that excessive LLM sycophancy can cause harm, and she knows that AI has other dark sides that she and other researchers haven’t yet explored. But her work isn’t all doom and gloom. She motivates herself, she says, by trying to picture what AI systems that would benefit humanity might look like.
Ayush Chopra
He uses swarms of agents to simulate complex events.
Many firms building AI agents have pitched them as the kind of personal assistants that can take on an individual’s mundane tasks. Ayush Chopra, a researcher at MIT, has a different vision: massive swarms of agents working together to explore problems for whole populations.
Chopra calls these swarms LPMs—large population models. Each individual agent might have limited intelligence, but the interactions between hundreds of millions of them produce a kind of hive mind that can be used to simulate a variety of complex phenomena, from the behavior of human crowds to the way diseases spread.
In the spring of 2024, Chopra and his colleagues set out to predict the spread of bird flu across the US with an LPM that simulated poultry and cattle farm supply chains. Different agents modeled various metrics: bird migration, wastewater spills from infected farms, the economic behavior of farmers and consumers across the country, and so on. “This agentic swarm is capturing dynamics all the way from migration patterns of a billion birds to consumer behavior at Whole Foods and Target,” says Chopra.
The simulation identified 100 hot spots that the team shared with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and US Department of Agriculture. By the end of the year, 87 of those locations had reported cases of bird flu.
Chopra is also using LPMs to simulate how AI tools might affect 151 million workers across 3,000 US counties and what kind of changes might result in the wider economy. The goal is to give policymakers a more detailed view of AI’s potential impact than employment numbers alone.
Looking beyond LPMs, as multi-agent systems become more widespread, Chopra’s work could also shed light on how to make huge numbers of AI agents interact in general. Today, most teams of agents run in a kind of harness controlled by a single software puppetmaster. But as the size of those teams increases, the ability to coordinate must be baked into the agents themselves, says Chopra: “Central orchestrators don’t scale to 100 billion agents.”
Danijar Hafner
He creates world models that train robots to navigate new spaces.
While he was still a high school student in rural Germany, Danijar Hafner began learning about AI thanks to an Innovators Under 35 alumni, Andrew Ng. “So many people in the field started learning from Andrew Ng’s Coursera course on machine learning,” says Hafner, of the Google Brain cofounder who was in the 2008 class of Innovators.
Today, Hafner focuses on creating world models that train agents to plan and robots to negotiate unfamiliar terrain and territory.
Hafner, 31, started out in AI as a student researcher at Google Brain in 2015, before moving to Google DeepMind, which he’s now left to form his own startup. Along the way, he worked with the likes of Geoffrey Hinton and Ashish Vaswani (a coauthor of the hugely influential “Attention Is All You Need” paper).
Hafner trains world models and then uses model-based reinforcement learning to enable AI agents to make decisions in new environments they have not previously experienced. Over the years, he has published a series of groundbreaking research papers, including in the journal Nature.
His first breakthrough was PlaNet, a world model that trained agents to execute actions by planning ahead. His Dreamer 2 trained the first agent to hit human-level performance playing Atari 2600 games using a world model; Dreamer 3 was the first agent to solve the Minecraft Diamond challenge—successfully mining in-game diamonds on its own. Dreamer 4 went a step beyond that by learning to mine diamonds from an offline data set of recorded gameplay videos, without ever interacting with the game directly. This was significant, as it showed an agent could learn about something in one environment and then apply that knowledge elsewhere.
But his research goes beyond gaming: It has real implications for robotics—as Hafner proved with his DayDreamer project, which used the Dreamer algorithm to let robots operate themselves in novel environments and react to new experiences (such as being pushed over) without specific training. These are the kinds of skills robots must master if we want them to live or work alongside us in unfamiliar environments such as our homes.
Sayash Kapoor
He tests whether AI is living up to its promise.
A few years back, several studies from political science researchers found that machine learning seemed to be able to do something miraculous: predict civil wars. The study results apparently showed that new AI models outperformed older statistical methods for figuring out which countries or regions were most likely to have conflicts break out.
But when Sayash Kapoor at Princeton went to reproduce these studies with a colleague, the results didn't stand up.
AI models are supposed to learn from one batch of data, then be tested on how well they make predictions about another batch they've never seen before. But Kapoor found these evaluation protocols had not been followed. In several of the studies he looked at, the AI had been accidentally exposed to information that should have been hidden until the final test. It’s a bit like grading a student after they've already seen the answer.
When accounting for this methodological flaw, Kapoor discovered that the new civil war prediction models weren't any better than the decades-old statistical models. Suspecting that political science researchers weren't alone in these mistakes, he conducted a wider review, ultimately finding nearly 300 papers across fields from psychiatry to nutrition that contained similar flaws.
One medical study that claimed AI could predict high blood pressure, for example, turned out to be feeding the model information about whether patients were already taking blood pressure medication, effectively giving away the answer.
Thus began Kapoor’s role checking the AI industry's hype against the evidence.
Kapoor grew up in Patna, a city in northeastern India, and studied machine learning at university. It was all quite theoretical until he jumped into a job at Facebook building AI models that would predict if children were at risk of abuse or suicide. “Pretty heavy stuff,” he says. Seeing AI's potential impact on the real world led him to a PhD at Princeton's Center for Information Technology Policy, which he finished in July 2026. In August 2027, when he'll still be just 30, he'll join the faculty of UC Berkeley to research AI's impact on society.
In an era of sweeping claims about what AI can do, Kapoor checks the receipts. When the field of “predictive optimization” promised that AI could determine whether a criminal defendant should be released before trial, for example, Kapoor and his colleagues outlined specific flaws in the technology that its proponents would have to account for. And when AI executives promised that AI would make human work obsolete, he helped chart how AI’s impact on society is more likely to be akin to other “normal technologies” like electricity or the internet.
In this way, you might call him a dissenting voice. But given the AI industry has no shortage of evangelists, Kapoor might just be leveling the scales.
Irene Li
She improves medical chatbots for non–English speakers.
With most major AI firms now pushing large language models designed to dish out medical advice, more and more people are getting health care by chatbot. Big caveats apply, but LLMs can provide people with helpful reassurance or recommendations. There’s a catch, though: You better speak English.
Researchers found that OpenAI’s GPT-4 (released in 2023) scored 90% when a set of multiple-choice medical questions were posed in English, but 55% when the queries were in French. In fact, almost all LLMs work better in English. That’s because they have been trained on English far more than any other language.
Irene Li, a researcher at the University of Tokyo, is working to fix this disparity using a two-step approach.
Li and her collaborators began by devising benchmarks to compare how different LLMs perform on the same medical tasks across various languages. In particular, Li is looking at South Asian and African languages, which are among those covered least by AI firms’ training data. Next, they used what those benchmarks revealed to come up with ways to improve LLM performance.
Li’s team found that LLMs worked through the logic of a problem more efficiently in English—a query that took three steps in English might take 10 steps in Swahili, for example. To address this, they have developed an approach called Med-CoReasoner, which forces a model to work through problems in both the local language and English at the same time, before responding in the local language. The local language provides region-specific medical information and English provides the problem-solving backbone, boosting accuracy overall.
It’s early days yet, but Li is optimistic. She wants to start rolling out health-care chatbots in hospitals. But the aim isn’t to replace humans: “I still suggest that people go to see a doctor,” she says.
Shayne Longpre
He’s finding scalable ways to hold tech firms to account.
Even as artificial intelligence becomes more intertwined with our lives, so much about the underlying models remains a mystery. Details are often scarce when it comes to the data they are trained on, or the harms they could cause. This is largely because we’ve depended on profit-driven AI companies to self-report. Shayne Longpre, 32, a recent PhD graduate from MIT Media Lab who just joined Anthropic, has worked for years to change this information asymmetry.
In 2023, he launched the Data Provenance Initiative (DPI), a global research project that systematically examines AI systems from four angles: (1) tracing the origins, licenses, and permissions of data sets used to train public and private models; (2) measuring how the internet is changing in response to the technology; (3) monitoring shifting power dynamics between countries, companies, and developers; and (4) studying how different groups use or misuse AI. Their research has shown, for example, that the internet is becoming more closed as websites try to prevent AI web crawlers from training on their content, and that downloads of Chinese open-source AI models have surpassed those of US-made models.
Building DPI also showed Longpre the importance of ensuring independent access to AI systems. AI firms have sometimes had strict terms of service and banned the accounts of researchers who test out guardrails. In 2024, Longpre coauthored an open letter, cosigned by more than 350 stakeholders, urging companies to provide safe harbors for people doing this kind of evaluation. After publication, OpenAI expanded protections to some researchers, and the European Union’s AI Act adopted a similar policy allowing red teaming.
Now he’s gone in-house at Anthropic. He hopes to continue the urgent task of building the infrastructure to make AI systems observable. As agents become more popular—and increasingly interact with each other—researchers will have to look beyond individual models to evaluate the broader AI ecosystem, requiring even more of the collaboration that Longpre’s work has enabled.
Giada Pistilli
She’s a philosopher examining how we interact with AI.
When ChatGPT launched in 2022, few predicted that millions of us would come to depend on chatbots for emotional support, and with such deeply mixed results. But that came as less of a surprise to Giada Pistilli. For years, Pistilli had studied how people interact with AI—first as a doctoral researcher at the Sorbonne University in Paris, then as principal ethicist for the AI company Hugging Face.
Her interest in the topic stems from her time as a master’s student at the Sorbonne. She wrote her thesis on whether conversational AI could serve democratic deliberation, which led her to work on more basic forms of chatbots used in France’s public sector. “My conclusion, after almost three years, was that chatbots weren’t working, and there was too much at stake,” she says.
Rather than turn away from the problem, she decided to help make them better serve our needs. Pistilli, 34, now leads a team at the French AI company Mistral that is trying to get AI to handle different languages and cultures more effectively. Among other things, this work allows her to build on the findings in her renowned paper “The Ghost in the Machine Has an American Accent,” which appeared as a preprint the same year as ChatGPT’s debut—2022. It flagged the level of cultural bias in foundation models, and argued for training them on more diverse data and perspectives.
Today, Pistilli moves between philosophy and hands-on AI research. Last year, she worked with colleagues at Hugging Face to create the first benchmark for evaluating AI companionship, called INTIMA. It measures whether AI models reinforce emotional dependency, maintain professional boundaries, or can provide neutral responses that address the user’s question without involving their relationship to the chatbot or veering off-topic.
Pistilli doesn’t want to make a moral judgment on AI companions, and is wary of repeating the 1990s panic about video games and violence—after more than 20 years of research, there is still no clear link. Evidence about chatbots may end up being similarly inconclusive.
This question is one she will undoubtedly return to throughout her career. In fact, she has written a book, due to come out this fall, that explores the consequences of so many people forming relationships with AI systems that are, first and foremost, products sold by tech companies.
Mengmi Zhang
She builds vision models that see the way we do.
Existing AI vision models are not bad. Self-driving cars are able to navigate city traffic, and ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini can describe in exhaustive detail the contents of a photograph. But these systems are far from perfect, and that’s something Mengmi Zhang is working to change. For the past decade, Zhang, 34, has studied both human vision and artificial vision to determine how the two differ—and to find ways to make AI as good as we are at seeing.
Compared with AI vision systems, we humans see more efficiently, flexibly, and reliably. Without even realizing it, we extract essential information from our visual environments, and our abilities don’t falter if we’re somewhere new. In one of her early studies, Zhang measured how humans search for objects in complex scenes—including “Where’s Waldo?” puzzles—and then built a vision model that solved the problem the way humans are thought to. Zhang says that the model anticipated the transformer architectures that have come to dominate modern AI.
More recently, Zhang has built other human-inspired models: Some use visual and auditory information simultaneously, and others are exposed to only blurry, grayscale images during the early stages of training, to simulate the way human infants learn to see. Her hope is that bringing vision models closer to the standard set by our own visual abilities will make them more useful. It could also open up new avenues for her research: Alongside building better AI, Zhang uses AI to model the brain and understand human vision, and more human-like vision models are better suited to that task.
In both of these threads of her research, Zhang is helping to push back against scientists who argue that neuroscience has little to offer AI. Although deep neural networks were originally inspired by the brain, most recent AI advancements have little to do with neuroscience. But brains are far more efficient than today’s AI models, and researchers like Zhang are going back to the human brain for inspiration. “Some people would argue that the neuroscience-helping-AI era is over. But I would like to argue it’s actually the opposite,” Zhang says.

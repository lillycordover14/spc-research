What they do Builds autonomous foundries. Founded 2020 Headquarters El Segundo, CA Employees ~13 Sector Industrials A missing part can ground a fleet The problem Rangeview is being paid to solve is less glamorous than the aircraft on its promotional materials. A military machine needs a replacement casting. The original tooling may be gone, the supplier may have closed, and a small order offers little reason for a surviving foundry to interrupt its production schedule. The drawing remains. The industrial capacity to turn it back into hardware is harder to find. In August, Rangeview announced a contract to provide foundry capacity for U.S. Navy maintenance, repair and overhaul. The work falls under the defense industrial-base programs DIBEDGE and IBAS. Its value was not disclosed. Days earlier, Kratos selected the company to develop flight-quality cast superalloy components for advanced turbine engines, including propulsion programs for drones and missiles. Together, the announcements put a small El Segundo manufacturer inside two different procurement problems: keeping old equipment working and making new engines at scale. Rangeview calls its operation a Cyber Foundry. Behind the branding is a concrete process change: print the ceramic mold directly, then cast the metal, instead of building a wax pattern and repeatedly dipping and drying it to form a ceramic shell. Robotics and factory software carry the process through production. Customers buy parts. They do not have to buy Rangeview's equipment and persuade their own plant to reorganize around it. That distinction grew out of an unsuccessful earlier idea. Founders Cameron Schiller and Aeden Gasser-Brennan initially wanted to sell robotic machinery into existing factories. They found the willingness to automate much harder to sell than the machinery itself. Their response was to become the factory owner. What disappears when the wax does Investment casting is useful precisely because it can produce difficult shapes in demanding materials. It is also a sequence of interdependent operations. In the traditional process described by Rangeview's founders, wax patterns are assembled into a tree, a ceramic shell is built around them, the wax is removed, and molten metal fills the resulting cavity. Each preparation stage creates work, waiting time and opportunities for variation. The founders say their direct printing approach removes roughly eight of about eighteen historical steps. The more consequential claim is repeatability: geometry and process parameters are controlled by machines and software, reducing reliance on the individual craft knowledge that often resides with experienced foundry workers. Eliminating wax tooling also changes the economics of the first part. A new design need not begin with a new physical tool. Rangeview advertises both vacuum and air-melt capabilities. Its listed materials include nickel and cobalt superalloys such as MAR-M-247, IN713LC, IN792+Hf, MAR-M-509 and IN718. These are a different proposition from producing a plastic prototype or a low-temperature metal demonstration. The useful output is a part whose geometry, surface and material properties survive its intended operating environment. The company lists as-cast surface finishes around 100 microinches Ra and inspection to AMS 2175, up to Grade A. Its offering extends into design for manufacturing, nondestructive testing, heat treatment, hot isostatic pressing, machining and metallurgical analysis. Those supporting operations matter because a casting is not necessarily ready for an engine when it leaves the mold. The customer needs a qualified component, with evidence that it meets the specification. Published company material does not establish which pieces of every inspection or finishing operation are performed internally rather than through partners. Nor does it provide a complete inventory of proprietary machines and purchased components. The substantiated differentiation is the combination of printed ceramic molds, process automation and an operated foundry. It would be premature to describe every element of that factory as Rangeview intellectual property. Kratos is buying a manufacturing route Kratos offers an unusually useful test of the technology because its engineers have identified the tradeoff they want Rangeview to address. In announcing the selection, chief engineer Russ Jones described complex geometries that had previously required laser powder-bed fusion, but with the properties of cast superalloys. The attraction is not simply replacing a slower supplier with a faster one. It is obtaining a different combination of shape and material behavior. Laser powder-bed fusion builds the metal part itself, layer by layer. Rangeview's approach prints the mold and then uses casting to make the metal component. Both can reduce dependence on traditional tooling, but they arrive at the part through different physical processes. A buyer choosing between them has to care about more than the freedom of the original CAD model. Qualification, material performance and the economics of repeated production all enter the decision. The Kratos work is described as funded development of flight-quality hardware. That is meaningful commercial evidence, but it is not a published annual production quantity or a disclosed recurring revenue stream. There is no public figure here for the value of the work, the number of engines it will support, or the share of a particular engine Rangeview will manufacture. The Navy contract tests a complementary use case. Sustainment demand may involve equipment whose original production ecosystem has withered. Removing tooling from the critical path could make short runs more practical, particularly when replacement parts are needed before a conventional tooling program would finish. Rangeview describes a move from months to weeks for first articles. The important measure will be accepted parts delivered on that schedule, across a range of real maintenance requirements. Two turbine markets meet in one factory The company's public imagery and facility reporting include impellers, brackets, latches, rotors, control-stick components and other cast hardware. Turbine blades have become a particularly relevant example because two sources of demand are rising together: gas turbines supplying electricity for data centers, and compact jet engines for missiles and unmanned aircraft. The customers and qualification routes differ, but both markets depend on difficult metal components. That gives Rangeview an industrial argument broader than a single defense award. If a factory can repeatedly deliver suitable superalloy parts, it may serve buyers whose budgets come from different sources while sharing part of the underlying production infrastructure. It also limits what can be inferred from general enthusiasm about drones or AI power. Demand for turbines does not automatically become revenue for one foundry. Parts have to be qualified, production must yield acceptable components, and the factory must expand without losing process control. Rangeview's published record does not disclose a company-wide revenue figure, gross margin or annual output. Its strongest evidence is the named customer work and the operation of a physical production facility. An August Pirate Wires visit placed that operation in an aging brick industrial building on Center Avenue in El Segundo. Founder interviews describe an early facility of roughly 4,000 square feet and an expansion ambition toward approximately 60,000 square feet. These accounts establish the direction of the effort; they should not be read as proof that every planned square foot is already producing qualified parts. The competition has several shapes The most direct competition comes from established investment-casting businesses: large operators such as Precision Castparts and Howmet, along with specialist foundries. Their advantage is not merely installed equipment. They have customer relationships, qualification history and experience producing demanding parts. Rangeview is trying to make tooling and labor-intensive preparation less decisive without giving up the confidence those buyers require. The startup's opportunity therefore differs by order. A customer with a stable, high-volume production program may value an incumbent's proven process more than a new method's flexibility. A buyer missing a tool, changing a design or struggling to find capacity has a stronger reason to examine another route. The Navy and Kratos announcements fit those latter circumstances particularly well. Foundry Lab is a closer digital-casting peer. Other ceramic additive-manufacturing and casting-automation companies address pieces of the same production problem. Desktop Metal represents partial overlap through metal additive manufacturing, while Velo3D's laser powder-bed fusion is an adjacent alternative for complex geometries. These are not interchangeable offerings: some sell machines, some enable a process, and Rangeview sells the output of its own factory. Owning production changes where value might accumulate. Rangeview can potentially retain the benefit of faster iteration, accumulated process data and improved yields rather than passing all those gains to a machine buyer. It also assumes the operating burden. Equipment utilization, scrap, delivery performance and working capital become its problems. Selling parts makes the customer's purchase simpler; it makes the manufacturer's execution more consequential. Two robotics competitors become partners Schiller and Gasser-Brennan knew each other through the Southern California robotics scene, where they competed before becoming co-founders. Schiller's public accounts describe a childhood spent taking things apart, building with Lego and participating in VEX robotics, including a world championship during his first year of high school. The company's eventual focus is industrial, but the partnership began with machines built to compete. Schiller attended UC Berkeley and, in a SendCutSend interview, described leaving after one semester. His account of time inside Chinese consumer-electronics factories is more central to his manufacturing philosophy than a conventional software-founder résumé. He became convinced that keeping design and production close together improved the speed of learning, and that separating the two weakened the ability to build competitive products. Early entrepreneurial efforts included charging rental scooters. The first Rangeview concept, initially called Rangeview Robotics, involved robotic arms and high-power gearboxes for existing factories. Interviews describe the founders surveying more than a hundred manufacturing processes before settling on investment casting: important, neglected and, in their view, unusually amenable to automation through printed molds. Gasser-Brennan leads the process and equipment work as CTO. His background includes mechanical engineering at Berkeley, Formula SAE vehicle dynamics and applied fluid dynamics. He is the principal investigator on the company's Air Force SBIR Phase II project for digital investment casting. In joint interviews, he supplies much of the detailed explanation of how the traditional shell process differs from Rangeview's approach. The wider leadership includes operations vice president Cullen McAlpine and CFO Ursula Ster. Public employee estimates put the company at roughly thirteen people, a small organization relative to the industrial ambitions of its customer base. That figure is an estimate, not a current audited headcount, but it helps explain why factory execution remains so central to the story. A seed round, a grant and a production test Rangeview's disclosed financing is modest beside the capital flowing into many of its prospective customers. Berkeley SkyDeck supplied early accelerator backing, with approximately $200,000 cited in company-data reporting. PitchBook records a $4.2 million seed round in December 2022, bringing reported equity funding to roughly $4.4 million. A valuation has not been made public. The listed investors include Calibrate Ventures, Mantis VC, Marque Ventures, Nonlinear Capital, Pathbreaker Ventures, Shield Capital and TenOneTen Ventures. Separately, the Air Force and AFWERX SBIR Phase II project is worth approximately $1.43 million, with a performance period running from September 2025 into December 2026. That is government research and development support, not another venture round. The funding history makes the current commercial work more interesting than a generic reshoring story. Rangeview is trying to turn a relatively small amount of venture capital, a development award and customer programs into productive capacity. The Navy contract's undisclosed value prevents any reliable calculation of how much runway that business provides. The same is true of Kratos. Neither should be converted into an invented backlog number. Schiller's public ambition extends to many factories and a revival of manufacturing communities. The immediate test is narrower and more useful: can the El Segundo operation produce difficult parts repeatedly, get them accepted, and deliver enough of them to support a durable business? A mold printed quickly is a process result. A customer returning for its next production order would be evidence that the new foundry works as a company.

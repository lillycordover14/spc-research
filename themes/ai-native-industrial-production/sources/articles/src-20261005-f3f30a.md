Manufacturer VulcanForms chief executive Kevin Kassekert hopes to double his workforce over the next year&nbsp;&nbsp;The Boston Globe

[page text]
The future of metals manufacturing is taking shape one laser beam at a time, in an unassuming industrial building about 40 miles northwest of Boston, under the watchful eye of 
Kevin Kassekert
.
The chief executive of 
VulcanForms
 is presiding over an aggressive expansion for the company, which uses its 3-D printing technology to produce complicated metal parts for a variety of industries, at speed and at scale. The company’s machines produce hip implants and missile fins — to name just a few items — with dozens of synchronized lasers firing away, carving shapes out of metal materials.
“We’re able to compete globally now, and we’re winning business back from overseas, which is exciting,” Kassekert said. 
It might sound like work that could take away human jobs. But Kassekert needs people to design and monitor these machines, software engineers, and other technical experts. He expects the company’s 500-person workforce could double within the next year; it recently opened a second plant in Devens, and also runs one in Newburyport. Three more are in the works, though they probably all won’t be in Massachusetts.
“Now, with a lot of automation and . . . machine learning, you can still hire and build great high-paying jobs in the US,” Kassekert said. “But your people can be much more productive.”
To help fuel this expansion, VuclanForms recently raised $220 million from private investors, and landed a pledge in June from state officials for up to $21 million in tax credits. 
The technology came out of MIT, where it was developed in the 2010s by engineering professor 
John Hart
 and 
Martin Feldmann
, who met Hart as a student. Hart remains on VuclanForms’s board, alongside Kassekert, 
Analog Devices
 founder 
Ray Stata
, and two San Francisco venture capitalists, 
Lior Susan
 and 
Greg Reichow
, from investor 
Eclipse Ventures
.
The board recruited Kassekert for the job in 2024, in part because of his success with helping 
Elon Musk
 build the first 
Tesla
 “gigafactory” in Nevada, where Tesla and 
Panasonic
 make electric-vehicle batteries. Kassekert left Tesla in 2020 to help lead 
Redwood Materials
, a nearby battery recycler where he worked for four years.
Kassekert moved to Massachusetts for the VulcanForms role, and thinks the state is a great place to build a high-tech workforce. 
“We were born out of MIT, we’ve kind of got our DNA here,” he said. “Massachusetts is a place where you can attract the best talent in the world
.
”
A big birthday gift
Around New England, there are many private high schools that can boast of having celebrated their 125th birthdays. But how many could enliven the festivities by announcing a donation valued at $200 million?
The 
Middlesex School
 in Concord might be the first. School officials announced a $200 million pledge from the late 
Victor Atkins
 during a Sept. 26 reception kicking off the school’s 125th anniversary celebrations.
“It’s an incredible gift,” said 
Bessie Speers
, head of school. “His deep love for the school has translated into this incredible legacy.”
Atkins died last year at the age of 80, in his Nevada home. His business career included a tenure at the former brokerage E.F. Hutton, and a chairmanship with the board of manufacturer 
Polaris
. The Middlesex alum supported the school with his time and money over the years. But his gifts were never as big as this one.
Atkins left a few stipulations for his latest gift: 25 percent should go to faculty, 15 percent to financial aid, and 10 percent to residential life. The rest is unrestricted. Middlesex has received 40 percent so far, while the rest will arrive over the next several decades.
“What this gift will allow us to do,” said board chair 
Jason Robart
, “and what [it] will inspire others to do from a philanthropic perspective is really humbling.”
Pressing the gas pedal 
As state attorney general, 
Maura Healey
 tried to 
slow down
 natural gas pipeline projects. Now, as governor, she wants to speed them up.
Last week, Healey wrote to US energy Secretary 
Chris Wright
, urging that the Trump administration provide an expedited review of 
a $300 million capacity-expansion project
 along 
Enbridge
’s Algonquin gas pipeline network in Southeastern Massachusetts and Rhode Island. 
Eversource
 has 10-year agreements to buy gas via the expanded system on behalf of its customers, reducing its need for higher-priced gas that 
arrives in liquefied form
 on tankers that dock at Everett. Healey wants to make sure the project is finished for a 2028 in-service date, to save money for Eversource customers. 
She starts her letter by reminding Wright about their shared desire to “get more U.S natural gas into New England” to reduce the region’s reliance on higher-cost imported fuel. 
So what changed from her AG days? The Healey administration is hearing from many people and businesses complaining about their bills. She is also running for reelection this fall against Republican 
Mike Minogue
, who has championed the need for more natural gas in New England. 
Eversource unsurprisingly cheered on Healey’s letter. Spokesman 
William Hinkle
 said the company “appreciates the Governor’s focus on an ‘all-of-the-above’ approach to energy supply as a key part of making service more reliable and affordable for our gas customers.”
New boss at Mintz
After 18 years with a New Yorker leading one of the city’s biggest law firms, the managing member’s role at 
Mintz
 soon will be held again by a Bostonian.
Last week, the firm’s partners voted to make 
Mike Fantozzi
 their next managing member, after 
Bob Bodian
 wraps up his tenure in mid-2027. Bodian, the New Yorker in question, will become chair, a title left open since 
Bob Popeo
’s death in 2023.
The 600-attorney firm’s revenue has grown each year for the past 11, and it came close to $770 million in the fiscal year ended in March — or nearly three times what it was when Bodian first became managing member in 2009. It has eight offices, including its largest one, in a tower across from South Station.
Fantozzi has led the local office for more than a decade and has been at Mintz for three decades, working with some of the city’s biggest companies. “There’s something really gratifying about working at a firm . . . on sophisticated work for clients that are really important to our local and our national economies,” Fantozzi said. “I’m looking forward to continuing the terrific run Bob has had.”
Remote control at Massport
When 
Massport
 launched its remote terminal in Framingham for 
Delta
 and 
JetBlue
 flights a few months ago, it marked the first time the concept was offered in the United States for passengers of all fare classes, and not just high-end fliers.
But it almost certainly won’t be the last.
In remarks to 
Associated Industries of Massachusetts
 members last week, Massport chief executive 
Rich Davey
 talked about how representatives from airports in Philadelphia and Washington visited to see the terminal in action. Davey also described more plans for Greater Boston, mentioning how a remote terminal will be incorporated into two garages that will go up at the Logan Express lot in Braintree. He’s looking at offering the service temporarily for large events at the Menino Convention & Exhibition Center in South Boston, and at a more permanent location behind the Hynes Convention Center in the Back Bay.
Travelers using the remote terminal can check their bags and go through security in Framingham, and then get taken by bus to their gates at Logan Airport, in front of security, avoiding Logan’s lines. 
So far, Davey said, about 25,000 people have used the service since its June launch. The buses draw about 200 travelers daily, roughly half their capacity. That represents a tiny fraction of Logan’s traffic, but every little bit helps to reduce congestion at the terminal curbs.
Davey also talked about how Massport has received thousands of applications for a “Voice of Logan” contest, to record a greeting for arriving visitors. A public vote from five finalists will pick the winner in the coming weeks. 
“There are two important elections in November,” Davey joked. “One of the things we’ve been trying to do is bring a little fun back to travel.”

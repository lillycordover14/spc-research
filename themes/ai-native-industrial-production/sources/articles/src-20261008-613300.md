Key Takeaways
Periodic Labs' Liam Fedus and Ekin Dogus Cubuk argue AI-driven autonomous labs, not pure compute, are the bottleneck to materials discovery, since noisy physical experiments like XRD phase identification require sample-efficient reasoning that clean digital training cannot provide.
Summary
Periodic Labs co-founders Liam Fedus and Ekin Dogus Cubuk argue that AI's next frontier is not more reasoning tokens but physical experiments, because scientific discovery is by definition the domain you were not trained on. The episode's title promises "autonomous labs and synthesis superintelligence," and that is genuinely the spine of the conversation, but the most useful material for a technical reader is the operational detail: how experimental noise, lossy characterization, and negative results get turned into reinforcement-learning environments, and why the founders think a frontier model will still have to run experiments even at generation seven.

Fedus (formerly of OpenAI, where he worked on large-model training) and Cubuk (a materials scientist who came out of solid-state physics and DFT work) describe building Periodic Labs as a Bell Labs-style merger of theorists, experimentalists, hardware and software engineers, and LLM researchers under one roof in Menlo Park. The discussion ranges across why "intelligence is necessary but insufficient," how X-ray diffraction and multimodality resolve phase ambiguity, why DFT cannot replace a lab even if it were perfect, the Landauer limit as a materials problem, and what deployment engineers will actually do for semiconductor customers. The reader should walk away with a concrete mental model of the closed experimental loop Periodic is trying to build, the specific bottlenecks it is betting on, and the founders' explicit skepticism that pure inference-time reasoning can substitute for touching the universe.

Scientific discovery is the domain you were not trained on

The founders' central thesis is that machine learning is most effective on what it was trained on, and scientific discovery is almost by definition the thing outside that distribution. Cubuk states this directly: "what is machine learning very effective in that, on what it trained. But scientific discovery — this, by definition, is, why don't you trained." This is why they build physical laboratories rather than relying on ever-better models — so that both open and closed models can interact with the universe. Fedus adds the corollary: "no one can just like that, 'from scratch', create superconductor room temperature."

The premise is stated on Periodic's own site, which the host quotes: "Intelligence necessary, but insufficient. New knowledge is created when ideas are found agreed with reality." Fedus explains the reasoning: the universe is too complicated to "think through" a discovery, so you must create hypotheses and check whether they are true. Reading a textbook or having a thought "short circuit in room" will not substitute for the iterative process of expected and unexpected experimental results.

A key structural argument: optimal reasoning strategies for mathematics or theoretical computer science cannot be optimal for science. In math and programming, the full context is available — axioms, consequences, functions, API calls — and you just have to be smart. In physics, you start with more atoms than you can ever store on a computer, so you must perform a reduction of dimensionality. The founders cite thermodynamics as the original example: early engineers were confused by steam engines until five or six thermodynamic variables (temperature, pressure) were found to explain the behavior of 10^23 to 10^27 atoms.

"We believe that it is possible to do great discovery, nothing testing. Yes, no one can just like that, 'from scratch', create superconductor room temperature." — Liam Fedus

Fedus notes that current AI improvements concentrate on math, programming, and theoretical computer science because those are easier for LLMs, but most real-world tasks that require intelligence resemble science: solid uncertainty, noise, missing context, and the need to decide what to do next anyway. He predicts theoretical physics may be the next domain to fall, but distinguishes that sharply from the physical world.

The experimental loop: noisy data, lossy characterization, and RL environments

This is the operational core of the episode. The founders describe a four-stage cycle for discovering new materials: (1) determine what to create — atomic stability plus target properties; (2) synthesize it — determining processing conditions, which they call extremely difficult even when the structure is known; (3) characterize it — the material comes out unlabeled; (4) decide the next step. Each stage carries distinct uncertainty.

Fedus gives the concrete example of the oven: over time it wears out because processed materials evaporate and coat the heating element, so every use makes it worse. Temperature in the furnace is not uniform, so where you place the material affects the result. Optical tools suffer from vibration — Cubuk recounts a doctoral lab where results varied until they discovered someone walking on the floor above at night was vibrating the laser setup.

The characterization bottleneck is where AI earns its keep first. Periodic directs X-rays at material; the diffraction pattern is an imprint of crystalline structure, but identifying what phases are actually present is very hard. Their early AI work built systems for phase identification, rewarded for correctly identifying present phases and penalized for incorrect phases or chemically invalid ones. Fedus frames this as a "small supporting thing" that, assembled with the rest, yields the full discovery cycle.

The most sophisticated idea in the episode is how they build RL environments from accumulated experimental data. By timestamping the state of the world — "on this date we had the following experimental evidence" — they can create environments where the model must choose the next scientist's step or predict the result of an experiment. This matters because of a contamination problem Fedus describes: if a model already memorized part of the data and you build tasks depending on those answers, the model can simulate work — it "gets the correct answer" without performing complex physical reasoning, and you then reinforce reasoning strategies that will not generalize to new systems. Traceable, timestamped experimental data lets them build environments that are impossible under other conditions.

Why DFT cannot replace the lab, even if it were perfect

Cubuk gives a compact explanation of density functional theory: Kohn and Hohenberg showed you do not need to work with the exponential Hilbert space of the wave function — everything needed for ground-state properties is a functional of charge density, which is only a three-dimensional object. Kohn and Sham then proposed a practical approximation via the Kohn-Sham equations. Cubuk notes the theorem is simple enough that a physics student can understand it, and that DFT is probably the most-used simulation method in materials.

But he is emphatic that DFT is not a substitute for a laboratory, for two reasons. First, DFT is by definition not required to be an approximation — the Kohn-Sham theorem shows it can be exact — but we lack access to the exchange-correlation functional, and for charge-density-based models we do not know the kinetic energy functional. Second, even with perfect DFT, you cannot fit 10^23 atoms into a computer. Fedus adds that even 100 million DFT tests would not be enough, because DFT typically models perfect crystals while real crystals have microstructure — medium-order structure that significantly influences properties — and some properties, like high-temperature superconductivity, are essentially impossible to model with DFT.

The practical division of labor: some measurements are easier and more accurate in simulation, others in experiment. Periodic uses DFT to estimate enthalpy of formation — if a material's energy is higher than the alternatives its atoms could turn into, it is unlikely to be synthesizable; stability means lying on the convex hull of competing materials. For a long time the Materials Project, described as the best open-source DFT database, used experimental calibration for DFT, and Periodic does the same internally: when interested in a chemical system, they run experiments and simulations and calibrate them against each other.

"Even if we could spend 100 million tests with DFT, we could never check their microstructure... even if you spend 100 million DFT tests, this will be not enough." — Ekin Dogus Cubuk

Phase transitions, multimodality, and resolving ambiguity

Cubuk explains phase transitions for a non-physics audience via melting ice and boiling water, then via the Ising model — spins pointing up or down, entropy winning at high temperature, sudden organization at a critical point. In Periodic's labs, phase transitions occur when precursors are mixed and temperature is raised to drive reaction; the atoms react and form a new crystal, which shows up on radiographs because sharp changes in atomic geometry change the diffraction pattern significantly.

The practical problem: when you try something for the first time, the result is rarely a clean yes or no. It is usually a mixed phase — some precursors, some amorphous phases, and a bunch of phases you did not intend to create. This is where simulations and AI must be combined for characterization. Simulations might say "this phase is a small variation of what we predicted"; AI might say "given prior experiments, this is probably not the phase we want, so change the conditions." In some cases a phase appears that has never been recorded in any article or database, and AI uses tools to ask what atomic configuration could explain the observed pattern.

The resolution strategy is multimodality. Two different phases can produce the same XRD pattern, so Periodic also measures electrical and magnetic properties and morphology via electron microscopy. Phases that look similar on XRD look different under other measurements. Cubuk's point is that a human given 10 modalities simultaneously and asked to analyze them in sequence struggles, but for AI this does not require superintelligence — it just requires performing many calculations and viewing all the data. Fedus frames the payoff as better decision-making under uncertainty: the system integrates signals from many tools over long time periods and across replicates, building a deeper model instead of over-relying on one measurement from one device.

The founders also address data retention. Cubuk says they do not throw data away — if God were watching the experiment there would be more data than any computer could hold, but mortals cannot track every atom, so all collected data is valuable. Fedus offers an analogy to probing a base model: God has the complete picture of the material and experiment; the experiment is a small linear probe with eight bits of output, and the job of the human or agent is to reconstruct what is happening inside from those eight bits.

Maxwell's demon, the Landauer limit, and energy as the real currency

Cubuk connects the data-retention question to Maxwell's demon: the 1800s thought experiment of a tiny omniscient demon sorting low-energy atoms to reduce entropy and violate the second law. Landauer's modern argument resolves it — deleting information requires dissipating energy, and the demon would have to delete enough information that the entropy increase is guaranteed. Cubuk calls the host's question about future data deletion "far-sighted."

This leads to one of the more striking forward-looking bets in the episode: whether we can approach the Landauer limit for computational energy efficiency. Cubuk notes that energy per flop has fallen exponentially, Moore's-law-style. Fedus recalls first learning about the limit 10 to 15 years ago, when we were roughly 10 to 20 orders of magnitude away; the last time he looked, we were within three or four orders of magnitude, or closer. He calls getting closer to the Landauer limit "amazing for humanity" because computation is probably one of the most important things humanity does, and energy is "the real currency of the universe."

Cubuk adds the heat-dissipation angle: calculations release heat that must be shed. On quantum computing, he is candid that he is not an expert and does not understand whether reversible computation avoids the energy cost — reversible processes do not delete information, so in principle they need not spend energy on the process itself, but he does not know if it works. He notes quantum computation must obey thermodynamics. He also relays that he agrees with Elad Gil's skepticism about near-term quantum applications, and makes a sharper point: even a perfect quantum computer would still face the same microstructure problems DFT has, because people would still be modeling the same "perfect crystal."

Automation pragmatism: IQ-140 instruments, not humanoid robots

The founders' most-quoted line — that every piece of equipment in the lab will have an IQ of 140 — comes from a concrete bottleneck. In early scaling, they had equipment technicians and scientists hunting for a certain morphology to determine what they were actually making. They first tried simple software for data collection on scanning electron microscopes: a program that selected a field of view, took a picture, approached, took another. Fedus says the data was too primitive for what they wanted to count, so they embedded AI systems directly in the machines. Now those systems hold the full context — the goal of the experiment, what they were trying to synthesize, other experimental evidence — and collect data intelligently, producing a much richer dataset for future models.

On latency, the constraint is not what an outside observer would guess. Experiments take hours or days, but specific stages matter: precise control, cost (slow models are expensive models), and human patience — a scientist waiting two hours for an XRD analysis versus two minutes. Fedus notes that deep research is not one tool call or one stream of conclusions, so latency compounds across many tokens. The split between tool-call latency (mostly DFT or computational methods) and reasoning latency depends on the process.

On humanoid robots, Fedus is explicit that Periodic believes humanoid robots would slow them down. The pragmatic approach is to connect people and automation, find bottlenecks in the experimental process, and eliminate them one by one. Tasks requiring dexterity, where humans are wonderful and automation would take months, are not worth automating; routine tasks that consume huge amounts of scientist and technician time are. Full autonomy is not the goal — high-quality, high-volume, diverse data is.

A concrete anecdote illustrates AI's role in data quality: at one stage of a cycle, an error arose because a machine was loaded incorrectly and the patterns were contradictory. The AI analyzed the data, said the result was not expected given what was launched, considered the array in dynamics, and determined that a cyclic permutation would make the data consistent. The team then returned to the physical infrastructure and found the loading error. Cubuk adds that reducing background noise in experimental data is critical — most literature experimental data is noisy enough that it performs worse than DFT accuracy, because different people in different labs at different times add variability. Standardizing workflows is one of the largest expected advantages.

Negative results, process data, and the campaign as the unit of learning

The founders treat negative results as a first-class asset. Fedus defines a negative result as intending to create a structure and finding all evidence indicates you did not — or, in some cases, discovering a new structure you were not looking for. Cubuk notes that classification algorithms need negative samples, and materials science has a structural problem: people publish only crystals they managed to synthesize and almost never publish synthesis failures. It is also never known whether a crystal is fundamentally unsynthesizable or just a matter of scale, method, or technique.

The more valuable data type, per Fedus, is process engineering: the series of negative results that eventually led to a positive one. Even when a material is known, reproduction can be difficult. Periodic accumulates this experience and builds systems that, given a number of negative results, can tell you how to reach the positive case. Cubuk notes that if you label each experiment, most labels would be negative; if you label each campaign — assuming campaigns end at success — the dataset is more balanced. Fedus says such data virtually does not exist elsewhere, and Periodic spends a lot of time laying the full scientific pedigree into the model: conversations, intuitive guesses, what was done in labs, where calculations were run, where code was written.

This is framed as analogous to recursive self-improvement, but with a twist: the model is taught to run better experiments, not to train better models. Cubuk notes the loop does not return to the base model — unless the physical models developed improve chips that then improve AI, making it a broader, higher-order RSI. Fedus argues that if inference-time reasoning were sufficient, leading labs would have stopped at GPT-4 and improved only at inference. He believes compression into weights will produce different systems and capabilities, and that even a seventh-generation model will still have to spend experiments, because scientific discovery is the thing outside its training distribution.

"Even if the model seventh generation will be very good, even better than now, she doesn't care will have to spend experiments for receiving results. And the reason is that machine learning very good copes with the fact that what is it on trained. And scientific discovery is almost by definition, that is, on why weren't you trained?" — Ekin Dogus Cubuk

Scaling labs, hiring, and the deployment-engineer role

Periodic's scaling thesis is that new types of laboratories matter as much as more of them. Fedus says resources will allow significant scaling of labs and compute, but emphasizes building new lab types. Cubuk says labs of this type have not been created at this scale or with this approach, and that lessons from building the first Menlo Park lab inform later versions. Some tools are very expensive; as they learn which bring the greatest return, they invest more, making hardware engineering central. In early stages they bought ready-made appliances for speed; at scale they build their own, discovering which components become bottlenecks and which design details reduce noise — for example, reworking a robot manipulator's rest position so it does not sit above a plate of mixed substances and risk contamination.

The hiring page spans AI, infrastructure, computation, experimental roles, hardware, and product. Cubuk frames these as holistic toward the goal of synthesis superintelligence, including product engineers who turn research results into tools experimenters can use. A guiding principle: highly skilled, experienced people should do hands-on work. Fedus contrasts this with academia, where writing grants and teaching leave no time for practical research, and cites Bell Labs and IBM — Bardeen was in the labs every day despite being a theorist; Alex Müller ran experiments even as a leader.

On deployment engineers: Periodic built its own tools, AI, and compute for itself, so it was its first customer. It now offers those tools to industry, focused on semiconductors, which requires working in some of the most protected environments. Deployment engineers and researchers will work on-site, integrating AI or computational systems to help partners. Fedus says this is a significantly expanded role versus standard deployment engineering — it requires deep ML expertise, precision with data and infrastructure, and physics/chemistry/materials knowledge. It partly means willingness to spend long stretches in Taiwan.

On monetization, Fedus offers software development as the analogue: GitHub Copilot and early ChatGPT as co-pilots, then Codex and far less hand-written code. He expects a similar arc for Periodic — systems that speed up materials scientists and process engineers, then, as autonomy grows, systems that evaluate results toward a target state. Cubuk frames the best contribution to solid-state physics as making these tools profitable, so that materials research demonstrates commercial impact and attracts young people to physics, as ChatGPT did for CS and LLM majors. He notes Bell Labs had huge commercial impact but failed to commercialize some of its own achievements, though it benefited greatly from the vacuum tube connecting the east and west coasts by telephone.

Historical precedents: MgB2, nickelates, and the case for brute-force search

Cubuk's most concrete argument for automation is the magnesium diboride story. A Japanese group tested roughly 30,000 substances; about 30 showed interesting superconductivity, and MgB2 was one — the conventional superconductor with the highest temperature at atmospheric pressure. MgB2 had sat on shelves as a precursor for decades, and BCS theory was invented in 1957. The discovery was not a direct result of theory and not a synthesis limitation — it was brute-force trying of many materials. Cubuk's point: with the perfect automation described, you could try those 30,000 options in one month instead of a career.

On high-temperature superconductivity, Cubuk distinguishes conventional superconductivity (mostly electron-phonon interaction, showing the isotope effect) from unconventional superconductors like cuprates above 77 K, such as 93 K, which do not obey that physics and whose governing physics is unknown. He notes we still cannot simulate strong correlation in simple quantum systems, and DFT cannot catch some strong-correlation effects. He cites Phil Anderson's "more is different" — you can understand all the basic laws and still find that many objects behave qualitatively differently than simple physics suggests. He draws a parallel to deep learning: power laws everywhere, emergent behavior, and no understanding of why.

The nickelate anecdote: Alex Müller tried nickelates (nickel, oxygen, some cations) before cuprates (copper, oxygen, some cations); cuprates worked and won him the Nobel, but we later learned nickelates were also a good idea — nickel and copper sit side by side on the periodic table. Haidong Wang, a Stanford professor, realized nickelates could be created as thin films and demonstrated superconductivity. Cubuk's conclusion: with powerful synthesis superintelligence, they will not run out of ideas — 3D transition metals and oxygen offer plenty, and people have tried cobaltates too.

On open source, Periodic contributes to pymatgen, the Materials Project codebase, Custodian (their DFT runner), and Torch Sim, created by researcher Abhijit Gangan and still supported by him, along with Jackson MD. They are active Megatron contributors and run an academic grants program funding university groups advancing toward synthesis superintelligence; the first paper from that funding is expected soon.

Open questions and unresolved tensions

Several threads remain genuinely unresolved in the conversation. The founders do not claim to know whether reversible or quantum computation escapes the Landauer energy cost — Cubuk says plainly he is not an expert. They do not have a systematic map of abstraction levels for automation; Cubuk offers atomic structure, continuum models, thermodynamics, and kinetics as levels, and concedes he lacks a systematic answer. They acknowledge that combinatorial sputtering — creating gradient compositions to try hundreds or thousands of crystals at once — has not worked well because diffusion mixes everything, and that high-throughput superconductivity measurement remains a bottleneck, with a single measurement taking hours. The idea of mixing candidates into one sample and testing, analogous to pooled COVID tests, exists but only some versions work.

The clearest strategic bet is that characterization, not synthesis, is the binding constraint. Cubuk says the founding hypothesis was that automated characterization is the large bottleneck — mixing powders is not that hard, but if you cannot characterize, analyze, and wisely choose the next step, random powder mixing yields little. That is why Periodic focused on automated characterization and closing the loop through simulations. Whether that bet pays off, and whether the timestamped RL environments built from proprietary campaign data produce capabilities that frontier models cannot reach through inference alone, is the thing to watch.

Key Takeaways
Periodic Labs' Liam Fedus and Ekin Dogus Cubuk argue AI-driven autonomous labs, not pure compute, are the bottleneck to materials discovery, since noisy physical experiments like XRD phase identification require sample-efficient reasoning that clean digital training cannot provide.
Summary
Periodic Labs co-founders Liam Fedus and Ekin Dogus Cubuk argue that AI's next frontier is not more reasoning tokens but physical experiments, because scientific discovery is by definition the domain you were not trained on. The episode's title promises "autonomous labs and synthesis superintelligence," and that is genuinely the spine of the conversation, but the most useful material for a technical reader is the operational detail: how experimental noise, lossy characterization, and negative results get turned into reinforcement-learning environments, and why the founders think a frontier model will still have to run experiments even at generation seven.

Fedus (formerly of OpenAI, where he worked on large-model training) and Cubuk (a materials scientist who came out of solid-state physics and DFT work) describe building Periodic Labs as a Bell Labs-style merger of theorists, experimentalists, hardware and software engineers, and LLM researchers under one roof in Menlo Park. The discussion ranges across why "intelligence is necessary but insufficient," how X-ray diffraction and multimodality resolve phase ambiguity, why DFT cannot replace a lab even if it were perfect, the Landauer limit as a materials problem, and what deployment engineers will actually do for semiconductor customers. The reader should walk away with a concrete mental model of the closed experimental loop Periodic is trying to build, the specific bottlenecks it is betting on, and the founders' explicit skepticism that pure inference-time reasoning can substitute for touching the universe.

Scientific discovery is the domain you were not trained on

The founders' central thesis is that machine learning is most effective on what it was trained on, and scientific discovery is almost by definition the thing outside that distribution. Cubuk states this directly: "what is machine learning very effective in that, on what it trained. But scientific discovery — this, by definition, is, why don't you trained." This is why they build physical laboratories rather than relying on ever-better models — so that both open and closed models can interact with the universe. Fedus adds the corollary: "no one can just like that, 'from scratch', create superconductor room temperature."

The premise is stated on Periodic's own site, which the host quotes: "Intelligence necessary, but insufficient. New knowledge is created when ideas are found agreed with reality." Fedus explains the reasoning: the universe is too complicated to "think through" a discovery, so you must create hypotheses and check whether they are true. Reading a textbook or having a thought "short circuit in room" will not substitute for the iterative process of expected and unexpected experimental results.

A key structural argument: optimal reasoning strategies for mathematics or theoretical computer science cannot be optimal for science. In math and programming, the full context is available — axioms, consequences, functions, API calls — and you just have to be smart. In physics, you start with more atoms than you can ever store on a computer, so you must perform a reduction of dimensionality. The founders cite thermodynamics as the original example: early engineers were confused by steam engines until five or six thermodynamic variables (temperature, pressure) were found to explain the behavior of 10^23 to 10^27 atoms.

"We believe that it is possible to do great discovery, nothing testing. Yes, no one can just like that, 'from scratch', create superconductor room temperature." — Liam Fedus

Fedus notes that current AI improvements concentrate on math, programming, and theoretical computer science because those are easier for LLMs, but most real-world tasks that require intelligence resemble science: solid uncertainty, noise, missing context, and the need to decide what to do next anyway. He predicts theoretical physics may be the next domain to fall, but distinguishes that sharply from the physical world.

The experimental loop: noisy data, lossy characterization, and RL environments

This is the operational core of the episode. The founders describe a four-stage cycle for discovering new materials: (1) determine what to create — atomic stability plus target properties; (2) synthesize it — determining processing conditions, which they call extremely difficult even when the structure is known; (3) characterize it — the material comes out unlabeled; (4) decide the next step. Each stage carries distinct uncertainty.

Fedus gives the concrete example of the oven: over time it wears out because processed materials evaporate and coat the heating element, so every use makes it worse. Temperature in the furnace is not uniform, so where you place the material affects the result. Optical tools suffer from vibration — Cubuk recounts a doctoral lab where results varied until they discovered someone walking on the floor above at night was vibrating the laser setup.

The characterization bottleneck is where AI earns its keep first. Periodic directs X-rays at material; the diffraction pattern is an imprint of crystalline structure, but identifying what phases are actually present is very hard. Their early AI work built systems for phase identification, rewarded for correctly identifying present phases and penalized for incorrect phases or chemically invalid ones. Fedus frames this as a "small supporting thing" that, assembled with the rest, yields the full discovery cycle.

The most sophisticated idea in the episode is how they build RL environments from accumulated experimental data. By timestamping the state of the world — "on this date we had the following experimental evidence" — they can create environments where the model must choose the next scientist's step or predict the result of an experiment. This matters because of a contamination problem Fedus describes: if a model already memorized part of the data and you build tasks depending on those answers, the model can simulate work — it "gets the correct answer" without performing complex physical reasoning, and you then reinforce reasoning strategies that will not generalize to new systems. Traceable, timestamped experimental data lets them build environments that are impossible under other conditions.

Why DFT cannot replace the lab, even if it were perfect

Cubuk gives a compact explanation of density functional theory: Kohn and Hohenberg showed you do not need to work with the exponential Hilbert space of the wave function — everything needed for ground-state properties is a functional of charge density, which is only a three-dimensional object. Kohn and Sham then proposed a practical approximation via the Kohn-Sham equations. Cubuk notes the theorem is simple enough that a physics student can understand it, and that DFT is probably the most-used simulation method in materials.

But he is emphatic that DFT is not a substitute for a laboratory, for two reasons. First, DFT is by definition not required to be an approximation — the Kohn-Sham theorem shows it can be exact — but we lack access to the exchange-correlation functional, and for charge-density-based models we do not know the kinetic energy functional. Second, even with perfect DFT, you cannot fit 10^23 atoms into a computer. Fedus adds that even 100 million DFT tests would not be enough, because DFT typically models perfect crystals while real crystals have microstructure — medium-order structure that significantly influences properties — and some properties, like high-temperature superconductivity, are essentially impossible to model with DFT.

The practical division of labor: some measurements are easier and more accurate in simulation, others in experiment. Periodic uses DFT to estimate enthalpy of formation — if a material's energy is higher than the alternatives its atoms could turn into, it is unlikely to be synthesizable; stability means lying on the convex hull of competing materials. For a long time the Materials Project, described as the best open-source DFT database, used experimental calibration for DFT, and Periodic does the same internally: when interested in a chemical system, they run experiments and simulations and calibrate them against each other.

"Even if we could spend 100 million tests with DFT, we could never check their microstructure... even if you spend 100 million DFT tests, this will be not enough." — Ekin Dogus Cubuk

Phase transitions, multimodality, and resolving ambiguity

Cubuk explains phase transitions for a non-physics audience via melting ice and boiling water, then via the Ising model — spins pointing up or down, entropy winning at high temperature, sudden organization at a critical point. In Periodic's labs, phase transitions occur when precursors are mixed and temperature is raised to drive reaction; the atoms react and form a new crystal, which shows up on radiographs because sharp changes in atomic geometry change the diffraction pattern significantly.

The practical problem: when you try something for the first time, the result is rarely a clean yes or no. It is usually a mixed phase — some precursors, some amorphous phases, and a bunch of phases you did not intend to create. This is where simulations and AI must be combined for characterization. Simulations might say "this phase is a small variation of what we predicted"; AI might say "given prior experiments, this is probably not the phase we want, so change the conditions." In some cases a phase appears that has never been recorded in any article or database, and AI uses tools to ask what atomic configuration could explain the observed pattern.

The resolution strategy is multimodality. Two different phases can produce the same XRD pattern, so Periodic also measures electrical and magnetic properties and morphology via electron microscopy. Phases that look similar on XRD look different under other measurements. Cubuk's point is that a human given 10 modalities simultaneously and asked to analyze them in sequence struggles, but for AI this does not require superintelligence — it just requires performing many calculations and viewing all the data. Fedus frames the payoff as better decision-making under uncertainty: the system integrates signals from many tools over long time periods and across replicates, building a deeper model instead of over-relying on one measurement from one device.

The founders also address data retention. Cubuk says they do not throw data away — if God were watching the experiment there would be more data than any computer could hold, but mortals cannot track every atom, so all collected data is valuable. Fedus offers an analogy to probing a base model: God has the complete picture of the material and experiment; the experiment is a small linear probe with eight bits of output, and the job of the human or agent is to reconstruct what is happening inside from those eight bits.

Maxwell's demon, the Landauer limit, and energy as the real currency

Cubuk connects the data-retention question to Maxwell's demon: the 1800s thought experiment of a tiny omniscient demon sorting low-energy atoms to reduce entropy and violate the second law. Landauer's modern argument resolves it — deleting information requires dissipating energy, and the demon would have to delete enough information that the entropy increase is guaranteed. Cubuk calls the host's question about future data deletion "far-sighted."

This leads to one of the more striking forward-looking bets in the episode: whether we can approach the Landauer limit for computational energy efficiency. Cubuk notes that energy per flop has fallen exponentially, Moore's-law-style. Fedus recalls first learning about the limit 10 to 15 years ago, when we were roughly 10 to 20 orders of magnitude away; the last time he looked, we were within three or four orders of magnitude, or closer. He calls getting closer to the Landauer limit "amazing for humanity" because computation is probably one of the most important things humanity does, and energy is "the real currency of the universe."

Cubuk adds the heat-dissipation angle: calculations release heat that must be shed. On quantum computing, he is candid that he is not an expert and does not understand whether reversible computation avoids the energy cost — reversible processes do not delete information, so in principle they need not spend energy on the process itself, but he does not know if it works. He notes quantum computation must obey thermodynamics. He also relays that he agrees with Elad Gil's skepticism about near-term quantum applications, and makes a sharper point: even a perfect quantum computer would still face the same microstructure problems DFT has, because people would still be modeling the same "perfect crystal."

Automation pragmatism: IQ-140 instruments, not humanoid robots

The founders' most-quoted line — that every piece of equipment in the lab will have an IQ of 140 — comes from a concrete bottleneck. In early scaling, they had equipment technicians and scientists hunting for a certain morphology to determine what they were actually making. They first tried simple software for data collection on scanning electron microscopes: a program that selected a field of view, took a picture, approached, took another. Fedus says the data was too primitive for what they wanted to count, so they embedded AI systems directly in the machines. Now those systems hold the full context — the goal of the experiment, what they were trying to synthesize, other experimental evidence — and collect data intelligently, producing a much richer dataset for future models.

On latency, the constraint is not what an outside observer would guess. Experiments take hours or days, but specific stages matter: precise control, cost (slow models are expensive models), and human patience — a scientist waiting two hours for an XRD analysis versus two minutes. Fedus notes that deep research is not one tool call or one stream of conclusions, so latency compounds across many tokens. The split between tool-call latency (mostly DFT or computational methods) and reasoning latency depends on the process.

On humanoid robots, Fedus is explicit that Periodic believes humanoid robots would slow them down. The pragmatic approach is to connect people and automation, find bottlenecks in the experimental process, and eliminate them one by one. Tasks requiring dexterity, where humans are wonderful and automation would take months, are not worth automating; routine tasks that consume huge amounts of scientist and technician time are. Full autonomy is not the goal — high-quality, high-volume, diverse data is.

A concrete anecdote illustrates AI's role in data quality: at one stage of a cycle, an error arose because a machine was loaded incorrectly and the patterns were contradictory. The AI analyzed the data, said the result was not expected given what was launched, considered the array in dynamics, and determined that a cyclic permutation would make the data consistent. The team then returned to the physical infrastructure and found the loading error. Cubuk adds that reducing background noise in experimental data is critical — most literature experimental data is noisy enough that it performs worse than DFT accuracy, because different people in different labs at different times add variability. Standardizing workflows is one of the largest expected advantages.

Negative results, process data, and the campaign as the unit of learning

The founders treat negative results as a first-class asset. Fedus defines a negative result as intending to create a structure and finding all evidence indicates you did not — or, in some cases, discovering a new structure you were not looking for. Cubuk notes that classification algorithms need negative samples, and materials science has a structural problem: people publish only crystals they managed to synthesize and almost never publish synthesis failures. It is also never known whether a crystal is fundamentally unsynthesizable or just a matter of scale, method, or technique.

The more valuable data type, per Fedus, is process engineering: the series of negative results that eventually led to a positive one. Even when a material is known, reproduction can be difficult. Periodic accumulates this experience and builds systems that, given a number of negative results, can tell you how to reach the positive case. Cubuk notes that if you label each experiment, most labels would be negative; if you label each campaign — assuming campaigns end at success — the dataset is more balanced. Fedus says such data virtually does not exist elsewhere, and Periodic spends a lot of time laying the full scientific pedigree into the model: conversations, intuitive guesses, what was done in labs, where calculations were run, where code was written.

This is framed as analogous to recursive self-improvement, but with a twist: the model is taught to run better experiments, not to train better models. Cubuk notes the loop does not return to the base model — unless the physical models developed improve chips that then improve AI, making it a broader, higher-order RSI. Fedus argues that if inference-time reasoning were sufficient, leading labs would have stopped at GPT-4 and improved only at inference. He believes compression into weights will produce different systems and capabilities, and that even a seventh-generation model will still have to spend experiments, because scientific discovery is the thing outside its training distribution.

"Even if the model seventh generation will be very good, even better than now, she doesn't care will have to spend experiments for receiving results. And the reason is that machine learning very good copes with the fact that what is it on trained. And scientific discovery is almost by definition, that is, on why weren't you trained?" — Ekin Dogus Cubuk

Scaling labs, hiring, and the deployment-engineer role

Periodic's scaling thesis is that new types of laboratories matter as much as more of them. Fedus says resources will allow significant scaling of labs and compute, but emphasizes building new lab types. Cubuk says labs of this type have not been created at this scale or with this approach, and that lessons from building the first Menlo Park lab inform later versions. Some tools are very expensive; as they learn which bring the greatest return, they invest more, making hardware engineering central. In early stages they bought ready-made appliances for speed; at scale they build their own, discovering which components become bottlenecks and which design details reduce noise — for example, reworking a robot manipulator's rest position so it does not sit above a plate of mixed substances and risk contamination.

The hiring page spans AI, infrastructure, computation, experimental roles, hardware, and product. Cubuk frames these as holistic toward the goal of synthesis superintelligence, including product engineers who turn research results into tools experimenters can use. A guiding principle: highly skilled, experienced people should do hands-on work. Fedus contrasts this with academia, where writing grants and teaching leave no time for practical research, and cites Bell Labs and IBM — Bardeen was in the labs every day despite being a theorist; Alex Müller ran experiments even as a leader.

On deployment engineers: Periodic built its own tools, AI, and compute for itself, so it was its first customer. It now offers those tools to industry, focused on semiconductors, which requires working in some of the most protected environments. Deployment engineers and researchers will work on-site, integrating AI or computational systems to help partners. Fedus says this is a significantly expanded role versus standard deployment engineering — it requires deep ML expertise, precision with data and infrastructure, and physics/chemistry/materials knowledge. It partly means willingness to spend long stretches in Taiwan.

On monetization, Fedus offers software development as the analogue: GitHub Copilot and early ChatGPT as co-pilots, then Codex and far less hand-written code. He expects a similar arc for Periodic — systems that speed up materials scientists and process engineers, then, as autonomy grows, systems that evaluate results toward a target state. Cubuk frames the best contribution to solid-state physics as making these tools profitable, so that materials research demonstrates commercial impact and attracts young people to physics, as ChatGPT did for CS and LLM majors. He notes Bell Labs had huge commercial impact but failed to commercialize some of its own achievements, though it benefited greatly from the vacuum tube connecting the east and west coasts by telephone.

Historical precedents: MgB2, nickelates, and the case for brute-force search

Cubuk's most concrete argument for automation is the magnesium diboride story. A Japanese group tested roughly 30,000 substances; about 30 showed interesting superconductivity, and MgB2 was one — the conventional superconductor with the highest temperature at atmospheric pressure. MgB2 had sat on shelves as a precursor for decades, and BCS theory was invented in 1957. The discovery was not a direct result of theory and not a synthesis limitation — it was brute-force trying of many materials. Cubuk's point: with the perfect automation described, you could try those 30,000 options in one month instead of a career.

On high-temperature superconductivity, Cubuk distinguishes conventional superconductivity (mostly electron-phonon interaction, showing the isotope effect) from unconventional superconductors like cuprates above 77 K, such as 93 K, which do not obey that physics and whose governing physics is unknown. He notes we still cannot simulate strong correlation in simple quantum systems, and DFT cannot catch some strong-correlation effects. He cites Phil Anderson's "more is different" — you can understand all the basic laws and still find that many objects behave qualitatively differently than simple physics suggests. He draws a parallel to deep learning: power laws everywhere, emergent behavior, and no understanding of why.

The nickelate anecdote: Alex Müller tried nickelates (nickel, oxygen, some cations) before cuprates (copper, oxygen, some cations); cuprates worked and won him the Nobel, but we later learned nickelates were also a good idea — nickel and copper sit side by side on the periodic table. Haidong Wang, a Stanford professor, realized nickelates could be created as thin films and demonstrated superconductivity. Cubuk's conclusion: with powerful synthesis superintelligence, they will not run out of ideas — 3D transition metals and oxygen offer plenty, and people have tried cobaltates too.

On open source, Periodic contributes to pymatgen, the Materials Project codebase, Custodian (their DFT runner), and Torch Sim, created by researcher Abhijit Gangan and still supported by him, along with Jackson MD. They are active Megatron contributors and run an academic grants program funding university groups advancing toward synthesis superintelligence; the first paper from that funding is expected soon.

Open questions and unresolved tensions

Several threads remain genuinely unresolved in the conversation. The founders do not claim to know whether reversible or quantum computation escapes the Landauer energy cost — Cubuk says plainly he is not an expert. They do not have a systematic map of abstraction levels for automation; Cubuk offers atomic structure, continuum models, thermodynamics, and kinetics as levels, and concedes he lacks a systematic answer. They acknowledge that combinatorial sputtering — creating gradient compositions to try hundreds or thousands of crystals at once — has not worked well because diffusion mixes everything, and that high-throughput superconductivity measurement remains a bottleneck, with a single measurement taking hours. The idea of mixing candidates into one sample and testing, analogous to pooled COVID tests, exists but only some versions work.

The clearest strategic bet is that characterization, not synthesis, is the binding constraint. Cubuk says the founding hypothesis was that automated characterization is the large bottleneck — mixing powders is not that hard, but if you cannot characterize, analyze, and wisely choose the next step, random powder mixing yields little. That is why Periodic focused on automated characterization and closing the loop through simulations. Whether that bet pays off, and whether the timestamped RL environments built from proprietary campaign data produce capabilities that frontier models cannot reach through inference alone, is the thing to watch.

📌 Periodic Labs, the $7.5B valuation with nothing published yet
The company went from a $1.3B valuation to a reported $7.5B in nine months without publishing a paper, announcing a discovery or naming a single customer.
The shape is familiar from OpenAI’s own first years, which were secretive, slow and apparently going nowhere until they produced something that reset the industry.
Liam Fedus co-created ChatGPT and ran post-training at OpenAI as VP of research. He resigned in March 2025 and two months later founded Periodic Labs with Ekin Dogus Cubuk, who had spent eight years at Google leading machine learning for physics.
They made the company public on 30 September 2025 with a $300M seed round led by Andreessen Horowitz at a $1.3B valuation. Bezos, Eric Schmidt, Nvidia’s venture arm, Accel and DST took part. OpenAI did not, despite Fedus saying it would when he announced his departure. Bloomberg reported talks at around $7B in March 2026 and Forbes put it at a minimum of $500M at $7.5B in May this year. 
Last week, on 8 October the founders gave their most detailed interview yet and confirmed another large raise is coming.
What it is, and the correction behind it
The company is a single laboratory in Menlo Park, filled with robotic arms, furnaces and X-ray equipment, where the work amounts to mixing powders, heating them and seeing what comes out. What comes out are crystals, which here does not mean gemstones but any solid whose atoms sit in a repeating pattern, the stuff inside of chips, magnets and batteries. Finding a useful one has historically taken between ten and twenty years.
Their target is a better superconductor, a material that carries electricity with no loss at all, which is why superconductors sit inside MRI scanners and maglev trains, although they only behave that way when kept extremely cold. The record at normal pressure is around 135 K, roughly 138 degrees below zero, and it has stood for decades.
On 19 January 2026 Nature corrected the paper behind the A-Lab, a robotic laboratory at Berkeley that had claimed 41 new materials synthesised in 17 days with almost no human involvement. After an audit by researchers at Princeton and University College London, the authors clarified that “new” meant new to their prediction software, not necessarily new to science, and the count fell to 36. Eighteen months earlier, two chemists had dismantled GNoME, the Google DeepMind system that used AI to predict 2.2 million new crystals, arguing that a material only counts if it is credible, novel and useful at once.
Cubuk was the corresponding author of GNoME and co-authored the A-Lab paper. Which makes Periodic Labs less a startup than a rectification, and explains the line on its homepage: intelligence is necessary, but not sufficient.
1. Thinking harder is not a method
The universe is too complicated to be reasoned through, so you conjecture and then you check. Fedus likes to say what science is not, and his version is that it “isn’t sitting in a room thinking really hard.” The industry consensus is the opposite, that a good enough reasoner eventually gets there.
2. The lesson comes from the experiment, not the text
A model improves by being told when it got something right. For almost every AI system that verdict comes from text, where the answer was already written down by somebody. Periodic takes it from its own furnaces instead, which is messier: materials do not come out labelled, furnaces drift as evaporated material coats the heating element, and in Cubuk’s doctoral lab results changed depending on who was walking on the floor above. The reasoning that wins at maths, where there is precision and no variance, is not the reasoning that survives that.
3. A model that knows the answer fakes the work
If a model already absorbed a result while being trained on the internet, and the task you set depends on that result, it arrives at the right answer without doing the physics. You then reward the shortcut and teach it a habit that collapses on anything genuinely new. Their defence is private data with dates attached: what did the scientist do next, knowing only what was known that day.
4. The failures are the asset
Scientific journals publish the experiments that worked and almost never the ones that did not, so a model trained on that literature only ever sees successes and has no idea what failure looks like. Running the experiments in-house produces the failures, and with them the exact run of attempts that eventually worked. In a field where repeating a result is often harder than getting it the first time, that run is the knowledge worth having.
5. The value sits in the process, not the paper
“Most AI has been trained on the final artifacts of science,” says Fedus. A published paper is the tidy summary, written months afterwards with the dead ends taken out. Periodic keeps the whole trail instead: the conversations, the hunches, what was tried and in what order. Nobody else has that written down anywhere, which is their argument for why a bigger model cannot catch up simply by reading more.
6. The bottleneck moved within months
They built up the laboratory expecting the limit to be how many experiments they could run, and it turned out not to be, because within months the harder question was working out what they had actually produced each time, so their first serious AI work went into identifying the contents of a sample rather than into anything more impressive. Fedus has said publicly that the speed of that shift surprised them.
7. The plan is to sell the result, not the tool
Revenue comes from engineers posted inside client sites, who run the models on the customer’s own machines and train them on its private data. The focus is semiconductors, and one job advert asks for Mandarin and long stays in Taiwan. As the systems get more autonomous, Fedus says, they can charge for the result rather than for the software and keep the intellectual property, the way a pharmaceutical company keeps the patent on a molecule it discovers. No customer has been named and no revenue disclosed.
8. The strategy is to industrialise luck
Magnesium diboride is still the best superconductor of its kind that works without extreme pressure. A Japanese group found it by trying around 30,000 materials over the course of a career, of which some thirty showed anything interesting. No theory pointed them there, and the ingredients had been sitting on laboratory shelves for decades. The bet is not that a machine skips the trial and error but that it gets through it in a month. The stated target is around 1,000 experiments a day within a year.
The numbers
Two listed companies do something comparable and have been at it for over a decade, using computation to find drugs and materials. Recursion is worth around $1.7B on $66M of annual revenue, and Schrödinger around $1.1B on $256M. At $7.5B, Periodic would be worth more than both together, a year after incorporation and with nothing published.
Its one technical claim is Neon, a model released on 15 September. Reading an X-ray diffraction pattern means working out which compounds a sample actually contains, the step that tells the lab whether the experiment worked at all. Periodic says Neon gets that right 55.3% of the time, against 2.7% for the open model it was built from. The test set is its own and nobody outside the company has checked the results.
The buyer is the harder problem. Chip plants do not order a new material, they qualify it, which means proving it behaves identically batch after batch before it goes anywhere near a production line. A leading-edge plant has more than 200 of these critical inputs and qualification has historically taken around 18 months, a gate that discovery speed does not compress.
Why it matters
Two theories about how knowledge gets made are now funded against each other. If Periodic is right, buying more computing power stops being enough on its own, and what becomes valuable is privileged access to a corner of the real world that produces data nobody else has. If it is wrong, a general model eventually swallows the lab, the furnaces and the $7.5B. Either way the answer arrives, which is rare in an industry that mostly argues about the unfalsifiable.
The wider version has nothing to do with superconductors. Producing something plausible is now cheap, whether a design, a strategy or an analysis. Finding out whether it was right has barely got cheaper, and Cubuk spent $300M on furnaces after learning that an untested prediction is not knowledge but a confident guess. Three things to watch: the first peer-reviewed paper, hard evidence of autonomous operation, and a customer with a name and a number.
🌱 New Venture Funds on the Street
24 funds · 21 managers · $7.22B closed · 6 first-time funds
$7.2B closed across 24 funds this week, spread across several large raises rather than concentrated in a single manager. Forbion accounts for over a third of the total, followed by Square Peg, USV and TCGX, and the long tail runs from deep tech in the Netherlands and Italy to space in Poland and Texas.
The more consequential shift is in LP composition. Eli Lilly sits in Forbion’s investor base, and Junzheng, Muyuan and Zijin Mining together supplied the majority of Gaorong’s $486M. Taken together with Tesco’s anchor commitment to Bramble last week, strategic capital is taking a growing share of fund formation, and it comes with different motives from pensions and endowments: pipeline visibility, M&A optionality and early access to technology. 🏦 LP Watch
Eli Lilly spent about $32B on 13 acquisitions this year. Now it’s an LP in Forbion’s $2.6B.
It was already the exit market for a chunk of biotech, with at least 50 venture investors getting cash back from its buying this year. If you’re in a fund with a pharma LP, ask the GP what information rights that LP has and whether they reach portfolio data. Usually it’s aggregate reporting only. The one time it isn’t, you’ve handed metrics to a future bidder.
Gaorong closed $448M and five industrial LPs supplied $268M of it.
Junzheng $137.5M for over 30%, the pig farmer Muyuan $48.1M, Zijin-linked entities $41.3M, Jiahua Energy $27.5M, Newland Digital $13.8M. Matrix Partners China has $511M from SF Express, East Money and Vipshop toward a $962M target. Raising RMB now works on supply chain adjacency, not returns.
Lazio Innova has anchored two deeptech funds at exactly 80%, seven days apart.
€18.83M of Nevermind’s €23.6M this week, €16M of Eureka’s €20M last week. Below half, a public anchor is validation you can take to other LPs. Above it, the mandate isn’t yours and geographic deployment obligations come attached. Founders should ask what share of a fund carries a regional restriction, because a lead that can’t follow you out of the region is a problem at Series B.
Khazanah caps every commitment at 30% of the manager’s own raise.
Jelawang backed Superscrypt and TNB Aura this week, five managers now, out of roughly $236M. That 30% is the number to cite when an anchor asks for more and calls it standard. Two state programmes running opposite designs: Lazio guarantees funds exist, Khazanah guarantees they were wanted.
Endeavor Catalyst V took 30% of its $320M from its own entrepreneurs.
Founders at Nubank, Revolut and Checkout.com putting their own money back into the network that backed them. Founder LPs are normally a rounding error at that size. At 30% they’re the strategy, and it’s the cheapest differentiated LP base on the list.
Also this week: BGK sponsoring Poland’s first space fund at Vinci’s $128M, NABARD anchoring NABVENTURES II, the British Business Bank into Fuel Ventures at $12.3M, Invest-NL and Oost NL into Graduate Ventures, and the Czech National Development Bank funding DEPO’s $4.3M vehicle for Ukrainian founders relocating to Czechia.
💰 Who Cashed Out
What closed or got signed this week: 19 transactions, with $5.1B in disclosed M&A and $577M in IPO proceeds. 
US buyers carried the M&A value, while the listings came from Hong Kong, India and the US.
Full price for certainty, team deals for the rest
4 acquisitions account for virtually all of the $5.1B in disclosed M&A value this week, and in each one the buyer was paying for something it could underwrite with confidence. Shionogi paid $2B for IntraBio entirely upfront, with no milestones or earnout, because Aqneursa already carries FDA approval. Uber paid $2.3B in cash for ezCater, roughly $700M above its 2021 valuation, for a marketplace with established corporate demand. Hexagon paid $535M for Rocscience, buying out TA Associates and the founders in a single transaction, and Grindr committed up to $320M for Freddie.
Below those 4, the structures change. Harness acquired Augment Code’s products and team rather than the company, after Augment had raised $252M. Temporal hired Oso’s team while Tiny bought the product separately for $1.9M, and Turso’s founders join Supabase as part of its acquisition. When a buyer purchases assets or hires a team, the proceeds go to the company and are paid out through the preference stack, so the founders’ outcome depends less on the price than on how much capital sits ahead of them. Buyers are paying full value where the risk has been removed and paying for people where it has not. With much of the 2021 vintage still looking for an exit, the second kind of deal is likely to become more common.

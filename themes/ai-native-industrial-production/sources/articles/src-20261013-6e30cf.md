Abstract
In outcome-selective Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer (PIT), reward-related cues selectively energize instrumental responding for associated outcomes. In humans, this effect has been attributed both to automatic associative processes and to cognitive inferences about which rewards are available in the presence of a cue. The present research examined whether outcome-selective PIT is influenced by manipulations that discourage such inferences. Across three experiments using a stock-market scenario with monetary rewards, participants learned Pavlovian cue–outcome relations and instrumental response–outcome contingencies before a transfer test. Experiment 1 established an outcome-selective PIT effect, which was substantially larger among participants reporting strategic use of the cues. Experiment 2 showed that instructing participants to forget the Pavlovian relations after an ostensible software error significantly reduced this effect. Experiment 3 showed that a concurrent working-memory load during the transfer test also attenuated PIT. However, a small effect persisted under the combined manipulations. Together, these findings indicate that outcome-selective PIT is modulated by cognitive reward expectancies and by beliefs about cue relevance. The residual transfer is not readily explained by experimenter demand, which here worked against cue-based responding. Cue reactivity therefore appears to rest on reward expectations that persist even when they are false and have been explicitly disavowed.

Introduction
Pavlovian reward signals can exert a strong influence on instrumental behavior, biasing action selection and enhancing response vigor in both adaptive and maladaptive ways
1
,
2
,
3
. These Pavlovian–instrumental interactions have often been explained by associative processes that guide reward-seeking behavior relatively automatically
4
, 
5
. However, alternative accounts have emphasized the contribution of cognitively mediated inferences about reward availability in humans
6
. Thus, it remains an open question whether these phenomena are driven primarily by automatic associative processes or cognitively mediated inferences.
Distinguishing between these accounts requires a closer look at the experimental paradigms used to investigate Pavlovian–instrumental interactions, particularly the Pavlovian-to-instrumental transfer (PIT) procedure. A typical reward-based PIT paradigm comprises three phases: (1) a Pavlovian learning phase, in which participants learn that conditioned stimuli (CS+) signal the delivery of specific rewards (e.g., tokens); (2) an instrumental learning phase, in which participants learn to obtain specific rewards through their own actions (e.g., by pressing specific response keys); and (3) a transfer test, in which participants can freely select among instrumental responses during presentations of Pavlovian cues without explicit reward delivery (i.e., under nominal extinction). A widely replicated finding is the outcome-selective PIT effect: Instrumental responding for a specific reward is selectively enhanced in the presence of the CS+ associated with that reward, relative to presentations of a CS+ paired with a different reward or a CS– associated with no reward (for reviews, see
1
,
6
,
7
).
A large body of work with animals suggests that outcome-selective PIT results from associative processes implicated in Pavlovian and instrumental learning (e.g
2
,
8
,
9
). Specifically, Pavlovian training is assumed to establish stimulus–outcome (S–O) associations, whereas instrumental training is assumed to establish response–outcome (R–O) associations. Given the bidirectional nature of these associative links, activation is assumed to spread from the CS + to its associated outcome representation, and then, via the R–O association, to the corresponding response representation
10
.
An alternative account holds that conditioning procedures lead participants to form cognitive expectancies about the availability of outcomes across learning phases (e.g
6
,
11
). Specifically, human participants may infer from the Pavlovian conditioning procedure that the CS+ signals the availability of a specific reward and, from instrumental learning, that this reward can be obtained through a specific action. During the transfer test, participants may then integrate these inferences into the belief that performing the action associated with the cued reward will be more advantageous. Consistent with this inferential account, when participants were asked to rate the likelihood of reward for each response in the presence of a CS, they gave the highest ratings to the response associated with the same outcome as the cue
12
. Conversely, when participants believed that the cues did not signal which response would be rewarded, no outcome-selective PIT effect was observed
13
.
Both accounts face difficulties in explaining some findings. For example, associative models struggle to explain instruction effects that can reverse outcome-selective transfer: Explicitly informing participants after conditioning that a CS signals which response will 
not
 be rewarded during the transfer test has been shown to motivate participants to bias choice away from the cued response
12
, 
14
; for a conceptual replication in the aversive domain). Inferential accounts, on the other hand, have difficulty explaining why human participants would form and maintain the factually unwarranted belief that working for the signaled outcome would be more effective. Indeed, if participants have correctly inferred from the Pavlovian learning phase that a CS predicts the receipt of a specific reward, it would be rational to invest less, and not more, effort in working for that reward.
However, the formation of such unwarranted beliefs in humans may be promoted by specific task procedures, such as vignettes that assign additional functional meanings to CSs, responses, outcomes, and their relations. For example, Allman et al.
15
 used a stock market vignette in which the CSs represented individual companies trading with different token currencies (as outcomes) during the Pavlovian learning phase. Participants thus learned about successful trades by these companies. In the subsequent instrumental learning phase, participants adopted the role of a stockbroker trading on their own behalf (by pressing response keys). For the transfer phase, they were only informed that “you will see a stimulus [the CS] on the screen,” without further explanation. Within the context of this vignette, participants may therefore have reasoned that the companies continued to trade successfully during the transfer test and that copying their trades would be advantageous, thereby producing an outcome-selective PIT effect.
These functional beliefs about the roles of the CSs during a PIT test may constitute implicit demand characteristics that motivate responding toward a common outcome. According to influential frameworks
16
,
17
, demand characteristics must meet three conditions to generate a hypothesis-consistent effect: (1) participants must detect them; (2) they must interpret them in a manner consistent with the experimenter’s hypothesis; and (3) they must be motivated to confirm that hypothesis.
Some of these conditions may be well met in PIT tasks with human participants
15
. First, after extensive learning sessions, participants become highly sensitive to the cue–outcome and response–outcome relations. Based on this contingency knowledge, they may detect the convergence between relations that yield the same outcome. Second, during the PIT test, participants may then misinterpret the PIT cues as indicators of which rewards are most readily attainable and select those responses that produce the cued outcome. Although these beliefs are factually incorrect, they are not refuted by experience due to the extinction procedure during the PIT test and may therefore be maintained. Consequently, and third, participants may pursue the cued outcomes because they believe that doing so will earn them higher rewards, or because they believe that the experimenter wants them to do so. Because it rests on a misinterpretation of the cues, this demand explanation would considerably weaken the validity of PIT studies, as the effect may emerge only within narrow task contexts. In the present study, we therefore address this interpretational ambiguity by informing participants explicitly that the Pavlovian relations are not predictive of reinforcement in the PIT test.
We were not the first who instructed participants to ignore the PIT cues during the transfer test. For example, Seabrooke et al.
12
 told participants, both before and continuously during the test, that the cues were uninformative (“Pictures do not indicate which arrow key is more likely to be rewarded!”). The cueing effect was significantly reduced but not abolished overall, an outcome the authors attributed to the possibility that not all participants believed the experimenter’s instructions. Asking participants first to learn Pavlovian relations and later to ignore this knowledge may indeed appear contradictory, as it undermines the perceived “legitimate purpose” of a study procedure
16
. Consequently, participants may deliberately or inadvertently rely on the learned contingencies even when those contingencies have become outdated
13
,
18
. Thus, the question remains whether participants will continue to use the cues for response selection when they are provided with a better justification to ignore the cues during the transfer test.
Furthermore, research investigating post-training instruction effects on PIT responding has produced mixed evidence regarding their effectiveness. For example, Seabrooke et al.
19
 combined a reversal instruction, specifying which response would 
not
 be rewarded during the transfer test, with a working-memory load manipulation (memorization of a six-digit sequence). As expected, the reversal instruction reversed the outcome-selective PIT effect in a transfer test without working-memory load. Under load, however, it neither reversed nor restored the effect (i.e., no PIT effect was observed in either direction). Notably, working-memory load did not affect the magnitude of outcome-selective PIT under standard instructions. It therefore remains unclear why standard instructions, and the corresponding beliefs about response efficacy, are translated into behavior more readily than reversal instructions, given that both are simple to follow.
Research investigating effects of Pavlovian extinction on outcome-selective PIT has also produced mixed findings. A common observation is that extinguishing specific cue–outcome relations by presenting cues without their outcomes does not reduce the outcome-selective PIT effect in standard paradigms
13
,
20
, and does so only in paradigms that use compound stimuli combining extinguished and non-extinguished components
21
. The absence of extinction effects in standard paradigms poses a challenge for both associative S–O–R theories and propositional accounts. From an S–O–R perspective, weakening the cue‒outcome association should reduce the spread of activation from the outcome to the linked response representation. From a propositional or demand perspective, being convinced that cues no longer reliably predict specific rewards (as evident in post-extinction expectancy ratings) should block the inference that certain responses are more likely to be rewarded in the presence of those cues. Neither account, then, explains why extinction leaves outcome-selective PIT intact.
In summary, prior research suggests that cognitive inferences about outcome availability can influence cue reactivity in PIT tasks and that the expression of this influence depends on available cognitive resources. However, this evidence either rests on correlations with self-reported beliefs
13
, or is derived from verbal instructions that assigned the cues a particular relevance for responding, for example, by informing participants which responses would not be rewarded
12
. Moreover, explicit instructions to ignore the cues during the test phase did not abolish responding to the cues
22
, suggesting that participants were either unwilling or unable to comply. In the present research, we therefore combined a standard human PIT task with a directed-forgetting manipulation that instructed participants to forget the Pavlovian cue–outcome relations after learning, ostensibly because of a computer error. This manipulation thus gave participants a plausible reason to disregard the learned relations, and it made clear that the experimenter wanted them ignored. Any demand characteristic induced by this instruction should therefore have biased participants away from outcome-selective responding. Thus, a cue-motivated response bias that survived the manipulation would be difficult to attribute solely to participants’ beliefs about what experimenter expected.
The manipulation was informed by research on directed forgetting, in which individuals are instructed to intentionally forget previously learned material (for a review, see
23
). For example, Dreisbach and Bäuml
24
, instructed participants to forget highly overlearned S–R associations after a simulated computer crash. Congruency effects in reaction times were significantly reduced following the forgetting instruction relative to a remember instruction. In line with this research, we reasoned that a crash-induced forgetting instruction could also trigger intentional forgetting of the cue–outcome relations acquired during Pavlovian learning. Furthermore, the forgetting instruction, ostensibly necessitated by a computer error, unambiguously communicated that the Pavlovian cues should not be used.
We conducted three experiments. In Experiment 1, we established a reward-based PIT task using the vignette introduced by Allman et al.
15
. Experiment 2 added a directed-forgetting manipulation: following Pavlovian learning, a computer crash was simulated, and participants were instructed to forget everything they had learned up to that point. Experiment 3 repeated this manipulation and added a concurrent working-memory load using Seabrooke et al.
19
 digit-sequence memorization task. Across experiments, we were specifically interested in whether outcome-selective transfer effects would be attenuated, or abolished completely, once participants had a credible reason to disregard the Pavlovian cues.

Sample
Participants were recruited from the University of Würzburg’s mailing list. The sample comprised 35 participants (25 women and 10 men; 31 right-handed and 4 left-handed; age: 
M

Apparatus and stimuli
Participants were seated approximately 60 cm from a 24-in. computer monitor with a screen resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels. They responded using the “1,” “2,” and “3” keys on the number pad with the fingers of their dominant hand; these keys were marked with green labels. The experiment was programmed in E-Prime 2.0 Professional (Psychology Software Tools, Inc.).
The CSs consisted of eight visually distinct geometric figures: two stars, two squares, two triangles, and two circles (each 112 × 112 pixels), representing four cue identities (star, square, triangle, circle). Token outcomes (550 × 550 pixels) were represented by fictitious currency symbols: B$ (Botswana dollar), N$ (Niger dollar), T$ (Tanzania dollar), and “--” indicating no reward. During Pavlovian training, each cue identity was paired with a specific outcome.

The procedure was adapted from Allman et al.

Pavlovian training
Participants were instructed that geometric symbols representing company logos (CSs) would appear on the screen and that each company traded exclusively in one specific currency (outcome). Their task was to learn these CS–outcome associations.
On each trial, a geometric figure (star, square, circle, or triangle) appeared for 1,000 ms. After a 50-ms blank screen, a token currency sign (Outcome, 1T$, 1B$ or 1 N$) or the symbol “--” indicating no trade was presented as the outcome for 2,000 ms. Participants were instructed to press the spacebar when a trade (token currency sign) was presented and to refrain from pressing any keys when no outcome was presented. This outcome-monitoring task was included to ensure attention to stimulus–outcome contingencies. An error message was presented for 5,000 ms if participants failed to respond to a trade within 2,000 ms or responded during a no-trade outcome. The intertrial interval (ITI) varied randomly between 500 and 1,500 ms.
Each CS– outcome pairing was presented 10 times across 10 blocks in randomized order (one presentation per block). Following training, a contingency knowledge test assessed explicit knowledge of CS– outcome associations. Each CS was presented once, along with the four possible outcomes displayed below. Participants selected the associated outcome using randomly assigned keys (“a,” “d,” “g,” “j”) and received immediate feedback.
If any responses were incorrect, Pavlovian training was repeated with half the number of trials (50 trials), followed by a second contingency test. We planned to exclude participants who would fail the second test; however, no participants failed the contingency tests in the present experiment.
Instrumental training
Participants were instructed that they could now earn money by pressing keys. After an unspecified number of presses, earnings in a specific token currency would be displayed. They were informed that they could switch freely between keys and that the computer might instruct them to discontinue responding on a particular key.
Three concurrent fixed-ratio 9 (FR9) schedules were implemented. Each response key produced earnings in one specific currency. After nine presses, a reward symbol (+ 1B$, +1 N$, or +1T$) was presented for 2,000 ms. Participants were required to press the spacebar to “bank” the reward; failure to do so resulted in an error message and forfeiture of the reward. Once 20 dollars had been earned in a currency, participants were instructed to stop responding on that key.
After training, participants completed a contingency test by pressing the key associated with each displayed currency (presented in randomized order). Incorrect assignments led to repetition of instrumental training with half the number of trials.
Transfer test
Participants were informed that they could continue earning dollars, but outcomes would no longer be displayed. Earnings were possible only during “opening periods” of the stock market, indicated by a green dollar sign at the center of the screen (8,000 ms). A red dollar sign signaled a closed period (2,000 ms ITI). Responses were registered only during green periods. This limitation was introduced to minimize motor exhaustion (cf. 
22
, Experiment 2).
During each opening period, a CS was presented for 4,000 ms, superimposed on the green dollar sign. In half of the trials, the CS appeared during the first half of the opening period; in the remaining trials, during the second half. Participants could respond freely throughout, and task instructions explicitly stated that the CS presentations (company trades) would not affect their earnings and could be ignored: “Occasionally, companies also trade on the stock exchange (i.e., a company logo appears on the screen). This trading does not affect your returns” (translated from German).
The transfer test comprised six blocks. In each block, all eight CSs were presented twice (once per market opening period), yielding 96 trials in total. At the end of the test, cumulative earnings in each currency were displayed.
Post-experimental questions
After the transfer test, participants completed a paper-and-pencil questionnaire consisting of three items (translated from German):
(1) “Which hypotheses/research questions do you believe were investigated by the experiment?” Participants could write their open-ended answer in a text field provided below.
(2) “Did you pursue a specific strategy for pressing the buttons? If yes, which ones?” Participants could write their open-ended answer in a text field provided below.
(3) “How was your response to seeing a company logo during the opening hours of the stock exchange?”, providing the following response options:
(A) “I ignored the symbols and/or made attempts, to let them not influence me” (ignore strategy),
(B) “I deliberately selected the button that traded in the same currency as the company (symbol)” (assimilation strategy),
(C) “I deliberately selected a button that traded with a different currency as the company (symbol)” (contrast strategy),
(D) “None of these.”

Discussion
Experiment 1 yielded a robust outcome-selective PIT effect, the magnitude of which was moderated by participants’ self-reported use of a response strategy. The majority of participants indicated that they pursued an assimilation strategy, intentionally selecting responses associated with the same outcome as signaled by the cue. Accordingly, the outcome-selective PIT effect was substantially larger in this group. The widespread use of a response strategy additionally suggests that many participants processed the CS strategically, indicating that explicitly instructing participants to disregard the cues is often ineffective.
Results also suggested a small PIT effect in the ignore-strategy subgroup. This finding would be remarkable because it appears to contradict participants’ self-reported strategy to ignore the CSs. However, the effect should be interpreted with great caution, as the ignore-strategy subgroup was small, the statistical evidence was weak, and response strategies were assessed only retrospectively via self-report. Indeed, the discrepancy between participants’ reported intention to ignore the cues and the observed influence of the cues on response choice in this group demonstrates that such retrospective reports may not always provide reliable indices of strategy use. Experiment 2 therefore employed a more direct manipulation — a directed forgetting procedure — to discourage participants from strategically using Pavlovian cues to guide response selection.
Experiment 2
In this experiment, a computer crash was simulated after the Pavlovian learning phase. The experimenter (a research assistant) subsequently informed the participant that an incorrect version of the program had been inadvertently started and instructed them to forget everything they had learned up to that point.
In line with previous research
24
, we reasoned that this explicit forgetting instruction, embedded within a computer-crash scenario, would be sufficiently credible to lead participants to treat the previously learned Pavlovian relations as no longer informative and thus to forget them. Hence, we expected a reduced, if not eliminated, outcome-selective PIT effect in the directed-forgetting condition compared to a standard condition without a simulated computer crash.
Method
Transparency and openness
The data were collected in 2015. The study was not preregistered. A sensitivity power analysis conducted with G*Power 3.1 indicated that the sample of 52 participants provided 80% power to detect PIT effects of 
\(\:{{\upeta\:}}_{p}^{2}\)
 ≥ 0.248 in a 4 × 3 repeated-measures ANOVA and a within-between interaction effect in a 4 × 3 × 2 mixed ANOVA, assuming α = 0.05 and a correlation of 
r
 = .50 among repeated measures.
Sample
In total, 57 volunteers were recruited via the University of Würzburg’s mailing list. Three participants failed the second Pavlovian contingency test, and two experienced technical problems that resulted in data loss. The final sample comprised 52 participants (35 women and 16 men, one missing value; 46 right-handed, 6 left-handed; age: 
M
 = 27.8 years, range = 19‒56, with one missing value). Of these, 
n
 = 25 participants were randomly assigned to the computer-crash and 
n
 = 27 to the no-crash conditions. Participation was remunerated with a performance-contingent payment averaging €11.0 (
SD
 = 4.7).
Procedure
Apparatus, stimuli, and task procedures were identical to those used in Experiment 1 except that, in the computer-crash condition, an error message appeared at the end of the Pavlovian learning phase, informing participants that a critical computer error had occurred (see Fig. 
3
).

The experimenter then hurried to the participant and provided the following explanation (translated from German):

“Oh no, I have accidently started the wrong program! I will start the correct program immediately. Please forget everything you have done so far, and we will start over. Don’t worry— the time you have already spent will be compensated!”

Thus, participants assigned to the computer-crash condition were explicitly instructed to disregard the cue-outcome relations learned during the Pavlovian phase. The experimenter then started the allegedly correct program, which proceeded with the instrumental training and transfer test, identical to those administered in Experiment 1. In the no-crash condition, the Pavlovian training phase was completed without interruption, after which the experimenter started a new program containing the instrumental training and transfer test, paralleling the procedure in the computer-crash condition.
Data were collected in groups of up to three participants. To maintain the plausibility of the deception, only one participant per group was assigned to the computer-crash condition. In the post-experimental questionnaire, the use of a specific strategy was assessed with the multiple-choice item (Item 3). However, the open-ended question asking for response strategies was replaced with an item asking participants to rate how disturbing they found the interruption caused by the program restart (0 = not at all disturbing, 4 = very strongly disturbing).

Discussion
As expected, the directed forgetting manipulation significantly reduced reactivity to the cues during the transfer test: the outcome-selective PIT effect was substantially smaller when participants were explicitly instructed to disregard the learned cue–outcome relations, ostensibly because a computer crash had rendered them irrelevant for the upcoming tasks. Furthermore, more participants in the crash condition indicated in the post-experimental questionnaire that they attempted to ignore the cues. This increase relative to the no-crash group may indicate that the experimenter’s directed-forgetting instruction effectively persuaded participants to do so, which could explain the reduced cueing effect in this condition. Alternatively, participants in this condition may simply have reported what they thought the experimenter wanted to hear. In Experiment 3, we therefore included more specific expectancy ratings, asking participants whether they expected one response to be more likely rewarded than the other in the presence of a cue. This procedure was adapted from earlier work reporting a positive relationship between the magnitude of outcome-selective PIT and self-reported expectancies collected after the PIT test
12
,
13
,
19
. Although such evidence is correlational, a positive relationship would further corroborate the view that the PIT effect is influenced by functional interpretations of the cues. In addition, we introduced a working-memory load manipulation to constrain deliberate use of the cues further.
Experiment 3
Experiment 3 examined whether imposing an additional working-memory load during the transfer test would affect cue reactivity in the PIT task by limiting the cognitive resources available for deliberate cue processing. Working-memory load was manipulated using a digit-sequence memorization task adapted from Seabrooke and colleagues
19
.
Participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups:
(1) a computer-crash condition with working-memory load (crash/load);
(2) a computer-crash condition without working-memory load (crash/no load); and
(3) a no computer-crash condition with working-memory load (no-crash/load).
The effect of the directed-forgetting manipulation was assessed by comparing the crash/load condition with the no-crash/load condition. The influence of working-memory load on PIT performance was evaluated by comparing the crash/load condition with the crash/no-load condition. A no-crash/no-load condition was omitted to maintain a feasible sample size, as it was not required for the planned comparisons.
Based on the results of Experiment 2, we expected a reduction in the PIT effect in the crash/load condition relative to the no-crash/load condition. However, we had no directional hypothesis regarding the working memory load manipulation. Given that Seabrooke et al.,
19
 found that working memory load reduced PIT only under reversal instructions but not standard instructions, the direction of the load effect in the present directed forgetting paradigm remained uncertain. In addition to these between-group comparisons, we were specifically interested in whether a selective PIT effect would emerge in the crash/load condition, which arguably constituted suboptimal conditions for inferring differences in the response efficacy based on the cues.
To examine whether these manipulations also affected cognitive expectancies which responses would be rewarded, participants provided expectancy ratings for each CS (company symbol) after the transfer phase. By including this measure, we aimed to determine whether changes in transfer-test performance were accompanied by corresponding changes in beliefs about response efficacy.
Method
Transparency and openness

In total, 116 participants were recruited via the University’s mailing list. In accordance with the preregistered exclusion criteria, five participants were excluded for failing the Pavlovian contingency test and one participant for failing the instrumental contingency test. Data from an additional nine participants were excluded because they provided more than 50% incorrect responses in the digit-sequence memorization task.

Procedure
Apparatus, stimuli, and task procedures were identical to those used in Experiment 2. In addition, a digit-sequence memorization task, adapted from Seabrooke et al.
19
, was implemented in the crash/load and no-crash/load conditions.
At the beginning of each transfer-test trial, participants heard a sequence of six randomly selected digits (1–9), with no repetitions within a sequence. The auditory stimuli were presented via headphones and consisted of six digits spoken by a female native speaker of American English. The interstimulus interval ranged from 200 to 330 ms, depending on the duration of each audio file (approximately 1,000 ms per digit). Participants were instructed to memorize both the digits and their serial order. They were further informed that “good performance” on this task would be rewarded with a bonus payment of €2. The bonus was granted for achieving at least 50% correct responses, although this threshold was not disclosed to participants.
After presentation of the digit sequence, participants proceeded with instrumental responding during the display of a green dollar symbol, which was temporarily superimposed by a CS, as in Experiment 2. The dollar symbol then changed to red for 2,000 ms, signaling a temporary pause in responding.
Subsequently, participants were presented with one digit from the previously heard sequence (excluding the sixth digit) and were asked to indicate the digit that had followed it by pressing the corresponding number key on the keyboard. If participants made an incorrect response, immediate error feedback was provided (“Incorrect. You chose the wrong number.”). Correct responses were followed by positive feedback (“Correct. You chose the right number.”). Feedback was displayed for 1,000 ms, after which the next trial began with a 500-ms blank screen.
We incorporated four practice trials of the digit-sequence memorization task at the beginning of the experiment. To parallel the procedure, participants in the crash/no-load condition also completed these practice trials but did not perform the task during the transfer test. In this condition, the display of the red dollar symbol in each trial was extended to 8,000 ms to approximate the trial duration in the other conditions. To reduce the overall duration of the experiment, the number of transfer test trials was reduced to 48 trials in all conditions.
After the transfer test, participants provided expectancy ratings for each CS (company symbol) following the procedure described by Seabrooke et al.
19
. Specifically, participants rated their expectation of receiving a reward after pressing each key in the presence of a specific CS on a scale ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 7 (“very high reward”). The question was phrased as follows: “If the company symbol [star, triangle, square, circle] shown below was presented, what was your expectation of receiving a reward by pressing key [1, 2, 3]?”
Following the expectancy ratings, Pavlovian knowledge was re-evaluated using the same Pavlovian contingency test administered after the Pavlovian learning phase. A second (post-training) rating of the token currencies was omitted to reduce the overall duration of the experiment. Finally, participants completed the post-experimental questionnaire, as in Experiment 2.

Discussion
Experiment 3 demonstrated outcome-selective PIT effects in all three conditions, including the suboptimal condition combining the directed-forgetting instruction with working-memory load (the crash/load group). Furthermore, cue reactivity in the crash/load condition was significantly reduced compared to the crash/no-load condition, suggesting that the digit sequence memorization task taxed cognitive resources involved in cue utilization.
However, the three-way interaction between CS, Instrumental Response, and Crash-Manipulation did not reach significance when comparing the crash/load and no-crash/load groups, indicating that the directed forgetting manipulation did not produce a statistically reliable additional reduction in cue reactivity beyond the attenuation already observed in the no-crash/load condition. One possible explanation is that working-memory load already substantially reduced PIT in the no-crash/load condition, leaving insufficient variance to detect an additional effect of the directed forgetting manipulation. This interpretation is tentatively supported by a cross-experimental comparison showing a significantly smaller PIT effect in the no-crash/load group of Experiment 3 relative to the no-crash/no-load group of Experiment 2 (
F
(1.61, 93.16) = 11.01, 
p
 < .001, 
\(\:{{\upeta\:}}_{p}^{2}\)
 = 0.160.). Hence, the additional reduction of the PIT effect by the simulated computer crash may have been too small to be detected with the present sample sizes.
Alternatively, working-memory load may have interfered with participants’ ability to implement the directed-forgetting instruction itself, as maintaining and applying a “forget” rule may require cognitive resources
26
. If so, the crash and load manipulations may not have operated independently, consistent with Seabrooke et al.
19
, who reported an interaction between instruction and cognitive load. Future studies should include additional control conditions to disentangle these possibilities.
Exploratory correlational analyses also revealed a positive correlation between the magnitude of transfer during the PIT test and self-reported expectancies of cued outcomes after the test. The positive relationship mirrors findings in previous studies using similar expectancy measures
12
,
19
. Notably, outcome expectancies and PIT effects were also correlated in the crash/load and crash/no-load conditions. These correlations admit two readings: Cue-consistent responding may have been motivated by the expectation of a higher reward, or participants may have inferred that expectation from their own responding
27
. The correlational design cannot separate the two.
General discussion
The present experiments investigated the influence of cue-triggered reward expectancies on response selection using an outcome-selective PIT paradigm with human participants. Previous research has used this paradigm to study maladaptive behaviors such as overeating
28
, drug seeking
29
, and gambling
30
, often emphasizing the automaticity of the underlying reward-seeking processes (for a critique of this view, see
31
). However, studies with humans have also shown that these processes are shaped by participants’ functional interpretation of the presented cues. This line of research suggests that higher-order cognitive factors, such as beliefs about which responses will be most rewarded in the presence of specific cues, modulate the expression of outcome-selective PIT effects in humans (for a review, see
6
).
Across three experiments, we examined the conditions under which reward expectancies signaled by Pavlovian cues operate using directed forgetting and working-memory load manipulations. Experiment 1 established a PIT task and showed a outcome-selective PIT effect, which was larger among participants reporting strategic cue use but remained present even among those who reported attempting to ignore the cues. Experiment 2 showed that a directed-forgetting manipulation significantly reduced selective PIT. In Experiment 3, adding working-memory load further attenuated the effect, indicating that cue-based expectancies partly depend on cognitive resources. Combining working-memory load with directed forgetting did not produce an additional reduction; however, an outcome-selective PIT effect persisted even under these combined conditions.
Several conclusions can be drawn from these findings. First, they corroborate prior research suggesting that cue-guided responding in outcome-selective PIT tasks is shaped by participants’ beliefs about reward availability as inferred from the presented cues. In the present study, the experimenter’s instruction to forget the Pavlovian relations following an ostensible software error was sufficient to reduce the outcome-selective PIT effect. This instruction effect is consistent with inferential accounts positing that outcome-selective PIT is mediated by beliefs about response–outcome efficacy
6
. Specifically, participants may have inferred from the error message that the CSs were no longer valid reward signals and could therefore be disregarded when selecting responses. This interpretation is further supported by corresponding changes in explicit reward expectancies observed in Experiment 3.
A resource-dependent, rule-based account could likewise explain the reduction of outcome-selective PIT under working-memory load. As discussed by Seabrooke et al.
19
 and others
32
, automaticity is not an all-or-none property but instead varies along a continuum. Accordingly, even the implementation of a relatively simple response-selection rule—such as, “When I see a company trading in a specific currency, I will also trade in that currency”—may consume cognitive resources. Imposing a concurrent working-memory load may therefore impair the application of such rules, thereby attenuating use of the cues for response choice. Notably, this account is compatible only with theoretical perspectives that do not assume strong automaticity (i.e., high efficiency) of simple inferences
33
.
However, a propositional account must also explain why participants continued to treat the Pavlovian cues as reward signals despite explicit information that the cues were no longer informative due to a computer error. If a selection rule such as “When I see a company trading in a specific currency, I will also trade in that currency” is based on deductive reasoning, and if the truth value of the underlying propositions is relevant to such reasoning, then it would be illogical to infer from the proposition “The cue is no longer related to rewards due to a software error” that the response sharing the outcome with the cue would be more effective. Thus, either the truth value of propositions is not critical for propositional reasoning (a position most propositional theories would reject), or participants judged the new information to be false. The latter interpretation would imply that participants did not believe the simulated computer crash.
It is difficult to assess post hoc whether participants believed the simulated computer crash. We did not assess this directly, because merely asking about its credibility may itself induce skepticism
34
. However, the self-reported response strategies provide some indirect evidence. Participants exposed to the ostensible computer error indicated more frequent use of the ignore-strategy, suggesting that they believed the experimenter’s explanation that the Pavlovian relations should be forgotten. Nevertheless, several participants in the crash-groups still indicated strategic use of the cues for response choice, which may account for the residual PIT effects in these conditions. That said, this account presupposes that participants’ self-report of strategies they employed during the PIT test were accurate; an assumption that is questionable, given that participants who reported ignoring the cues during the transfer test nevertheless exhibited an outcome-selective PIT effect (see Experiment 1).
Furthermore, even if some participants doubted the veracity of the simulated computer crash, the associated directed-forgetting instruction was crystal-clear. The experimenter instructed participants unambiguously (i) to disregard what they had learned during Pavlovian training, because this knowledge would not be needed for subsequent tasks, and (ii) to ignore the cues presented during the test, because these were irrelevant to the rewards that could be earned by pressing keys. Both statements were true; neither involved deception. Furthermore, participants received neither additional reward nor any recognition for selecting cue-consistent responses. Why, then, did participants in these conditions nevertheless base their responses on the cues? Accounts that appeal exclusively to experimenter demand face a dilemma here: Either these participants set the forgetting instructions aside because they did not care about the experimenter’s instructions or mistrusted them, which would imply that they were not receptive to the experimenter’s demands; or they deliberately worked against the experimenter by using the cues contrary to the explicit request. Neither explanation is parsimonious, and both require additional post hoc assumptions (e.g., mistrust or reactance) to account for the results.
An alternative account of the residual effect in the directed-forgetting conditions would be that participants were willing to comply with the forgetting instruction but failed to do so during the transfer test — either deliberately, because they believed that using the cues would still be useful, or unintentionally, because the cue–outcome knowledge was automatically reactivated. If this knowledge was used strategically, for example, to simplify the free-choice decision task, one would additionally need to explain why participants regarded responses with matching outcomes as more useful than responses with mismatching outcomes. If it was applied automatically, the processes involved would need to be specified. Candidates are associations that prime outcome expectancies through an S–O–R chain
8
,
10
 and the uncontrolled activation of belief structures underlying reward expectancies
6
. The latter process is supported by research showing that beliefs formed after extensive learning of contingencies between responses and outcomes are not erased but superseded by new, updated information. Such old beliefs may therefore be reactivated even when they are no longer accurate, and reactivation of this kind is more likely under suboptimal operating conditions involving time pressure and low motivation
18
,
35
,
36
. Participants in the crash/load conditions may thus have selected the responses matching the cues on the mistaken expectation that these responses would yield better outcomes. This account is also supported by the post-experimental expectancy ratings and by their correlation with the magnitude of PIT.
To conclude, research on habits has emphasized the automaticity of individuals’ reactivity to cues, proposing that cues can trigger the pursuit of specific rewards without an accompanying conscious intention. The present findings suggest that cue-dependent behavior may be more controlled than this account claims. Verbal instructions from the experimenter to disregard the cue knowledge, because it had been acquired as a result of a computer error, were sufficient to reduce selective PIT effects. This finding indicates that the expression of PIT depends on the functional interpretation of the cues. Participants also expressed the belief that responses associated with the same outcome as the cue would be rewarded more often. Taken together, these findings imply that cue reactivity in these tasks is driven by reward expectations that can persist even when they are false and have been explicitly disavowed by the experimenter. Future research should examine why these expectations are so persistent.

🔥 1. The Take — The Autonomous Factory
The traditional factory as we know it is getting a brain,
 potentially unlock a $100 trillion market  with physical AI. 
Siemens and NVIDIA
 are turning Siemens’ Erlangen electronics plant into 
the blueprint for a fully AI-driven, adaptive manufacturing site
, starting this year. 
PepsiCo says AI-driven digital twins raised throughput at one US Gatorade plant by 20% within three months
. Software agents now plan schedules, reroute work around a failing station and adjust process settings while the line is running.
The productivity case is strong, but what it getting less attention is how autonomy changes the way factories fail.
A traditional automated plant mostly fails loudly, meaning a machine fails, the line stops and an alarm goes off. An autonomous plant is designed not to stop and for the most part that’s an advantage. But when the system itself is wrong, about a drifting sensor or a bad optimization, it keeps producing confidently and the line never stops.
That changes where the money is lost. The first exposure is recall, where a wrong decision can ship a defective batch across thousands of units before anyone notices, and recall costs usually dwarf the downtime.
The second is concentration. 
NVIDIA and Siemens
 already list Foxconn, HD Hyundai, KION and PepsiCo among the customers evaluating their industrial AI stack, so one bad update could hit many plants at once. 
The Jaguar Land Rover cyberattack
 showed that the stakes are high. That incident forced three UK plants to stop for about five weeks and roughly 5,000 suppliers were affected, all without a single robot being damaged.
Insurance covers only part of this. 
Non-damage business interruption
 cover exists, but it usually needs a named trigger, such as a cyberattack, a supplier failure or a utility outage. An AI planner that simply optimizes badly may not fit any of them, because nothing was breached, nothing happened outside the plant and nothing was damaged. Specialist products that cover AI performance errors exist, but few manufacturers carry them yet.
That’s not a case against autonomy. In fact, self-healing schedules and predictive maintenance will probably reduce ordinary downtime, and sensors will keep getting better at catching machines that break.
The harder problem is quality control. Everything from 
ISO 9001 to automotive PPAP
 assumes a fixed process, where any change is validated and signed off before it reaches production. That discipline is why most recalls can be traced to a specific lot, line and shift. An autonomous factory changes its own process continuously, at machine speed, and nobody signs off.
Closing that gap means applying change control to decisions made by software. Every adjustment gets logged, the limits the system can move within get validated, and traceability stays fine enough to scope a recall. Those are also the things an underwriter will ask for before covering the loss.

📡 2. Signal Watch — This Week in Risk
We’ve been saying it for quite some time now and last week Satya Nadella made the case to millions of people that companies should treat AI models like insider risks.
 It’s a strong piece and I’m glad he wrote it. 
I marked the three lines that matter most. 
The red line is the attribution problem.
 You can’t trace a model’s behavior back to its training data or weights the way you could trace software back to code, yet we’re handing these systems sensitive data and the authority to act. We wrote about this in The Trust Layer: the most valuable question in enterprise AI is who did this, and on whose authority.
The yellow line is the call for a new trust architecture.
 That’s the argument of our manifesto, Software Scaled. Risk Didn’t. Capability is scaling far faster than our ability to verify, contain and take responsibility for what these systems do, and that gap is where the next infrastructure category gets built.
The orange line is the one I’d frame: separate the intelligence from the authority over it.
 That’s what we meant in Identity Is the New Perimeter and Permissioning Machines. Every agent needs its own identity, scoped permissions and controls that live outside the model.
We didn’t just write about it, we actually invested in it, backing founders building this layer before most investors had a name for the category. And we built a network around it through our Executive Risk Dinners, where CISOs, insurers, regulators and founders have been debating these exact questions.
When the CEO of one of the largest AI providers says a model provider’s assurances aren’t enough, a contrarian thesis becomes a market. We’ve been building for this moment. If you’re building in this space, we’ve been waiting for you.

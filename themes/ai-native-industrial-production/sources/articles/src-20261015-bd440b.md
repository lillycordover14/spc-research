Two couples struggle with taboo loves in Silas House’s lush novel, 
The Tulip Poplars
 (Algonquin).
We don’t have enough love stories these days, and we certainly don’t have enough epic ones, so I created three in 
The Tulip Poplars
. Two of them are between couples whose love is legislated all their lives. Set from 1918–1976, the novel follows a gay couple, Tom and Amos, and an interracial couple, Maeve and Paschal, as they pine for each other, face many obstacles that keep them from being together, and have moments of great joy. For Tom and Amos, being gay is an act punishable by imprisonment and gets one of them sent to an infamous asylum for conversion therapy. Their only solace is being able to hide who they are, which is not possible for Maeve and Paschal. Jim Crow laws keep them from being allowed in the same spaces and forbid them from marrying. For both couples, their desire to be together is frowned upon by most everyone they know.
The novel is about more than bigotry, though. It’s also about faith, doubt, what it means to be truly good, and the way a place can take up residence in us. That last big theme is illuminated by the third love story in the novel, between the lead character, Tom, and the titular grove of trees that his family has been protecting from logging for generations. Tom goes to incredible lengths to save the trees and all the characters keep being drawn back to that patch of land. The tulip poplars are home.
In this book I wanted the place and the people to be fully alive, and I wanted the reader to feel as if they were time-traveling to rural Appalachia and the gritty streets of New York City for the 60-some years the novel covers. When they open the pages of the book I want them to see, hear, taste, feel, and smell everything in that time and place. I wanted them to feel the pining and to root for these characters to be together. I hope they’ll also luxuriate in the language. I was inspired and influenced by several novels that do all those things, and I can only hope to achieve a fraction of the power they have.

The Woodlanders
 
Thomas Hardy. Oxford Univ., $8.95 (416p) ISBN 978-019953853
 
It’s hard for me to choose a favorite of Hardy’s (I count this one in my top three along with 
Jude the Obscure
 and 
Tess of the D’Urbervilles
) but 
The Woodlanders
 has the most in common thematically as it explores a group of people fundamentally connected to their work and land. Plus, these characters pine for each other in ways only Hardy’s creations can. You’ll never forget Marty and her longing for Giles Winterbourne.

The Color Purple
 
Alice Walker. Penguin, $18 trade paper (304p) ISBN 978-0-14-313569-2
 
The Color Purple
 has had an influence on everything in my life since it first blew my mind wide open as a teenager. This modern classic looks at race, violence, sexuality, gender, belief, and everything in between without ever feeling like it is doing too much. It’s also an epic that covers most of the main character’s entire life and in doing so makes Celie one of the most powerful and memorable characters in literature. I love the way it presents its time and place and shows the way that both things shape our lives.

Doctor Zhivago
 
Boris Pasternak, trans. from the Russian by Richard Pevear and Larissa Volokhonsky. Vintage, $20 trade paper (704p) ISBN 978-0-307-39095-0
 
If you want to study epic love stories or historical fiction then this one ought to be high on your list. This novel was also instructive for me on how to make sure that the human story takes precedence over any kind of social agenda. Like Yuri Zhivago, my characters are just trying to live their lives but their happiness keeps being interrupted by the actions of those in power. Their existences have been politicized and they only want to love one another.  I love the way Pasternak masterfully balances intimate scenes between people against the huge canvas of history playing out in the background.

Alec
 
William Di Canzio. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, $27  (336p) ISBN 978-0-374-10260-9
 
Alec
 is a companion to E.M. Forster’s 
Maurice
 (1971), which wasn’t published until one year after Forster’s death due to its controversial nature. 
Maurice
 gives us the story of an upper-class British man who only begins to accept his sexuality after falling in love with Alec, a gamekeeper. Forster’s novel is beautiful, of course, but hemmed in by the lead character’s own deep-seated classism and the author’s inability to fully express himself. In 
Alec
, the working-class title character not only gets to tell the story from his point of view, but he expands upon it (with the blessing of the Forster estate) in a decidedly modern way that nonetheless works perfectly in the original time period. This is an underrated novel of the gay experience that should be more widely read.

The Welsh Girl
 
Peter Ho Davies, Author
 .  Houghton Mifflin $24 (338p) ISBN 978-0-618-00700-4
 
This novel was widely read and longlisted for the Booker when it was first published in 2007, and rightly so. Its use of multiple points of view was valuable for me as I attempted to use this same device effectively. Another exercise in longing, 
The Welsh Girl
 focuses on Esther, a teenaged girl from North Wales who is carrying on a secret affair with a young German prisoner of war. I’ll never forget any of these characters or their corner of the world.

The Overstory
 
Richard Powers. Norton, $27.95  (512p) ISBN 978-0-393-63552-2
 
This Pulitzer Prize-winning novel is a modern masterpiece of environmental fiction. 
The Overstory
 operates on a huge canvas of time and geography. In essence it is a book about nine people whose lives are connected through trees. Among them are an artist, a researcher, an activist, a solider, and others. It’s a feat of structure and theme, always bringing us back to the central theme: the interconnectedness of all life, and how we must protect it all if we hope to last.

Alias Grace
 
Margaret Atwood, Author
  Nan A. Talese $24.95 (0p) ISBN 978-0-385-47571-6
 
Few books have transported me to a historical period as effortlessly as this 1996 beauty that fictionalizes the story of Grace Marks, a 19th-century homicidal maid known as “Canada’s Lizzie Borden.” Atwood conjures the place and time in practically every sentence without ever making the reader feel it is too heavy-handed. She flavors the entire book with details that are so vibrant the reader feels as if the rest of the world has fallen away while reading it.

The Prophets
 
Robert Jones, Jr. Putnam, $27 (400p) ISBN 978-0-593-08568-4
 
Not enough novelists these days care deeply about the art of the sentence but Jones certainly does. The language manages to be lovely even when describing the most horrific scenes, as he sometimes does in this harrowing account of two enslaved men who are in love with each other and risk their families and community to express it. Like other books on this list, 
The Prophets
 is exquisite at balancing intimate scenes with the hugeness of history.

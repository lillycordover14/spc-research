Local advocates rally against sale of Boston Dynamics’ robot dogs to ICE

By 
Samantha Mandala

 ● 

Community
 

 ● 

				October 17, 2026			

Protesters outside of Boston Dynamics’ offices on Oct. 15. Photo by Joseph Sharkey.

Local activist groups are protesting the sale of Boston Dynamics’ robot dogs to US Immigration and Customs Enforcement, demanding, among other things, that the Waltham-based company pull the software license that would allow ICE to operate the machines. 

Don’t Mourn Organize and De-ICE Action Network are spearheading two protests outside Boston Dynamics headquarters this month. One was held Oct. 15, with another planned for Oct. 20.  

“It’s just shocking that they would do something so likely to result in human rights violations,” said Betsy Leondar-Wright, a member of both Don’t Mourn Organize and De-ICE Action Network.“This just seems to go against everything that Boston Dynamics says that it stands for,” she said.

In addition to revoking the software license, Don’t Mourn Organize also wants the commonwealth of Massachusetts to rescind a $25 million tax credit awarded to Boston Dynamics in June, according to a 
petition
 with signatures from more than a dozen local organizations. The tax credit is part of a $100 million expansion of the Boston Dynamics manufacturing facility in Waltham, according to the company website. 

“We cannot claim to protect our residents while at the same time we provide a subsidy to create weapons used against them,” the petition reads.

These local activist groups are targeting any company doing business with ICE, but felt a special responsibility to protest Boston Dynamics because it was so close to home. 

Elizabeth Hoffecker, founder of activist group Indivisible Waltham, said IW’s goal in joining these protests is preventing businesses like Boston Dynamics from collaborating with or selling to ICE, and making it “as economically painful” as possible for them to continue doing so. 

“A lot of corporations have been willing to sell out on community values to make a buck at participating in the infrastructure of dismantling democracy,” Hoffecker said. 

A contentious contract 

The contract for the robot dogs is between ICE and Connecticut company RADeCO, which modified Boston Dynamics’ original machines, called SPOT dogs, to include a robotic arm, thermal camera and sensors for explosive materials. ICE spent about $1.3 million on four such modified versions, called Cerberus, according to a 
federal contract
 document. 

Although Boston Dynamics is not named as a contractor in the recent sale, the company has long partnered with RADeCO, especially for “government-related applications,” according to a Boston Dynamics spokesperson. They added, however, that Boston Dynamics’ ethics code applies to all sales of their products like SPOT, even when officially sold by RADeCO.
Advertisement

The company’s robots are “legally prohibited from being weaponized or used for purposes of harm or intimidation,” the spokesperson said. Boston Dynamics also published an 
open letter
 against the weaponization of general use robots like SPOT. 

Boston Dynamics has 
previously said
 that if ICE misused the robots, it would void the warranty, terminate the license for the SPOT software, and take legal action. The company has also said that it has not recorded any instances of misuse in the time that it has contracted with law enforcement agencies. 

RADeCO declined to comment. 

A different call to arms

So far, Cerberus has been used for tasks like “hazardous gas detection, unexploded ordnance inspection, suspicious package investigation, search and rescue, subterranean or confined space exploration and structural assessments following fires, disaster events and other hazards,” according to the Boston Dynamics representative. 

“Even if they’re not like Boston Dynamics is saying, they’re not going to physically attack humans, they can aid and abet the harm that ICE does. It just seems really, really outrageous,” Leondar-Wright said.

Even if Boston Dynamics didn’t directly sell the technology, “It’s the duty of the researcher to promote the well-being and safety of the individual in the community,” said Thomas Vaughn, a Northeastern engineering ethics professor, adding, “And non-malfeasance is an obligation on the part of the researchers to avoid causing harm by their discoveries.” Selling robots to ICE goes against that ethical principle, he said.  

It’s unclear how ICE plans to implement the robots in their operations, but the agency is known to rely on technology, from facial recognition to spyware, for its surveillance and enforcement efforts. Congressional funding in 2026 also allowed ICE to purchase a device meant to stop and capture a moving car, and battery powered electric shock gloves for agents to wear during arrests.

“I think it is a trend of dehumanizing conflict, kind of turning conflict into some kind of video game that I think makes it even easier to take life without thinking about it,” Hoffecker said.

Building momentum

This month’s protests are not the first time the local groups have mobilized. Don’t Mourn Organize and De-Ice Action Network held their first protest back in September at the Museum of Science, where a SPOT robot was on display. Protesters dressed up in dog outfits and held signs decrying the original sale. 

De-Ice Action Network also has previous experience with protests — and with success. Its campaign against Citizens Bank saw dozens of rallies around the country and the bank ending its financial support of private prisons used by ICE to detain immigrants. 

Hoffecker, who was part of the Citizens Bank Coalition, said IW and other local activists aim to bring awareness to such issues that people may not be aware of. 

“Ultimately, if we’re going to shift course, it’s going to come down to just regular people in large enough numbers changing who gets elected and also changing … the dynamics of all these businesses that are propping up the current system,” she said.

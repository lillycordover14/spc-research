Hermeus Pairs Mach 3 Quarterhorse Drone With Anduril Autonomy for 2027 Flight&nbsp;&nbsp;The SOFX Report

[page text]
Hermeus has selected Anduril Industries to run the autonomy on its Quarterhorse Mk 2 high-speed drone, with the two firms targeting the first autonomous flight of the aircraft in 2027.

Anduril 
announced the agreement
 September 3. Under the deal, Anduril will integrate its Lattice for Mission Autonomy software, the same stack the company is developing for the Air Force’s Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA) 
program
, onto the Quarterhorse, an uncrewed jet roughly the size of an F-16 that Hermeus is building to reach speeds as fast as Mach 3.

Autonomy has reached high-speed flight.

The arrangement is a first for Anduril. The company said it is the first commercial deal to place its mission autonomy software on a third-party Group 5 aircraft, the Pentagon’s category for large drones over 1,320 pounds.

Until now, Lattice for Mission Autonomy had flown only on Anduril’s own YFQ-44A Fury, a CCA prototype. An Anduril spokesperson said the firm intends to become Quarterhorse’s long-term autonomy partner rather than supply a one-off integration.

For Hermeus, the deal marks a shift from remote piloting to automated flight control. The Quarterhorse aircraft flown to date have been operated by humans from a ground station. “We now need to automate a lot of those flight controls,” Hermeus CEO Zach Shore said in an interview with 
Breaking Defense
. “I want to be able to push a button, have the aircraft spin up, have the aircraft auto takeoff, all those basic features that allow one person to manage multiple platforms.”

Shore knows the vendor well. Before joining Hermeus in 2022, he was an early Anduril employee who built the company’s command-and-control business line, a detail that gives the CEO firsthand knowledge of the software his aircraft will now carry.

The first flight will not hand the aircraft over all at once. Anduril said Quarterhorse will take off under human control, switch mission autonomy on to fly a defined route, then land under human control, with later tests expanding what the software manages. Operators will interact with the aircraft through Anduril’s 
Menace-T
 command-and-control hardware across planning, execution, and post-flight debrief.

Autonomy at high Mach demands faster-than-human reaction. The Quarterhorse flies at close to a mile per second, and the Autonomy Government Reference Architecture (A-GRA) that guides the software requires the aircraft to fly itself through every flight regime, including the jump to supersonic. “We will spend the rest of this year on a build-up from software in the loop to integration testing to hardware in the loop and validation testing, then we’ll go toward flight,” said Brett Darcey, Anduril’s vice president for mission autonomy.

The pairing rests on a design principle both firms are testing in public, that the airframe and the autonomy software can be developed separately and matched later. A-GRA is meant to let the same software move across compliant aircraft rather than being rebuilt for each one. Anduril has said it plans to integrate Lattice onto all A-GRA-compliant platforms.

Hermeus, founded in 2018 and backed by more than $500 million in private capital, flew its first Quarterhorse in May 2025 and reached Mach 1.21 with the Mk 2.1 aircraft on May 26, 2026, its first supersonic flight.

The program is funded in part by the Defense Innovation Unit, which has raised the company’s contract ceiling to $219 million for high-speed flight testing and payload-release work. The next aircraft, Mk 2.2, was built in under a year, with the steel-framed Mk 2.3 slated to target Mach 3.

The combined system has not yet flown. The first autonomous Quarterhorse mission remains planned for 2027.

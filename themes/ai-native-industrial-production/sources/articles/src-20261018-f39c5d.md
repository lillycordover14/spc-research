Christian McBride's 'Art Blakey era' begins well with Ursa Major and 'Points of Light'

Blakey's legacy refreshed and broadened in obvious - and also deeper - ways  

by 
Sebastian Scotney
Monday, 19 October 2026

share this article

Share

 

 

Christian McBride (foreground) with, left to right, Ely Perlman, Nicole Glover, Savannah Harris, Mike King

Evelyn Freja/ Mack Avenue

Nobody in the world is better suited to this new 
venture 
than Christian McBride. The label's "album blurb" (sic) for 
Points of Light
 contains the fascinating idea behind forming the band Ursa Major: that the bass titan has now stepped into his “Art Blakey era.”
The blurb-writer is referencing McBride’s fellow Pennsylvanian here, the drummer Art Blakey, and the “era” in his life being referred to is one which lasted from around 1954 – when he announced: "I'm gonna stay with the youngsters. When these get too old I'll get some younger ones. Keeps the mind active…"  – through several iterations of his band the Jazz Messengers... right the way through to his death from cancer in 1990.

The idea of a band with top-flight younger musicians is by no means a new departure for McBride, who has previous form here. He formed Tip City Trio (2017-continues) with pianist Emmet Cohen and guitarist Dan Wilson, a unit modelled after Ray Brown’s trio with Monty Alexander and Herb Ellis or Russell Malone, and its name is a homage to two great pianists (both of whom, incidentally, had their stint as Jazz Messengers), James Williams and Mulgrew Miller, and the way they liked to refer to hard swing.
The new band Ursa Major is so-called because of the idea that the four young players are all genuine stars. And the more one listens, the more that becomes apparent. Each one of the four musicians here is worthy of getting to know individually. Remember these names: Nicole Glover, Savannah Harris, Mike King, Ely Perlman. 
Three and a half decades on from the death of Art Blakey, it is clear that a few things in Blakey’s legacy were going to need a bit of re-invigorating. On the most superficial and obvious level, the more-or-less complete absence of either either guitarists (!) or women (!!!) in the Jazz Messengers wasn’t going to last in the 2020s. (Jazz completists will no doubt want to single out exceptions to the rule such as guitarist Bobby Broom, and pianist Joanne Brackeen.)
That said, the ways in which the Blakey mission has been updated by Christian McBride also go much deeper. The presence of saxophonist Nicole Glover in particular is symptomatic of the renewed and updated focus. She has written the opening track, “Sakura”, and it is the most un-Messengers-ish piece imaginable. Ethereal, floaty, pulse-less, atmospheric, reminiscent of Oregon. She also stars on “Same Folks” playing a remarkable solo which mainly stays in subtone, and is in obvious contrast to her assertive, extrovert, more conventionally “killing” playing on McBride’s composition “Familiar Steps”.
Modern virtuosity such as Glover’s is about stylistic versatility and these are examples of how freely she can and will roam. Perhaps that gives the key to what Ursa Major is all about. These are musicians of an age where the possibilities seem limitless. They might get set in their ways one day, who knows... but in the meantime McBride – and producer Terrace Martin – have given them the capacity to wander at liberty down several stylistic avenues.    
The musician who already has the glitziest CV imaginable is drummer Savannah Harris. Born in Oakland, she has performed in Cécile McLorin Salvant’s touring band and Kenny Barron’s trio and even formed her own European group ØKSE with Mette Rasmussen, Val Jeanty and Petter Eldh. Her presence, versatility, awareness and tonal variety on this album are consistently jaw-dropping. This is already an exciting career, and it has only just begun.
Another astonishingly many-faceted player – one can completely understand McBride’s choice – is the piano player Mike King, originally from Chicago. He switches to organ and takes a ferociously memorable solo on “Cow”. He is also the composer of perhaps the most hauntingly memorable track on the album, “Orquieda”.
The band’s guitarist Ely Perlman, originally from Tel Aviv, is another player whose stylistic breadth comes across strongly. He has clearly absorbed everything from Wes Montgomery to Metheny and Rosenwinkel’s Heartcore, and his technical ease goes hand-in-hand with a real sympathy for melody.
McBride has openly admitted that when he set out in his career he would have loved to follow in the footsteps of many great musicians and to join the Jazz Messengers, but that the death of Art Blakey, just one year after he graduated from Philadelphia High School for the Creative and Performing Arts thwarted that ambition...even if it did open up a host of others in what has been and continues to be a hugely successful and influential place in music. 
McBride’s nascent “Art Blakey era”, then, is a reason for optimism. The musicians who currently are or who will be involved in Ursa Major deserve to flourish. We need that.

Key takeaways
- China’s one-year pause on its October 2025 rare-earth export controls runs until 10 November 2026 under Announcement No. 70, and no notice extending it appeared on the commerce ministry’s 2026 list as of 16 October.
- Washington said in late September that the US-China truce now runs to 10 January 2027, but Beijing has not published matching legal text.
- The pause never covered China’s April 2025 licensing regime for seven heavy rare earths, including dysprosium and terbium, which still requires a licence for every export.
- China’s rare-earth exports fell 9.6% to 43,711 tonnes in January to September 2026, even as September alone rose 6.7% year on year.
- The IEA estimates full enforcement could put $6.5 trillion a year of downstream production outside China at risk, while planned magnet output elsewhere covers only about a third of expected needs by 2035.
One date in Washington, another in Beijing
On 7 November 2025, China’s Ministry of Commerce and the General Administration of Customs issued Announcement No. 70, suspending six export-control notices from the previous month. The text is short and precise: the suspension applies immediately and runs until 10 November 2026.
That date now sits three weeks away. In late September, during Xi Jinping’s state visit to Washington, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the trade truce agreed in Busan in October 2025 would run for two more months, to 10 January 2027, as a trade-law analysis published on Mondaq sets out in detail. The rare-earth pause was China’s side of that bargain.
Beijing’s paperwork has not caught up. The commerce ministry’s list of 2026 announcements, checked on 16 October, runs to Announcement No. 48 without any notice that extends or replaces No. 70. The US side has not formalised its half either: the same analysis notes that the Commerce Department had not yet published a notice extending its own suspended measure, a rule extending export restrictions to affiliates of listed companies.
Until a new text appears, the only legal date companies can rely on is 10 November. A political promise may hold, as it did last year. But a supplier’s compliance team cannot ship magnets on the strength of a Treasury Secretary’s remarks.
What was paused, and what never was
The October 2025 measures were sweeping. They added five more rare earths to the control list (holmium, erbium, thulium, europium and ytterbium), covered rare-earth processing equipment and technologies, and brought lithium batteries, graphite anode materials and superhard materials under licensing. Most contentious was Announcement No. 61, which reached beyond China’s borders to foreign-made products containing Chinese-origin rare earths or made with Chinese rare-earth technology.
Those are the measures now on hold. The rules that disrupted car factories in 2025 are not. On 4 April 2025, Beijing’s Announcement No. 18 required export licences for seven medium and heavy rare earths: samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium, along with their oxides, alloys and compounds. China said the controls protect national security and meet non-proliferation obligations. That regime has stayed in force throughout the truce.
Dysprosium and terbium are the ones that matter most for industry. Small amounts of them keep neodymium magnets working at the high temperatures found in electric motors, so they sit inside EV drivetrains, wind turbines, industrial robots and missile guidance systems.
What the 2025 controls did
The April controls showed how quickly a licensing queue can stop a production line. By early June 2025, Europe’s car-parts makers said several production lines had already shut. The supplier association CLEPA said only about a quarter of the hundreds of licence applications its members had filed since April had been granted, according to Reuters, and it complained that procedures varied by province.
Beijing later eased the flow without changing the rules. In December 2025 it began issuing year-long general licences to large Chinese magnet makers, including JL Mag, Ningbo Yunsheng and Beijing Zhong Ke San Huan, covering exports to individual customers, Reuters reported. A commerce ministry spokesperson said at the time that China had approved compliant civilian export applications in a timely manner.
The IEA’s Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2026 puts the stakes in one number. If the expanded controls were fully implemented, it estimates, about $6.5 trillion a year of downstream production outside China would be at risk across the automotive, high-tech, defence and energy sectors.
Where shipments stand now
Trade data shows supply flowing, but unevenly. China exported 4,269.8 tonnes of rare earths in September 2026, up 6.7% on a year earlier, according to Reuters, citing customs figures released on 14 October. Over the first nine months of the year, exports were still down 9.6%, at 43,711 tonnes.
Magnets show a sharper split by destination. In January and February 2026, China shipped 4,775 tonnes of rare-earth magnets to the EU, up 28.4% year on year and 44.4% of its magnet exports, while shipments to the United States fell 22.5% to 994 tonnes, the South China Morning Post found in an analysis of Chinese customs data.
Europe has not escaped Beijing’s toolkit, though. The same commerce ministry list shows China adding seven EU entities to its export control list in April 2026 and 14 more in July. Prices tell the rest. The IEA says dysprosium and terbium currently cost around five times more in Europe than on China’s domestic market.
Europe’s answer is still under construction
The European Commission’s RESourceEU plan, presented in December 2025, earmarked about €3 billion for raw-materials projects over the following twelve months, as electrive reported at the time. It also proposed a European Critical Raw Materials Centre to handle joint purchasing and stockpiling, a stockpiling pilot, and restrictions on exports of permanent-magnet scrap so that it can be recycled in Europe.
The nearest physical example of that ambition is in the Baltics. Neo Performance Materials opened a €75 million magnet plant in Narva, Estonia, in September 2025, Automotive World reported, with an initial capacity of 2,000 tonnes a year, which the company says is enough for about one million electric vehicles. Expansion is planned after 2027.
Scale remains the problem. The IEA says existing and announced refining capacity outside the dominant supplier would cover about two-thirds of expected mined supply by 2035, but planned magnet production would cover only about one-third. Closing the magnet gap would need around $60 billion of investment over the next decade, it estimates. China mined about 69% of the world’s rare earths in 2025, according to GlobalData figures cited by Mining Technology, and its share of refining is higher still.
The case against panic
There are reasons to expect no cliff edge on 10 November. Washington has already announced a longer truce, and before the September summit, analysts at Commonwealth Bank of Australia expected the rare-earth question to be handled through another temporary extension. China also has its own interest in keeping customers: every licence delay pushes buyers towards suppliers in Malaysia, the United States and Estonia.
That shift is already measurable. The IEA says the leading refiner’s share of rare-earth refining fell from above 90% in 2023 to 85% in 2025 and could reach 70% by 2035 if planned projects go ahead. Rare earths were the one major mineral group where refining concentration fell last year.
Costs are another reason for calm. The IEA estimates rare earths account for about 40% of a permanent magnet’s cost but less than 1% of a car’s value; tripling their price would raise vehicle costs by roughly 0.1%. For carmakers, the risk is availability, not price.
The counter-argument is that availability is exactly what export licences control. A truce that depends on summit diplomacy can be withdrawn in the same way, and the April 2025 regime gives Beijing a lever that no truce has removed.

What buyers should do before 10 November
For European manufacturers, the practical question is narrower than the geopolitics. Magnets and alloys containing the seven April 2025 elements need a Chinese export licence whatever happens next month. Companies that rely on general licences held by their Chinese suppliers should check the validity dates, because those permits were issued for a year from late 2025.
Exposure to the suspended measures is harder to see. If Announcement No. 61 returns, it could reach products made outside China that contain Chinese-origin rare earths, so a component bought from a supplier in Asia or Europe may still carry a Chinese licensing requirement. Mapping where magnet material originates, two or three tiers down, is the work that protects production either way.
The likeliest outcome is another quiet extension, announced late. The cheapest preparation is to assume nothing until it is published.

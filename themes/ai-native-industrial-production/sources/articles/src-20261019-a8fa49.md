CHIPART Project Demonstrates Recycled Titanium Powder Reduces CO2 Emissions in AM Production


            The concluded CHIPART (Chips to Parts: Circularity of titanium scrap from machining to additive manufacturing) project, supported by the European Defence Agency, validates that titanium chips from aerospace suppliers can be converted into metal powder suitable for additive manufacturing without energy-intensive remelting.

The recently concluded CHIPART project is paving the way toward less environmentally damaging additive manufacturing (AM) production that can also yield economic benefits — achieved by recycling aerospace titanium chips into AM powder without energy-intensive remelting.

The aerospace and defense sectors are under pressure to reduce CO
2
 emissions across supply chains and secure strategic raw materials. That challenge was the focal point of the CHIPART project, supported by the European Defence Agency (EDA).

Following 12 months of work, the project — which included partners 
IDONIAL
 from Spain, 
Danish Technological Institute
 from Denmark and 
CRM Group
 from Belgium — has validated that titanium chips from Tier-2 aerospace suppliers can be converted into metal powder suitable for additive manufacturing, notably without the energy-intensive remelting process typically used in the manufacturing of titanium powder.

"By converting machining chips directly into additive manufacturing metal powder without the use of remelting, we have demonstrated that circularity in titanium manufacturing not only provides environmental benefits — it is also economically viable at scale," says Magdalena Müller, materials specialist at Danish Technological Institute.

A lifecycle assessment confirms that the project's recycling method can potentially reduce CO
2
 emissions by between 85% and 92% compared to using conventional virgin titanium powder — and, according to the project team, without compromising on quality.

The recycled material was tested on two different additive manufacturing technologies: laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and directed energy deposition (DED).

"During the process, we found that we achieved the best results by blending recycled powder with virgin titanium powder, and that controlling the oxygen level in the printer was the decisive factor for print quality," Müller explains.

Once optimal printer settings were identified, the project achieved a material density of 98% or higher in the printed parts, fulfilling the project's target.

Within the project, Danish Technological Institute developed an economic model around a demonstrator component. The model shows that although recycling at laboratory scale incurs higher costs, industrially scaled recycled powder can be produced significantly cheaper than virgin powder. When machine time and post-processing are factored into overall production, there is still a positive reduction in total manufacturing costs compared to the exclusive use of virgin powder.

With the conclusion of the CHIPART project, the results are now being presented to the European Defence Agency in Liège.

CHIPART ("Chips to Parts: Circularity of titanium scrap from machining to additive manufacturing") is a 12-month proof-of-concept project that ran from October 2025 to September 2026. The project falls under the Incubation Forum for Circular Economy in European Defence (IF CEED) and is supported by the EDA. The project was built upon a previous project, VARETIT, which also focused on recycling titanium scrap into additive manufacturing powder. The project also collaborates with external industrial partners in the defense and aerospace sectors, which provide a demonstrator component for reference as well as a source for the titanium chips used.

The CHIPART project is co-funded by the European Union's LIFE Programme and the European Defence Agency, within the framework of the Incubation Forum for the Circular Economy in European Defence (IF CEED).

Key Points
JPMorgan Chase has deployed $5 billion into startup investments in the first year of its U.S. security and resiliency initiative, halfway to its initial $10 billion goal.
The bank may exceed that target, co-President Doug Petno told CNBC.
JPMorgan has taken stakes in startups Hadrian, which builds automated factories for aerospace and defense firms, and Castelion, which is developing low-cost hypersonic missiles.
Separately, JPMorgan has financed and facilitated more than $230 billion in transactions in the initiative's first year, part of a broader goal of supporting $1.5 trillion in activity over a decade.

JPMorgan Chase
 has made $5 billion in startup investments in the first year of its U.S. security and resiliency 
initiative
, the company said Monday, a faster-than-expected pace that indicates the bank is finding plentiful opportunities in emerging defense, manufacturing and supply chain firms.
The bank, which 
last year said it would deploy $10 billion
 into startups over a decade, may expand the program beyond that initial dollar amount, JPMorgan co-President Doug Petno told CNBC in an interview. 
Todd Combs, the former 
Berkshire Hathaway
 executive who leads the 
JPMorgan group
 in charge of 
making startup investments
, has "hit the ground running," Petno said. "He's done a great job and put us in some really interesting companies," he added. 
Most of those investments have yet to be disclosed, but two known stakes seem emblematic of the project's mission to bolster companies that are needed for a more 
dangerous
 and chaotic era of geopolitics. JPMorgan, the biggest U.S. bank by assets, has taken direct stakes in defense-related startups 
Hadrian
 and 
Castelion
. 
Wars in Ukraine and the Middle East, as well as trade disputes with China, have threatened the flow of energy and key components needed for U.S. industries and defense, which spurred JPMorgan CEO 
Jamie Dimon
 into creating the initiative a year ago.
He put Petno, widely seen as 
one of two key possible successors
, in charge of the overall investment project. Combs, a former board 
member
 of the bank, reports directly to Dimon.
The scope of the project extends beyond supply chain, defense and manufacturing to include frontier technologies and the pharmaceutical and health-tech industries. 
Troy Rohrbaugh and Douglas Petno, co-CEOs of Commercial & Investment Bank at JPMorganChase.
Courtesy: JPMorganChase
"There's a lot of really interesting companies that have been working to solve some of these problems," Petno said. 
These "early venture-stage, advanced manufacturing companies have found ways to build these capabilities in the U.S." rather than relying on overseas trading partners, he said. 
In August, the bank helped lead a $1.4 billion 
funding round
 for Hadrian, which builds automated factories that make parts for the defense, aerospace and maritime sectors. A California factory run by Hadrian has 10 robots for every human employee, 
according
 to the startup's founder.
That month, JPMorgan was also involved in a $1 billion 
funding round
 for Castelion, whose flagship product is a relatively low-cost hypersonic missile called Blackbeard. Castelion was founded in late 2022 by a group of former SpaceX engineers.
"Our approach is to find generational companies and partner with them for the long term," Combs said in a statement Monday. 
"Putting more than $5 billion to work in our first year shows how many exceptional businesses are building in industries that strengthen critical supply chains," Combs said.
Risky business?  
JPMorgan making billions of dollars of direct investments in nascent companies is a departure from its usual role as a lender and advisor.
Before the defense initiative, the bank had moved away from taking startup stakes, which can be relatively risky bets on unproven technologies. JPMorgan 
spun off
 its private equity business, One Equity Partners, more than a decade ago.
In its more traditional role, JPMorgan has also financed and facilitated more than $230 billion for industries that support American national interests, according to the bank.
That part of its security and resiliency project, meant to hit $1.5 trillion over 10 years, is led by 
Jay Horine
, a former co-head of investment banking. 
JPMorgan built a team for the project of about three dozen bankers, who are aided by hundreds more across the firm, as it 
expanded
 the initiative to Europe and Canada.
Its bankers are mapping distribution lines for key products, from submarines and missiles to aircraft, to identify suppliers that may lack the capital or capacity to meet rising demand, according to Petno.
JPMorgan is also in regular communication with the U.S. Department of Defense and Department of Commerce about the government's needs, he said.

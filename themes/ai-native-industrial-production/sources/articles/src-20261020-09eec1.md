Hadrian brings automated production to Lockheed Martin’s Texas missile plant

Tuesday, 20 October 2026, 13:40

A Texas weapons plant is adopting a new production approach as pressure mounts to build more munitions. The partnership could add valuable capacity to the supply chain.

Hadrian will establish manufacturing operations at Lockheed Martin’s plant in Grand Prairie, Texas. The automated production cells will run around the clock and help manufacture components for missiles and other munitions.
As reported by 
Axios
.

The partnership comes amid the U.S. war in the Middle East and Pentagon efforts to boost weapons production. Under some agreements, munitions output is expected to triple or quadruple. For Hadrian, an $8 billion startup, partnering with a major defense contractor is an important step.

What the plant will produce

On October 14, Lockheed Martin marked the expansion of its missile and fire control plant in Grand Prairie, near Dallas. The expansion covers more than 100,000 square feet. Installing Hadrian equipment was part of the project.

Lockheed Martin employees will operate Hadrian’s production cells. They will help manufacture interceptors for Patriot systems, GMLRS rocket artillery rounds and precision-strike missiles.

The ceremony featured, among other things, a HIMARS launcher, internal and external stabilizers, Javelin anti-tank systems, an air-launched cruise missile and an air-launched rapid-response missile.

Hadrian CEO Chris Power explained that the partnership will allow the companies to combine their manufacturing systems and teams, while also providing additional capacity to support Hadrian’s plant in Arizona.

If the plant fills up, we’ll have significant capacity in reserve.

– Chris Power, CEO of Hadrian

Last year, the Grand Prairie plant manufactured hundreds of thousands of parts. Lockheed Martin CEO Jim Taiclet said new technologies, work methods and quality control systems are transforming traditional metalworking processes.

Technology, work methods and quality control systems are transforming 19th-century processes such as forging, casting and machining metal.

– Jim Taiclet, CEO of Lockheed Martin

To capitalize on the strengths of the United States, systemic change is needed. That’s exactly what’s happening now.

– Jim Taiclet, CEO of Lockheed Martin

Defense companies expand partnerships

The Lockheed Martin-Hadrian partnership reflects a shift in how major defense contractors work with young technology companies. Taiclet described Lockheed Martin’s strategy as one that is open to working with new players in the market.

Lockheed Martin recently also entered into partnerships with Saildrone, which makes unmanned boats, and Fortem Technologies, which develops radar and counter-drone systems.

Michael Duffy, the Pentagon’s head of weapons acquisition, tied expanded production to the idea of “peace through strength” and emphasized the deterrent value of a powerful industrial base.

The president says, “Peace through strength.” I prefer to say, “Peace through industrial might.”

– Michael Duffy, Pentagon head of weapons acquisition

A powerful industrial base can itself serve as a deterrent. I think we’ve seen that over the past year and a half.

– Michael Duffy, Pentagon head of weapons acquisition

A March report by the Ronald Reagan Institute gave U.S. manufacturing capabilities a D+ and defense modernization a D. The authors rated private-sector innovation A-.

Tulip Mania 1637: What the Dutch Bubble Reveals About AI in 2026

Tulip Mania 1637: What the Dutch Bubble Reveals About AI in 2026

                      Author:
                      
Charon N.

                      Reviewed by:
                      
EBC Research & Review Team

                    Published on: 2026-10-20                  

                                        
                    

                      Updated on: 2026-10-20                    

Tulip Mania was a rapid rise in prices for selected Dutch tulip bulbs during 1636 and early 1637, followed by a trading breakdown in February. The familiar account of a country ruined by flowers finds little support in the surviving financial records.

The episode has regained relevance in 2026 as investors debate whether spending on artificial intelligence will produce the earnings reflected in market valuations. The comparison concerns uncertain future demand, not equivalent assets or a prediction that AI will collapse.

A tulip bulb was a luxury horticultural good. AI businesses sell products and services, own infrastructure and publish financial results. Their differences are central to understanding the comparison.

Key Takeaways

Tulip Mania centred on rare bulbs traded within a relatively small network of Dutch collectors, merchants and growers.

Forward-style contracts allowed prices to be agreed before delivery, creating disputes when the market reversed.

The February 1637 collapse did not produce the nationwide economic ruin described in popular accounts.

AI companies generate measurable revenues, but heavy capital investment leaves future returns uncertain.

The historical comparison concerns market expectations and valuation, not evidence that AI is already in a bubble.

What Was Tulip Mania and Why Did Prices Rise?

Tulips reached the Dutch Republic through European networks connected to the Ottoman world during the sixteenth century. They were prized for their unusual colours and patterns as prosperous merchants and craftsmen spent more on gardens, art, and collectable goods. Rare named varieties attracted buyers who valued prestige as highly as horticulture.

Some of the most desirable flowers displayed streaks of contrasting colours. Modern botanical research attributes many of those patterns to tulip breaking virus, which often weakened infected plants. Their appearance could be prized even as the stock became more difficult to propagate reliably.

Growers also had a commercial reason to acquire rare specimens. A bulb could produce offsets that eventually became marketable bulbs, but multiplication took time. A fashionable variety could not be supplied as quickly as collectors wanted it, leaving buyers uncertain about future scarcity and prices.

Historian Anne Goldgar found that trading involved an interconnected circle of growers, merchants, craftsmen and collectors. High prices for exceptional varieties were known before 1636. The acceleration in late 1636 and January 1637 brought greater attention to contracts and anticipated resale prices.

How Did Tulip Trading Work Before the 1637 Collapse?

The timing of cultivation shaped the market. Bulbs remained underground for much of the year, so buyers and sellers agreed on prices and deferred payment and delivery until the bulbs could be lifted. Such agreements resembled forward contracts, although they lacked the standardised clearing and margin systems used by modern futures exchanges.

An agreed price was not necessarily a completed sale for cash. Consider a buyer promising to pay 1,000 guilders for a bulb delivered in summer. If comparable bulbs subsequently attracted only 400 guilders, that buyer would have a strong incentive to dispute the commitment. The numbers are hypothetical, but the problem of unsettled obligations was real.

Expectations of higher prices encouraged speculative contracting. Yet archival records do not support the popular claim that the same bulb routinely changed hands many times each day. A purchase agreement, a quoted resale price and a completed delivery were different transactions.

This complicates claims about the fortunes lost. A contract’s nominal value does not establish how much cash changed hands, what payment was eventually made or whether the original terms were enforceable.

What Caused Tulip Mania To End In February 1637?

Trading confidence broke down in early February 1637. Buyers became unwilling to pay prices agreed during the boom, leaving sellers with disputed contracts and bulbs they could no longer readily place at earlier valuations. Historians have not established one definitive trigger.

Accounts often describe traders in Haarlem failing to attract bids at previously accepted prices. Yet an estate auction at Alkmaar on 5 February reportedly drew strong prices for prized bulbs. The contrasting accounts caution against describing the collapse as one nationwide auction failure in which every bulb simultaneously lost its market.

Payment disputes continued after the reversal. In Goldgar’s archival research, a Haarlem grower named Pieter Jacobsz had received a valuable Gouda bulb that remained unpaid for. Even after his death, the seller pursued the debt through the grower’s family. The record captures the personal consequences of contracts that had been easier to sign than to settle.

The episode damaged trust within trading networks. Tulip cultivation continued, however, and collectors still purchased exceptional varieties. Prices agreed during the speculative surge, and the confidence supporting those obligations, reversed sharply.

Did Tulip Mania Really Bankrupt the Dutch Economy?

The familiar story includes workers abandoning their occupations, ordinary households staking their savings and a wave of bankruptcies after the crash. Charles Mackay helped popularise that narrative in the nineteenth century, drawing on earlier writers who relied heavily on seventeenth-century satire.

Goldgar’s examination of court documents and private records supports a narrower account. Traders belonged to a comparatively small and socially connected group. Some experienced financial difficulties and commercial relationships deteriorated, but the evidence does not establish a national depression or widespread bankruptcy caused by the tulip market.

Historians also disagree about whether the episode qualifies as an asset-price bubble. Some identify 
speculative prices followed by a sudden reversal
, while others question whether the surviving transactions establish prices persistently detached from economic value. Rare bulbs had value to collectors and growers even when later agreements proved unsustainable.

A quoted price cannot establish that money was paid, while a disputed promise does not automatically produce a realised loss of the same amount. These differences limit how confidently the episode can serve as a model for modern financial crises.

How Does Tulip Mania Compare With the 2026 AI Boom?

The common feature is the difficulty of pricing future demand. A seventeenth-century buyer could not know how fashionable a bulb would remain or what another collector would pay. Investors in AI-related businesses can assess present revenues, but must still estimate how much 
new computing capacity will be used
 and what profit it will generate.

AI Investment, 2026

Revenue, earnings and expected future cash flow

Semiconductor capacity, data centres and electricity

Capital commitment

Private contracts, often with deferred payment

Corporate capital expenditure and infrastructure financing

Main uncertainty

Future bulb prices and contract settlement

Demand, profitability and returns on invested capital

The scale of present-day spending is substantial. In April 2026, the International Energy Agency reported that five large technology companies spent more than $400 billion in combined capital expenditure in 2025, with a further 75% increase expected in 2026. Those figures cover overall company spending, driven heavily by data centres, and cannot be attributed exclusively to AI.

Building computing capacity involves purchasing processors, securing electricity and constructing facilities before all prospective demand becomes billable revenue. Even where adoption expands, low utilisation or declining prices for computing services can reduce returns on the capital committed.

Modern investors have access to evidence unavailable to Dutch bulb buyers, including financial statements, operating margins, cash flow, and management guidance. Nevertheless, group earnings do not always reveal the returns of individual AI projects, while announced construction pipelines differ from completed facilities generating revenue.

The IMF’s October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report acknowledged that core companies in the AI ecosystem had remained profitable. It warned that weaker or less durable investment returns could trigger repricing, with leverage and concentrated exposures potentially intensifying selling. The assessment identified a financial risk, not a confirmed AI bubble.

Capital Expenditures by Hyperscalers

A technology can become commercially important while some investments in it disappoint. If a market valuation assumes profits will triple but they only double, asset prices can fall despite genuine business growth. That possibility offers a more meaningful comparison than treating computers and tulip bulbs as economically equivalent.

What Can Traders Learn From Tulip Mania?

A tulip price agreed in 1637 did not guarantee payment. Modern exchanges have more formal settlement arrangements, but an executable market price can still differ from the last quoted price when liquidity deteriorates. The cost of exiting a crowded position remains relevant even in markets with far more sophisticated infrastructure.

For AI-related securities, traders can compare revenue growth with capital expenditure, free cash flow, data-centre utilisation and the timing of new capacity. They can also assess whether valuations leave room for construction delays, slower adoption or weaker operating margins.

Goldgar’s findings show how easily an exaggerated historical narrative can survive long after the original transactions are forgotten. Modern financial reporting provides better evidence, but 
market euphoria
 can still lead investors to price optimistic forecasts as though they were assured outcomes.

Conclusion

Tulip Mania was a striking breakdown of confidence within a narrow trading market, not a documented collapse of the Dutch economy. For AI in 2026, the question is whether future earnings will justify the capital already committed and the valuations investors are willing to pay.

Why we invested in Arda - by Apoorv Agrawal - Tailwinds Tailwinds Subscribe Sign in Why we invested in Arda Speeding up American manufacturing Apoorv Agrawal Oct 22, 2026 4 Share Why we invested in Arda Speeding up American manufacturing Flip over your iPhone and it says: Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China. America is still the best place in the world to design things. We’ve just mostly stopped building them here. Arda, which came out of stealth this week, wants to rewrite the second half of that sentence with AI and robotics. Its founder, Bob McGrew, and I go back over a dozen years, to when we were both engineers at Palantir. Bottom line up front on why we invested: Bob makes no small plans, and he’s been preparing for this one for at least two decades The mission is a big one... and it MUST be pursued The plan is sequenced right. Act 1 is software for manufacturing engineers. Act 2 is robotic lines that update as fast as the design It’s early. If you’re an engineer or researcher looking for a big mission, now is the time. Please reach out! Our detailed thesis is below. If you’d rather watch than read, Bob and I also talked through all of this with Kate Rooney on CNBC : The team Bob makes no small plans. He has a habit of picking the most audacious mission in the room, usually before anyone agrees it’s possible. He joined PayPal in 2000, when moving money over the internet sounded reckless. In 2005 he left his PhD to become Palantir’s second engineer, ran engineering and product for its first decade, and co-developed forward deployed engineering... a model half of AI is copying today. Then OpenAI, a small nonprofit lab that wanted to build AGI. He spent his first year on its robotics team, led research from 2018 to 2024, and left as Chief Research Officer right after shipping reasoning. Seen that way, he’s been preparing for Arda for two decades. A problem this size needs frontier AI, robotics and the forward deployed muscle to ship inside a customer’s four walls, and Bob has spent a career collecting all three. Robotics has been on his mind for at least ten years. After Palantir he taught himself deep learning, had a robot playing checkers from vision by 2016, decided robotics wasn’t ready to be a startup yet, and went to OpenAI to learn. He looked hard at other ideas before picking this one, and we spent a lot of that time with him. There was as much conviction in the paths he didn’t pick as in the one he did. Alex Mark, CTO, ran engineering for Palantir Gotham’s framework and application layers, about 80 developers. Then, instead of starting a company, he went to the US Digital Service and led the technical build of the IRS Direct File pilot, which shipped in seven months. Augustus Odena, Chief AI Officer, spent about six years at Google Brain and co-authored Scratchpads , an early version of the think-before-you-answer idea that Bob later shipped at OpenAI. He then co-founded Adept to build agents that take actions inside software, which is pretty much what Arda’s first product is. Former Palantir engineer Jakob Frick rounds out the co-founders, and the team adds manufacturing engineers from Tesla and AI researchers from MIT, plus Palantir alums Patrick Woody, Grace Wang, Lucas Lemanowicz, Tim Yousaf, Jennifer Tao, and Paul Ryan. They’re friends from my Palantir days, all of them certified legends, and in very good company with the rest of the Arda team. Bob and me outside Palantir HQ. The Mission Let’s start with one chart: This isn’t only an economics problem. It’s a sovereignty problem. Over forty years we moved the assembly of our most important products into the territory of our main geopolitical adversary, and that assembly base became a cornerstone of China’s industrial strength. The risk isn’t hypothetical. When China sanctioned Skydio , the largest US drone maker, in October 2024, its battery supplier was ordered to cut it off, and Skydio had to ration one battery per drone. A country that can’t build its own drones, or the equipment that powers its data centers, isn’t fully in control of its own security. Labor cost is part of the story. Chinese manufacturing wages run at roughly a fifth of US levels. But Bob’s diagnosis is sharper: the products that matter now change constantly. An iPhone is mostly redesigned every year, only about a quarter of its parts carry over, and the line runs at full rate for roughly four months. You can retrain a person in a day. Reprogramming a conventional automated station takes three to six months and can cost close to $1M. So fast-changing products get built by armies of people who can be retrained quickly... and that’s Shenzhen. The strategic need is clear but can we make the economics work in the US? Should we answer cheap labor with cheaper labor? No. Well, we can’t. And even if we wanted those jobs, we don’t have the people. US manufacturers could need 3.8 million workers between 2024 and 2033, and about 1.9 million of those roles could go unfilled. The answer is technology: AI and robotics that let a factory reconfigure as fast as the design changes. And you start where the stakes are highest. Arda is starting where I think the US can least afford to depend on anyone else: Defense. The Army wants to buy at least a million drones over two to three years, versus about 50,000 a year today. Roughly 20x. AI infrastructure. Large power transformers averaged about 128 weeks of lead time in Wood Mackenzie’s 2025 survey. Arda already works with manufacturers of transformers and electrical equipment that bring data center power online. Anduril is the anchor customer. The teams started working together before Arda was even incorporated, and Arda is now used in every Anduril division as part of ArsenalOS, Anduril’s manufacturing software. Basic work instructions that took about 10 hours now take 15 minutes. Workflows that took up to 30 hours take about 2.5. As Anduril co-founder and COO Matt Grimm put it : “We would be behind on our production goals without it.” The Plan: Act 1 and Act 2 Act 1: Cursor for manufacturing engineers. Picture a 7,500-piece Lego set that shows up fully built, with no instruction book. A CAD file is a lot like that. Every part sits in its final position, but nothing says what order to assemble them in, with what tools, at which station. Working that out is the manufacturing engineer’s job, and today it’s spread across Lucidchart, PowerPoint and Excel, with no single source of truth. Arda built one: an IDE for manufacturing engineering with an agent inside. The agent reads the design files, works out the assembly sequence (using robotic motion planning under the hood) and writes the work instructions for the engineer to review. One Anduril ME lead had 56 steps written for him in 15 minutes, against his own estimate of 5 to 10 hours by hand. Pricing is usage-based. For customers with ITAR data, Arda deploys inside the customer’s own systems... the forward deployed engineering playbook! Act 2: robotic lines that change as fast as the design. Traditional automation is a Rube Goldberg machine. Half the effort goes into designing it, half into calibrating it, and it’s built for exactly one product. Change that product by 5 or 10% and you’re looking at months of rework. Act 2 is AI agents that program robots to carry out the Act 1 plan, station by station, with perception and manipulation models making them robust enough to deploy without weeks of tuning. The goal is a line you can reconfigure in days, about as fast as you can retrain a person. Arda is already prototyping it. If that works, the economics flip, and fast-changing products no longer have to be built in Asia. Bob’s line: “Imagine a fully automated version of Shenzhen in West Texas.” The path runs from defense and data center equipment to routers and consumer drones, and eventually to the smartphone, which no one has ever built at scale in America. Why this order? The manufacturing plan has to live in software before a robot can execute it, and Act 1 puts Arda on real factory floors every day. Starting with the robots would have meant guessing at the problem. Who should work at Arda It’s early, and the foundation gets poured in the next year or two. You should talk to them if you’re: A software engineer who wants to sit next to the user. If you loved the FDE life, this is that... applied to atoms. An AI researcher in agents, planning, perception or manipulation. Act 2 is an open research problem with a customer already waiting. A robotics or automation engineer tired of one-off stations that break the moment the product changes. A manufacturing engineer from a place like Tesla, Apple or SpaceX who has lived through a design change hitting the line. A call to arms One thing I took from Palantir is that the strongest force on a team is a big mission. That’s why I joined Palantir. Arda is that kind of a life-changing mission. For fifteen years the best engineers went to work on software, and lately on models. The next decade needs some of that talent pointed at the physical world... at the factories that build our drones, our grid equipment and, one day, our phones. If you’re looking for a mission big enough to spend the next ten years on, this is for you.

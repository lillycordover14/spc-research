World's biggest economies by GDP, from the U.S. to Canada
The largest economies in the world by nominal GDP, from the U.S. and China to Canada, and what drives each one today
By
Colleen Cabili
·

Illustration: Quartz
Gross domestic product remains the default yardstick for comparing national economic power, even though it is a blunt one. GDP measures the total value of goods and services produced inside a country's borders over a set period, and it reveals nothing about how that output is shared or what it costs to live there. Lined up against one another, though, the largest economies in the world show how economic weight has shifted over the past half-century. The United States has held first place for well over a century. The distance between it and China, however, has narrowed sharply since Beijing opened its economy to foreign investment and private enterprise. India has climbed the table faster than any other major economy, carried by a young labor force and an expanding services sector. Several longtime European powers have grown more slowly and lost ground in relative terms.
These 10 countries are also where capital, trade flows, and diplomatic leverage concentrate. Together they account for the bulk of global output, and decisions made by their central banks, legislatures, and trade negotiators land far outside their own borders. A slowdown in Chinese construction reaches iron ore exporters in Brazil and Australia. A rate decision by the U.S. Federal Reserve moves currencies from Tokyo to Johannesburg. Germany's energy and industrial policy sets the terms for much of the European Union's manufacturing base.
This ranking uses nominal GDP, the measure compiled by the International Monetary Fund that converts each country's output into U.S. dollars at market exchange rates. Nominal figures carry no adjustment for differences in the cost of living from one country to the next. That adjustment, known as purchasing power parity, produces a noticeably different order — one in which China ranks first and India sits higher than any European economy. Both measures are legitimate. They answer different questions.
What follows is a country-by-country look at the top 10 economies by GDP — their size, and also the industries, demographics, and policy choices that keep each one on the list.
United States leads in nominal GDP
Illustration: Quartz
The United States holds the largest economy in the world by nominal GDP, a position it took after overtaking the U.K. more than a century ago. Its output rests on a broad mix of technology, finance, health care and consumer services, reinforced by the dollar's role as the primary global reserve currency.
Consumer spending accounts for roughly two-thirds of U.S. economic output, a far larger share than in most other major economies, which lean harder on exports or industrial production. That composition makes American growth sensitive to household income, credit conditions and employment in ways that export-led economies are not.
Technology companies clustered in California and Washington state —

Deep capital markets are part of the explanation. American firms can raise money by issuing public stock and bonds rather than depending mainly on bank lending, which is more common across much of Europe and Asia. That financing structure helped a generation of software and semiconductor companies scale to enormous size.
The U.S. labor market carries fewer restrictions on hiring and dismissal than most Western European systems. That flexibility has allowed employment to rebound quickly after downturns, including the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic contraction.
Energy has shifted in the country's favor over the past 15 years. Hydraulic fracturing in shale formations turned the U.S. into one of the world's leading producers of both oil and natural gas, cutting dependence on imported fuel and reshaping its standing in global energy markets.
Federal Reserve rate decisions ripple outward because so much international debt and trade is priced in dollars. Borrowing costs in emerging markets often move with U.S. monetary policy rather than local conditions.
Structural pressures persist. Federal debt has grown substantially relative to GDP, income inequality remains wide by rich-country standards, and health care spending per person exceeds that of every other large economy.
China ranks second by nominal GDP
Illustration: Quartz
China holds the second-largest economy in the world by nominal GDP, a position built on manufacturing scale, infrastructure investment, and exports since market reforms began in the late 1970s under Deng Xiaoping. It is the world's largest manufacturing nation and the leading goods trading partner for dozens of countries.
That expansion leaned on state-directed investment, heavy infrastructure spending, and a factory base supplying electronics, machinery, textiles, and chemicals to the rest of the world. Shenzhen, a modest border town across from Hong Kong when it was designated a special economic zone in 1980, became a manufacturing and technology hub within a single generation. The Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2013, extended that reach through infrastructure financing across Africa, Southeast Asia, Central Asia, and parts of Europe.
Growth has cooled from the double-digit annual rates common in the 2000s to a far more moderate pace. The slowdown tracks a maturing economy, an aging workforce, and a property sector carrying heavy debt at developers such as Evergrande, whose 2021 default showed how much household wealth and local government revenue rested on continuous construction.
Beijing has steered the economy toward domestic consumption and advanced industry in response, treating semiconductors, electric vehicles, and renewable energy as strategic priorities. BYD has become one of the world's biggest producers of electric vehicles and batteries, and Chinese factories dominate global solar panel manufacturing.
The yuan is not fully convertible on international markets, which caps its role as a reserve currency against the dollar and euro. The state also retains control over the largest banks, energy firms, and telecom operators, letting the government channel credit toward favored sectors in a way more market-driven systems do not.
Population decline has become a structural constraint. China's population began shrinking in 2022, the result of decades under the one-child policy, which ended in 2015. Sustaining expansion from here depends on productivity gains rather than a larger labor force.
Germany is Europe's largest economy
Illustration: Quartz
Germany ranks third in the world by nominal GDP and holds the largest economy in the European Union, built on precision manufacturing, automotive production, and exports. Volkswagen, Siemens, and BASF anchor an industrial base whose engineering reputation underpins much of the country's export earnings.
Machinery, vehicles, and chemical products make up the core of what Germany sells abroad. The rest of Europe, China, and the U.S. absorb much of it. That export orientation leaves the economy more exposed to swings in global demand than countries whose growth rests mainly on domestic spending. Weaker car sales in China or higher tariffs in North America show up quickly in German factory output.
Energy supply turned into a liability after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. German industry had leaned on relatively cheap Russian pipeline gas. Replacing it with liquefied natural gas imports and other sources raised input costs for chemicals, steel, and glass — sectors where energy accounts for a large share of production costs.
The Mittelstand, the layer of small and medium-sized, often family-owned manufacturers, supplies much of the country's industrial depth. Many of these firms lead narrow global niches, making printing presses, machine tools, or specialized components. They stay largely unknown outside their customer base even while shipping worldwide.
Demographics work against long-term growth. The working-age share of the population is shrinking as the postwar generation retires, a pattern Germany shares with Japan and much of Western Europe. Immigration has offset part of that gap, including the intake of refugees and labor migrants over the past decade. Moving newcomers into skilled industrial roles still requires language training and credential recognition that take years.
Fiscal policy has traditionally prized balanced budgets, a preference written into the constitutional borrowing limit known as the debt brake. Berlin has since loosened that constraint to fund higher defense spending and infrastructure investment. The change follows a shift in European security conditions and years of underinvestment in rail, bridges, and digital networks.
India is the fastest-growing major economy
Illustration: Quartz
India holds the fourth spot among the largest economies in the world by nominal GDP and expands faster than any other major economy, with growth led by services rather than factory output. It passed the U.K., its former colonial ruler, in total economic output in recent years.
Information technology and business process outsourcing anchor the country's export earnings. Bengaluru, Hyderabad, and Pune host most of that industry, serving corporate clients across North America and Europe. Tata Consultancy Services and Infosys grew from domestic software houses into global contractors running software development, systems maintenance, and back-office work for multinationals. The sector employs millions of white-collar workers and earns foreign currency without the port capacity and industrial land that export manufacturing requires.
Demographics set India apart from the other economies near the top of the ranking. Its median age sits in the late 20s, and its labor force keeps growing while China's, Japan's, and Western Europe's contract. That pattern is known as a demographic dividend. Turning it into higher incomes depends on creating enough jobs for the millions of young people entering the workforce each year, and manufacturing employment has expanded more slowly than successive governments planned.
Digital infrastructure has advanced faster than the physical kind. The Unified Payments Interface, developed under the government's Digital India program, made the country one of the largest markets for real-time digital transactions and drew millions of previously unbanked households into the formal financial system. Payments that once required cash now move through mobile phones at street stalls and small shops.
The constraints are concrete. Electricity supply remains inconsistent in parts of the country, ports face congestion, and the road and rail network is still being upgraded. Income is concentrated in a handful of large conglomerates and the biggest cities, while rural districts trail on earnings, health care, and schooling. Those gaps determine how much of the headline growth rate reaches the average household.
Japan remains a manufacturing and technology power
Illustration: Quartz
Japan has the fifth-largest economy in the world by nominal GDP, built on advanced manufacturing, automotive production, and electronics. It ranked second worldwide for decades until China passed it in 2010, and

The economy has contended with deflation and weak growth since its asset price bubble collapsed in the early 1990s, a stretch known as the Lost Decades. Land and stock valuations that peaked in 1989 slid for years afterward, leaving banks and corporations clearing bad debt well into the 2000s. The Bank of Japan held interest rates at or below zero for an extended stretch, a stance that set it apart from other major central banks until it ended its negative rate policy in 2024.
Demographics are the sharpest constraint on growth. Japan has one of the oldest populations on Earth, with a median age near 49, and the total population has been shrinking for more than a decade. Fewer working-age residents means a smaller labor pool alongside rising government spending on pensions and medical care. Policy responses include raising the effective retirement age, drawing more women into paid employment, and widening visa categories for foreign workers, though Japan admits far fewer immigrants than most other wealthy countries.
Export strength sits in precision engineering and the components that feed global supply chains, not only in finished consumer goods. Fanuc and Keyence lead in industrial robotics and factory sensors installed in plants worldwide, and Japanese firms supply a large share of the equipment and specialty materials that semiconductor fabrication depends on. Auto parts remain a major export across Asia and North America.
The yen's exchange rate carries unusual weight for Japan. A weaker yen makes exports cheaper abroad and inflates profits repatriated from overseas operations, but it also raises the cost of imported energy and food — a direct hit to household budgets, since Japan buys most of the energy it consumes from abroad.
United Kingdom ranks among the top six
Illustration: Quartz
The U.K. sits among the six largest economies in the world by nominal GDP, with services — financial, legal, insurance, consulting and creative — generating the clear majority of national output. London handles a larger share of global foreign exchange turnover than any other city.
The City of London and Canary Wharf host international banks, insurance underwriters and asset managers whose clients sit largely outside Britain. Lloyd's of London has operated as a specialist insurance market since the late 17th century and still writes cover for shipping, aviation and energy projects worldwide. Financial and professional services are among the country's largest export earners and among its biggest sources of tax revenue. The London Stock Exchange remains one of the larger venues by market capitalization, though its share of global listings has narrowed as U.S. and Asian exchanges expanded and as some British companies shifted their primary listings to New York.
Britain's departure from the European Union took full effect at the start of 2021, once the transition period ended, and it reset trade with a bloc that is still the country's biggest commercial partner. Goods crossing the Channel now require customs declarations, rules-of-origin paperwork and regulatory checks that were unnecessary inside the single market. Isolating that change from the pandemic and the 2022 energy price shock is difficult, and estimates of the cost to growth vary widely.
Manufacturing accounts for a smaller slice of output than in Germany, Japan or Italy. Pharmaceuticals, aerospace components and high-end carmaking still matter, with Rolls-Royce building aero engines and Jaguar Land Rover and Bentley assembling vehicles for export.
Regional imbalance is stark for a wealthy country. London and the southeast produce far more per person than the northeast of England, Wales or Northern Ireland, a gap that successive governments have tried to close through investment programs aimed outside the capital.
Sterling is one of a small group of currencies central banks hold in reserve, though it trails the dollar and the euro by a wide margin in the amounts held worldwide.
France combines industry with tourism revenue
Illustration: Quartz
France ranks among the largest economies in the world on the strength of an unusually diversified base — luxury goods, aerospace, agriculture, nuclear electricity, and tourism. It draws more international visitors each year than any other country.
LVMH, the conglomerate behind Louis Vuitton and Dior, counts among Europe's most valuable listed companies and has turned French design heritage into a durable export. Airbus, with its main commercial aircraft operations in Toulouse, competes directly with 
Boeing
 
$BA
 and anchors a supply chain running through Germany, Spain, and the U.K. France is also the European Union's largest agricultural producer, and its farmers have long collected the biggest share of Common Agricultural Policy subsidies.
Tourism money reaches well beyond Paris. The Louvre is the most-visited museum in the world. Visitor spending also spreads across the Côte d'Azur, the Alpine ski resorts, the Bordeaux and Champagne wine regions, and the château corridor of the Loire Valley. Hotels, restaurants, and transport operators collect foreign currency that helps offset a persistent deficit in goods trade.
The state plays a larger direct role here than in the U.S. or the U.K. Public spending takes up a high share of GDP by rich-country standards, and the government holds controlling stakes in strategic firms such as the utility EDF and the rail operator SNCF. That reach shapes how policy gets made and how it gets resisted. A 2023 plan to lift the retirement age from 62 to 64 triggered months of strikes before it passed without a final vote in the National Assembly.
Energy policy separates France from its neighbors. Nuclear reactors supply most domestic electricity, a structure built deliberately after the oil shocks of the 1970s, and the country usually exports power to the rest of Europe. Germany went the other way and shut its last reactors in 2023.
Labor rules remain contested. The statutory 35-hour work week, phased in around the turn of the millennium, sits alongside dismissal protections that employers say discourage hiring. Successive governments have tried to loosen those rules to bring down youth unemployment.
Italy anchors southern Europe's economy
Illustration: Quartz
Italy is the third-largest economy in the eurozone after Germany and France, and it ranks among the 10 largest in the world by nominal GDP. Its output rests on export manufacturing, design-driven consumer brands, and a tourism sector that draws visitors to Rome, Florence, and Venice year-round.
Italian manufacturing is unusually decentralized. Much of it sits in small and medium-sized family-owned firms clustered in industrial districts across the north, producing machine tools, packaging equipment, furniture, ceramics, and textiles for buyers abroad. These companies rarely appear in global brand rankings, but their components and production machinery turn up in factories across Europe and Asia.
The consumer-facing side of the economy is far more visible. 
Ferrari
 
$RACE
 builds its cars in Maranello, while Gucci, Prada, and Armani have turned Italian design and craftsmanship into export categories in their own right. Fashion and luxury goods command a price premium abroad that few other national industries match, and the sector supports a long supply chain of leather workers, tanneries, and textile mills across Tuscany and Veneto.
Public debt is the central constraint on fiscal policy in Rome. Italy carries one of the heaviest debt loads relative to GDP among large economies, the accumulated result of decades of government borrowing. That leaves Italian government bonds sensitive to swings in investor sentiment and to European Central Bank policy — a dynamic that turned acute during the eurozone debt crisis of the early 2010s.
A north-south divide runs through the economy. Milan and Turin anchor a wealthy industrial corridor, while southern regions have long recorded higher unemployment and lower output per person. The gap has shaped Italian politics for more than a century and remains the focus of national development spending.
Demographics narrow the long-term outlook. Italy has one of the lowest birth rates in Europe and an aging workforce, which shrinks the tax base that services the debt. Productivity growth has also been weak for roughly two decades, leaving the country less room to grow its way out of that debt load.
Brazil leads Latin America's economy
Illustration: Quartz
Brazil is the largest economy in Latin America and ranks among the world's 10 biggest by nominal GDP, built on agricultural exports, mining, and a domestic consumer market of more than 200 million people. It is a leading global producer of soybeans, coffee, sugar, and iron ore.
Brazilian agribusiness expanded sharply over the past two decades as farming pushed into the cerrado savanna and planting techniques improved. The country became one of the largest food exporters anywhere and a central supplier to China and the rest of Asia. JBS, the world's largest meat processor, and the mining company Vale rank among Brazil's biggest sources of export earnings and industrial employment.
Growth has been less steady than in wealthier developed economies. Brazil has moved through stretches of high inflation, sharp currency depreciation, and recession, interspersed with commodity-driven booms. The Banco Central do Brasil has repeatedly used high benchmark interest rates to pull inflation back down, holding its policy rate well above levels typical in the U.S. or the euro area. The real's value against the dollar has swung widely through those cycles.
Commodity prices shape much of the trajectory. Strong Chinese demand for iron ore and soybeans lifts Brazilian export revenue and government tax receipts together, and weak demand pulls both down. China has been Brazil's largest trading partner for years, which makes economic planning in Brasília partly contingent on decisions made in Beijing.
Income is distributed unevenly. Wealth clusters in São Paulo, the financial and industrial center, and in Rio de Janeiro. Incomes and access to public services lag across much of the north and the northeast.
Infrastructure has not kept pace with export volumes either. Ports, highways, and rail lines carrying grain from the interior to the coast create bottlenecks. Those delays raise the cost of moving goods to market, eroding margins that Brazilian producers would otherwise capture from their scale and farmland.
Canada rounds out the top 10
Illustration: Quartz
Canada ranks among the 10 largest economies in the world by nominal GDP, supported by natural resource exports, a tightly regulated banking system, and deep trade integration with the U.S. The two countries share the world's longest international border, and their supply chains — automotive manufacturing above all — operate as a single continental network.
Energy anchors the export side. Canada holds some of the largest proven oil reserves on the planet, concentrated in the Alberta oil sands, and ships crude, natural gas, and electricity south of the border. The country is also a leading global supplier of potash, uranium, timber, and base metals used in agriculture, construction, and electronics. Commodity prices swing, and government revenue in producing provinces such as Alberta and Saskatchewan swings with them. Services account for most employment, concentrated in Ontario and Quebec, so the resource sector shapes the export mix more than it shapes the domestic job market.
Canadian banking has a reputation for caution that held through the 2008 financial crisis, when no major Canadian bank failed or needed the emergency rescues seen in the U.S. and parts of Europe. Stricter mortgage underwriting and oversight concentrated in a single federal regulator are the usual explanations. A small group of large institutions dominates the sector, among them Royal Bank of Canada and Toronto-
Dominion
 
$D
 Bank, several of which run sizable retail networks in the U.S.
Exports to the U.S. make up the large majority of Canadian foreign sales, which leaves the economy exposed to shifts in Washington's tariff and trade policy. The Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement, in effect since 2020 and the successor to the North American Free Trade Agreement, governs most of that commerce and keeps the bulk of goods crossing the continent duty-free.
Housing affordability is the most visible domestic strain. Prices in Toronto and Vancouver have climbed far faster than wages over the past two decades, pushed by population growth through immigration and limited new construction in dense urban cores. The gap between incomes and prices has moved housing policy to the center of federal and provincial politics.

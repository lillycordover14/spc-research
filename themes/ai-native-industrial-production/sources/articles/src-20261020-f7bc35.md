Summary
Goldman Sachs says nominal growth, not the level of rates, is the key driver for U.S. small caps, noting they have historically done best when nominal GDP is above 5% and rising. It also sees attractive valuations and broadening earnings strength.
The note offers a framework for holding small caps even if Treasury yields stay elevated, which could support rotation flows toward the segment as long as growth data hold up. The flip side is that the thesis is explicitly tied to nominal growth, so small caps may become more sensitive to economic data surprises than to moves in yields, with a softer PMI or GDP print a bigger threat than a rate rise. Goldman’s view that inflation pressure is largely energy-driven also links the outlook to oil: a further spike in crude would test the argument that broader inflation is moderating. The emphasis on dispersion suggests index-level small-cap exposure may lag a more selective approach.

Goldman Sachs says investors weighing small-cap stocks should focus less on the level of interest rates and more on the strength of nominal economic growth, arguing in a new note that the growth backdrop currently favours the asset class.
The bank said the central question is not whether the 10-year Treasury yield sits above 5%, but whether nominal growth remains healthy. Small caps have historically performed best when nominal GDP, which combines real growth and inflation, is above 5% and accelerating, according to Goldman Sachs. Nominal GDP is currently running near 6% year on year, with real GDP at around 2.3%.
Goldman Sachs said higher rates alone have not historically been a reason to avoid small caps, noting the group has generally posted positive returns during periods of sharply rising rates.
The bank’s macro outlook supports that view. It expects U.S. growth of about 2.3%, unemployment near 4% and relatively contained recession risk, while purchasing managers’ indices have reached their highest levels in more than five years. Goldman Sachs also said inflation looks narrower than headline figures suggest, with much of the pressure coming from energy and broader measures continuing to moderate.
On fundamentals, the bank said earnings strength among smaller companies appears to be broadening rather than concentrating in a handful of names. It described valuations as attractive relative to both large caps and historical levels, offering what it sees as a compelling entry point alongside improving fundamentals.
Goldman Sachs added that a rising cost of capital raises the bar for companies, rewarding profitability, durable earnings and strong returns on capital while penalising weaker businesses. That widening gap between winners and losers, it said, may create a more favourable environment for stock picking and active management.
The argument rests on growth holding up. If nominal GDP slows meaningfully, or energy-driven inflation broadens into wider price pressure that forces rates higher still, the conditions Goldman Sachs identifies as supportive for small caps would weaken. For now, the bank sees the growth backdrop doing more of the work than the rate debate suggests.

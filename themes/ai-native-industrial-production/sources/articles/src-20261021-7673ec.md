Hermeus, the venture-backed startup that has been building hypersonic aircraft since 2018, wants to sell the Pentagon an unmanned supersonic "bomb truck" first — and use the cash to fund a Mach 5 passenger jet later. In a wide-ranging interview, co-founder AJ Piplica and new CEO Zach Shore lay out a roadmap that runs from the Mach 1.21 supersonic flight in May 2026 to a pre-cooled turbine at Mach 3 in 2027, and argue that hardware-rich iteration — not 20-year contracts — is the only way to close the gap with China. Since the interview, Mk 2.1 has reached Mach 1.6 and released a payload in flight. The company floats outside the Air Force's Collaborative Combat Aircraft program of record, leans on the Defense Innovation Unit as its window into the Pentagon, and says the real bottleneck is not physics but test ranges and procurement culture. The report maps Hermeus's technical milestones, the economics of unmanned mass versus manned fighters, and the structural case for why hypersonic flight may arrive through a defense balance sheet before it arrives through a boarding pass.

For roughly eight years, the United States has poured money into hypersonics with little to show for it. "We've spent huge amounts of money, tens of billions of dollars, and really haven't fielded anything yet," said AJ Piplica, co-founder of the aerospace startup Hermeus, speaking on the Core Memory podcast. His diagnosis is not that the physics is too hard. It is that the way America builds aircraft is too slow.

The budget trail is consistent with the first half of that claim: the Congressional Research Service put the Pentagon's fiscal 2026 request for hypersonic weapons at $3.9 billion, down from $6.9 billion in fiscal 2025. Hermeus wants to fix the second half by doing the opposite of what the defense industrial base does. Since 2018 the Atlanta-born company has been building aircraft largely at its own expense, flying them early, and crashing them on purpose if that is what it takes to learn faster. Its Quarter Horse Mark 2.1 went supersonic on just its third flight — Mach 1.21, with 45 minutes of total flight time on the airframe — and a fourth flight was scheduled for the day of the recording. A second airframe, Mark 2.2, was due at Spaceport America the following Monday.

The Mach 5 jet of the company's name is the long-term goal. What Piplica and CEO Zach Shore spent most of the interview describing is the ungainly, cash-generating stepping stone that has to come first: a low-cost, unmanned supersonic aircraft that the military can load with bombs and, if necessary, lose.

The case for crashing planes on purpose

Piplica credits Elon Musk with the cultural shift that makes Hermeus possible: reducing "the stakes" and "the consequences of something going wrong." In aerospace, unexpected outcomes are where the learning is. If a company can get back into flight in weeks or a few months rather than years, risk becomes manageable.

Shore, a former Marine signals-intelligence officer and Anduril alumnus who took over as CEO about six weeks before the recording, calls the same idea being "hardware rich." Mark 2.2 was arriving that Monday, which meant: "If I lawn dart that plane, I just roll the next plane out."

The pace claim is concrete. Flight one was takeoff, a circuit and a landing. Flight two raised and lowered the gear. Flight three went supersonic. Shore contrasts this with programs that "notch up by 0.1, 0.1, 0.1" over years before daring to attempt Mach 1.

Since the interview, the campaign has run ahead of the table's pending column. On its fourth flight in mid-July, Mk 2.1 reached Mach 1.6 — above the Mach 1.5 target — with under 90 minutes of total flight time logged. On July 31 it released an external payload in flight, its fifth flight since spring under DIU's HyCAT program, a step toward the weapons-carrying role Shore describes. In September the FAA authorized Mk 2.2 for up to six supersonic flights through August 2027; Hermeus has not announced a first-flight date.

An F-16 underneath, with a pre-cooler bolted on

Hermeus's propulsion architecture is a hybrid. It starts with a Pratt & Whitney F100 — the engine that has powered the F-16 and the F-15 — with a pre-cooler placed in front of it. Around Mach 3, the aircraft switches to a ramjet.

Piplica explains the physics plainly. As speed and altitude rise, gas turbine performance "drops off a cliff" until the engine cannot generate enough thrust to accelerate. The pre-cooler chills incoming air so the turbine can keep working at higher speed and altitude. Once a ramjet can run efficiently near Mach 3, the aircraft switches over for high-speed cruise.

The deliberate choice was not to build a clean-sheet gas turbine. That decision became "a really important piece of the customer story," Piplica said. Strip the skin off the aircraft and "it's effectively an F-16 underneath," sharing supply chains, maintenance and repair infrastructure, logistics and training with the Air Force, the Navy and foreign air forces. Building an engine from scratch is expensive and slow, and getting a customer to trust a new engine takes a long time — though Piplica said Hermeus is open to it eventually "if it makes sense."

The airframe is about the size of an F-16 and carries seven hard points, according to Shore. The ramjet is built in-house; the turbine is bought. That arrangement deepened in September 2026, when Pratt & Whitney granted Hermeus a production license to build the F100-PW-229 itself — creating a new qualified production source for an engine that has powered U.S. fighters for more than five decades.

Why the military wants an unmanned bomb truck

Shore's pitch is that Hermeus fills a gap between the MQ-9 Reaper, with about 3,000 pounds of external stores, and the F-15EX, a two-seat fighter that the Air Force's fiscal 2027 budget prices at more than $100 million apiece. He cites the Iran conflict to argue that fourth-generation aircraft still do the work: F-15s, F-16s and probably F-18s flew "85 to 90%" of sorties, according to Piplica. Fifth-generation platforms dominate the first days of a fight, but "the entire air wing engages," and fourth-gen aircraft keep flying — and keep being bought.

The argument has three parts. First, an unmanned supersonic aircraft with useful payload lets the military treat losses the way it treats an MQ-9 loss. "Instead of having the risk of, you know, two downed pilots in Iran, okay, we treat it like an MQ-9, move on to the next one," Shore said.

Second, unmanned platforms accelerate weapons integration. "One of the biggest choke points in the Department of Defense is integrating new weapons on unmanned platforms," Shore said. Bolting "an explodey thing on the wing" of a manned fighter creates certification bottlenecks; a high-payload unmanned aircraft can take far more risk on store separation and push a weapon from technology-readiness level 3 to 7 faster, then certify it on manned aircraft. "When you have a high payload unmanned aircraft I can take way more risk to accelerate integrating weapons."

Third, speed itself is a force multiplier. "If I am releasing a payload at Mach 2, Mach 3, I am the first stage before that engine lights, and I can double, even maybe triple the ranges of some standard munitions without having to add costs to them," Shore said.

Shore invokes General Wilsbach, the Air Force Chief of Staff, who has testified that he wants more F-15s because it is his "bomb truck" — the most flexible platform for carrying diverse payloads. Shore's counter: that capability is expensive, manned and not necessarily mass-producible. Why not build it unmanned?

"Nobody's really been conceptualizing unmanned high-speed aircraft of this scale before. So this is truly like breaking the paradigm," he said.

Outside the CCA program, inside the Pentagon's blind spot

The Air Force's Collaborative Combat Aircraft program is where the Pentagon's unmanned future is being written. Anduril and General Atomics are flying Increment 1 prototypes. Hermeus is "not a part of it," Shore said — "both and" when asked whether that leaves the company outside, a relationship he described as nuanced and sensitive.

He frames CCA aircraft such as Anduril's Fury and General Atomics' Gambit as near-wingmen to stealth fighters, while Hermeus is "more of an independent platform." He calls CCA "the beginning of the Air Force conceptualizing unmanned high-thrust platforms," run increment by increment, with Increment 2 and 3 to follow.

Hermeus also sits awkwardly in the military's classification system. Group 5, the Pentagon's category for large unmanned aircraft, starts at 1,320 pounds and is defined mainly by operating altitude — typically above 18,000 feet — with no upper weight limit. Hermeus's F-16-scale aircraft is heavier than the Increment 1 CCA designs that share the category.

In September 2026, Hermeus selected Anduril's Lattice for Mission Autonomy for the Mk 2 aircraft — Anduril's first commercial deal to put its autonomy software on a third-party Group 5 platform. The first autonomy test flight is planned for 2027.

The company's anchor partnership is with the Defense Innovation Unit, which Shore calls "the window right into the Pentagon for things that don't fit cleanly." He says Hermeus is "absolutely the biggest physical item they have worked on," certainly on the contract side. In May 2026, a $159 million modification took the DIU contract ceiling to $219 million — one of the largest awards in the unit's history — with the Air Force and Navy as partners on the flight-test campaign. But DIU work at this level requires some Air Force concurrence, and the Air Force is watching closely.

The structural problem Shore names is the chicken-and-egg of defense innovation: a company must build to a thesis before the customer states a requirement — the way Steve Jobs would not ask customers what they wanted. The Department of Defense historically works the other way. "The only thing that's really gonna guarantee a negative outcome is be PowerPoint deep," Shore said.

The real bottleneck is the test range, not the physics

The sharpest criticism in the interview targets the test and evaluation community.

Piplica explains why Spaceport America works: it is a commercial airfield with fully unrestricted airspace, which shrinks the safety analysis to "zero airplanes, zero risk" and the ground area is unpopulated. That let Hermeus get to flight far faster than otherwise. But the federal test range at White Sands Missile Range controls the flight-termination system and decides whether to terminate. The company gets a window — about two hours on the day of recording — and the ranges are, in Piplica's words, "underfunded for years," with old infrastructure and a workforce depleted by deferred retirement.

Shore is harsher. Test and evaluation is "one of the last bastions that has not really faced the innovation pressure," he said. The science and technology component of the Department of War historically does not work with startups, so defense-tech companies "exist in parallel to the AFRLs and the ONRs, and they compete, frankly, with them." Startups build prototypes on internal R&D and then try to sell them at a program office, which caps the complexity they can afford. Big, hard systems remain the incumbents' domain.

He notes that autonomy companies outside aviation avoid this. A boat can start on a lake; a ground vehicle needs only 1,000 acres. High-altitude, high-speed flight has no equivalent. "The S&T world often is more in competition with the defense tech community than it is in concert with them," Shore said, and DIU's existence is evidence of it.

Host Ashlee Vance raises the FAA's role in ceding the consumer drone market to DJI as a cautionary case. Piplica concedes the FAA has made strides in recent years, partly by reading congressional authorities that were "sitting there on somebody's desk" and building a safety case the agency believed.

You can't rebuild Skunk Works without a matching customer

Piplica's key historical point is that Kelly Johnson's Skunk Works depended as much on the customer as on the contractor. When aircraft like the SR-71 were built, the military program office was "like six people." Today's Skunk Works is not Johnson's because the customer changed.

Shore cites Johnson's own three requirements: a team operating that way, direct access to the acquisition decision maker, and direct access to the operational decision maker — in modern terms, the three of them on a text chain. "Interfaces are where risk resides," Shore said. The bureaucracy has been built on adding more interfaces, which obscures that clarity.

The same logic explains why Hermeus is moving its headquarters to El Segundo: talent. The team that designs, builds and tests these systems lives there. "You can't hire that team. That team doesn't exist. You have to recreate that team," Shore said.

He frames Hermeus's seven-and-a-half years to supersonic flight against SpaceX's roughly six and a half years to orbit — noting Hermeus did not start with $100 million.

The commercial jet is real, and it is gated by defense cash

Piplica confirms the founding ambition was always hypersonic flight, and that the defense business was there from the start but not discussed openly early on. The sequencing is explicit: "defense first, earn our right to enter the commercial piece later."

Shore's rationale is economic. "If you really want to go after sustainable commercial flight operations the economic hurdles you have ahead of you mean you need to create a stable cash flowing defense business first period," he said. Piplica adds that no major successful large aircraft manufacturer, especially on the military side, has been founded in the U.S. since the 1950s, because the capital required is beyond venture capital.

The commercial vision is an ocean-crosser, not a round-the-world aircraft. London to New York would take about 90 minutes. Piplica says fast aircraft struggle beyond roughly 5,000 miles because they get "incredibly big." Earlier framing put the range around 3,400 miles with about 20 passengers. Asked whether Vance will fly hypersonic in his lifetime, Shore says he wants "to be on the plane less, so I have a lot of motivation to make them faster."

On China, Shore argues the U.S. advantage is cultural, not volumetric. American doctrine pushes decision-making to the lowest level; top-down systems require permission. He concedes China can "brute force" problems with volume and that the outcome is uncertain, but says the American way is "the questioning of why, why not." He wants Hermeus's success to "mow the lawn" for other aviation startups rather than be the only one.

The same bottleneck is now visible in Tokyo. In March 2026 the Japan Ground Self-Defense Force fielded the Type 25 hyper-velocity gliding projectile — the country's first operational domestically developed boost-glide missile — at Camp Fuji, with future variants intended to reach 2,000 to 3,000 kilometers. The U.S. has supported the program and hosted test launches in California. Japan's version of the Hermeus question is whether a procurement system built around incremental upgrades can absorb weapons that arrive on a hardware timetable.

What to watch

The near-term test sequence is the real scorecard. Mk 2.2 flying with the pre-cooler, now with FAA authority for up to six supersonic sorties. Then Mk 2.3 demonstrating a turbine stable at ramjet transition speed around Mach 3 — which Shore says "is not a thing that has been done." Then the first ramjet flights. In September, Hermeus also unveiled Ramjet-X, an expendable ramjet vehicle designed to be released from Quarterhorse at high Mach, a cheaper way to test ramjet propulsion than building a new booster for each attempt. Each step either validates the hybrid architecture or exposes the gap between Hermeus's iteration thesis and the physics.

The unresolved tension is structural. Hermeus's speed depends on a permissive range and a small set of empowered customers, and neither is guaranteed as demand for test resources grows across the department. The company's bet is that delivering hardware converts skeptics faster than any argument about procurement reform.

For investors, the Hermeus story fits a pattern that has become the defining trade of the decade in defense technology. Anduril's valuation has climbed from $61 billion in May 2026 to a reported $90 billion in October, and the market has rewarded companies that pair Silicon Valley iteration with Pentagon contracts. Hermeus is earlier and smaller — it closed a $350 million Series C at a $1 billion valuation in April 2026, led by Khosla Ventures — and its cash-flowing product is still in flight test. But the logic is the same: the defense budget is the bridge that funds the ambitious technology, and the companies that build the bridge fastest get to decide what gets built on the other side. If Shore is right that the Pentagon has spent tens of billions on hypersonics and fielded almost nothing, the first company to actually fly a Mach 3-capable aircraft at scale will not just have a contract — it will have a template.

When AI Holds the Red Button, Deterrence Could Put Humanity at Risk

Handing nuclear retaliation to artificial intelligence might strengthen deterrence on paper, but it would remove the human judgment that has prevented catastrophe before.

Nuclear deterrence rests on a grim principle: no country should believe it can launch a devastating first strike and escape retaliation. Nuclear powers have built command structures intended to ensure that authority to respond survives even a catastrophic attack on their leadership.

But what if an entire chain of command were destroyed? Could 
artificial intelligence (AI)
 then serve as a “last resort” and automatically order retaliation? And would that make humanity safer—or bring us closer to catastrophe?

The question connects nuclear command and control to warnings from AI researchers and technology leaders. In 2023, Geoffrey Hinton, 
Sam Altman
, Demis Hassabis, 
Dario Amodei
, and others endorsed a 
statement
calling for the risk of extinction from AI to be treated as a global priority, alongside nuclear war and pandemics. In September 2026, Altman and Amodei 
briefed
 the United Nations (UN) Security Council on advanced AI and its risks. These warnings do not establish that AI will destroy humanity. They do underscore the stakes of developing powerful systems without adequate safeguards.

The nuclear command problem shows how catastrophe could arise without a conscious or malevolent AI machine. The danger may come from humans choosing to delegate too much.

Why Automatic Nuclear Retaliation Is Tempting

On September 8, 2022, 
North Korea
 promulgated a 
law
 on its nuclear forces. Article 3 says that if hostile forces put the command-and-control system over the country’s nuclear forces in danger, a nuclear strike would be launched automatically and immediately according to a preplanned operation. The provision does not say that an AI system would make the decision. But it raises a stark question: might 
Kim Jong-un
 someday delegate that role to one?

An AI system could, in principle, monitor warning data, assess whether an attack had occurred, and determine whether decision-makers remained able to respond. If no one could issue an order, the system might initiate retaliation automatically.

The strategic appeal is clear. If an adversary knew that killing a country’s leadership would not prevent a devastating response, it might be less inclined to launch a decapitation strike.

The idea has a Cold War precedent. The 
Soviet Union
 developed the Perimeter system, widely known in the West as the “
Dead Hand
,” to preserve the possibility of retaliation under extreme circumstances. Modern AI might add analytical capabilities, but it would not resolve the underlying dilemma.

To make retaliation credible, a system must be able to act when human authorities are unavailable. Yet that same autonomy removes the chance for human intervention when the stakes are highest.

How a Technical Error Could Become a Nuclear Catastrophe

A machine cannot be assumed to distinguish reliably between a genuine nuclear attack and every alternative explanation for the information it receives.

Communications might fail during a cyberattack. Warning sensors might produce false indications. A conventional conflict could disrupt command networks. A surviving leader might be unable to communicate but still capable of preventing escalation.

The record shows why judgment under uncertainty matters. In 1983, Soviet officer 
Stanislav Petrov
 judged that satellite warnings of incoming US missiles were false. His decision helped keep a warning incident from escalating into a global nuclear war. An automated system designed to overcome hesitation might not show the same caution.

AI need not malfunction in a dramatic or mysterious way to create danger. It could follow its rules exactly, while those rules prove inadequate for an unforeseen situation. A system designed to guarantee retaliation could therefore carry out its mission successfully—and trigger a nuclear exchange that no surviving human authority would have approved.

How AI Could Endanger Humanity Without Turning on Us

Public discussion focuses mainly on whether increasingly capable AI systems might become difficult to control. Another danger arises when people surrender control because automation seems faster, more reliable, or strategically advantageous.

A government might reason that automatic retaliation strengthens deterrence. Its adversary might respond by accelerating its own decisions and relying more heavily on automated warning systems. Each side could feel pressure to act faster, leaving less time to examine ambiguous signals or correct mistakes.

Humanity, in this scenario, would not necessarily be destroyed by an AI that turned against its creators. It could be endangered by governments using AI to protect themselves from one another.

The question is not only whether machines can be trusted. It is whether institutions can safely delegate decisions with irreversible, potentially existential consequences.

How to Preserve Nuclear Deterrence Without Surrendering Control

The answer is not to exclude AI from nuclear command systems altogether. AI could help protect communications, identify technical anomalies, assess warning information, and make command structures more resilient.

But the authority to initiate nuclear use must remain under meaningful human control. Decisionmakers must be able to examine the evidence, question a system’s conclusions, and prevent an erroneous launch. A nominal human presence is not enough.

Such safeguards would not eliminate every risk or solve the problem of ensuring retaliation after a country’s leadership is destroyed. But that unresolved problem is preferable to assuming an algorithm can safely settle it.

The purpose of nuclear deterrence is to prevent nuclear war, not to guarantee that nuclear weapons will be launched in every conceivable circumstance.

There is a profound irony in developing increasingly powerful AI partly because we believe machines can overcome the limits of human judgment. When the decision could destroy civilization, those limits include something essential: the ability to hesitate, reconsider and recognize that the rules may be wrong.

Humanity should not respond to the fear of losing control over AI by giving AI control over the decision that could end humanity.

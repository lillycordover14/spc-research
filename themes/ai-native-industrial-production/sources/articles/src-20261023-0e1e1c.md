A growing trend in the 
UNC College of Arts and Sciences
 echoes the improv rule: “Yes, and …”
The percentage of students who decide to double-major at UNC-Chapel Hill has grown from 24% to 31% in the past decade. Almost half of undergraduates majoring in arts and humanities or social sciences add a second major.
“There is space in their Carolina educational journey to explore and learn about many disciplines they are passionate about and that might lead them to exciting career paths,” said 
Jim White
, Craver Family Dean of the College of Arts and Sciences. “We hope they pursue what interests them most — what lights them up — because we know that will lead them to a richer life, both throughout their careers and in their personal lives.”
They want to pursue multiple interests and learn more skills across disciplines.
“We have been encouraging students to explore,” said 
Meredith Petschauer
, associate dean of undergraduate curricula and a teaching professor in 
exercise and sport science
. “When you make yourself more well-rounded, you make yourself more interesting. You never know what you will discover.”
Senior Kate Blizzard is one of those students pursuing two majors instead of one. When she started at UNC-Chapel Hill, she decided to connect with her Brazilian family by majoring in Romance languages and learning Portuguese.
Later she discovered a passion for ecology and the environment and picked up a second major in environmental science. Carolina’s field site programs, run by the 
UNC Institute for the Environment
 and designed to provide students with hands-on experience, seemed like a great way to explore her newfound interest.
Blizzard chose the Morehead City Field Site at the 
UNC Institute of Marine Sciences
 for its focus on marine biology and ecology. She packed her bags for a full semester living, learning and researching on the North Carolina coast.
The experience was so transformative that she added a marine science minor to her courseload.
“You get an introduction to research in your biology and chemistry courses, but you don’t learn what research is like until you’re the one in charge,” Blizzard said. “Real research has so many twists and turns, and you have to push through them and keep yourself accountable.”
Her field site project built upon previous research in the
 lab of Janet Nye
, a professor of Earth, marine and environmental sciences and interim IMS director. Blizzard sought to determine the thermal range — the range of temperatures an animal can tolerate — of pigfish living off the North Carolina coast, which can help predict how climate change might affect them.
Blizzard is using the project for her 
Honors Carolina
 thesis. She received funding through the inaugural Roberts-Watson Family Research Award to return to IMS for the summer and expand her project.
The research is one less thing she has to fit into a busy senior year. Fortunately, Blizzard enjoys the balance in her coursework.
“I love being able to go from a computer science class to a Romance language class where I’m learning about Brazilian literature,” she said.
Blizzard also appreciates the exposure to diverse perspectives and different aspects of the world. And, if the opportunity arises, she’d love to leverage both of her passions doing marine science research in Brazil.
But for now, being able to converse with her mother in Portuguese — excitedly showing her the new vocabulary and grammar she’s learned — is its own reward for her hard work.

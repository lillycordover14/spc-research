The market is much more concentrated than the number of startups suggests. Across 97 companies, roughly half of all cumulative funding sits with the top five, and around 80% sits with the top 20. The biggest checks are increasingly paying for industrial capacity, not just product development. Shipyards, automated factories, missile lines, satellite production and inventory are becoming core uses of venture and growth capital. That helps explain why hardware-heavy companies dominate the ranking. Autonomous ships, aircraft, missiles, satellites and manufacturing platforms simply require more capital than military software businesses with lighter infrastructure needs. Europe has clearly raised its ceiling. Helsing, Quantum Systems and ICEYE show that European defense companies can now reach billion-dollar funding totals, even though the U.S. still has a much deeper bench of heavily funded startups. Recent round headlines also need more scrutiny than they used to. Several transactions mix primary equity, secondary sales, preferred equity, debt or credit facilities, so the headline financing number can be materially larger than the amount of fresh operating capital entering the company. Space has become one of the deepest funding clusters inside defense tech. ICEYE, True Anomaly, K2 Space, HawkEye 360, Apex and a wider group of satellite, sensing and space-security companies now form something close to a full stack. The investor base has widened alongside the round sizes. Traditional venture firms are still central, but JPMorganChase, Blackstone, Carlyle, Goldman Sachs Alternatives, Fidelity, Wellington and other large institutions now appear in deals that would once have been too large for normal VC syndicates. Young companies are scaling unusually fast. Saronic, True Anomaly, CHAOS Industries and others have reached several hundred million dollars or more in funding within only a few years, especially when investors believe manufacturing capacity needs to be built early. The ranking is best read as a map of financial firepower, not product quality. It shows which startups have attracted the most outside equity capital and where investors are underwriting industrial scale, but revenue, contracts, production output and customer adoption still need separate analysis. Which defense tech startups have raised the most money? Anduril is by far the best-funded defense tech startup today, with roughly $11.3 billion raised, while Helsing, Shield AI and Saronic form a much smaller second tier between about $2.6 billion and $3.3 billion. The gap gets large very quickly. Hadrian has raised around $1.8 billion, Quantum Systems about $1.7 billion, and Castelion and ICEYE roughly $1.3 billion each. True Anomaly, Mach Industries and CHAOS Industries have also crossed the $1 billion line. That gives us 11 companies with at least $1 billion in cumulative funding in the dataset. A few years ago, reaching $1 billion would have made a defense startup an extreme outlier. Today, it gets a company into the upper tier without putting it particularly close to Anduril. The ranking also shows where investors are putting the biggest checks. The top positions are dominated by autonomous weapons, drones, ships, satellites, missiles, radar and manufacturing infrastructure. These are businesses where scaling usually requires factories, inventory, testing facilities and physical production capacity. Anduril's funding lead is huge: the company has raised about three times as much as Helsing and almost four times as much as Shield AI. The latest jump came from a $5 billion Series H led by Thrive Capital and Andreessen Horowitz. That one round was larger than the entire funding history of every other company in our ranking. Anduril was valued at $61 billion in the transaction after being valued at $30.5 billion less than a year earlier. The money is arriving alongside much larger operations. Reuters reported that Anduril said revenue had more than doubled to $2.2 billion in 2025, while the workforce had nearly doubled. The company is also investing heavily in manufacturing and has continued to win programs covering missiles, autonomous aircraft, command-and-control software, maritime systems and other defense products. So Anduril's funding lead now comes with a level of commercial and industrial scale that most younger defense startups have not reached. The gap remains large even after the wave of billion-dollar rounds elsewhere in the market. Is Helsing now Europe's best-funded defense tech startup? Yes. Helsing is currently Europe's clearest defense tech fundraising heavyweight, with roughly $3.3 billion raised and a latest $1.8 billion Series E that valued the company at $18 billion. That financing included Dragoneer, Lightspeed, ICONIQ, Goldman Sachs Alternatives, JPMorganChase, CPP Investments, General Catalyst and other investors. Demand exceeded the available allocation, according to Helsing. The size of the round shows how much the European market has changed. Helsing raised more in one financing than Quantum Systems had raised across its entire history before its own latest mega-round. The $1.8 billion raise is also larger than the total funding accumulated by ICEYE, STARK, TEKEVER or almost any other European defense technology company. Helsing has also expanded well beyond its original image as a defense AI software company. Its current portfolio covers strike drones, autonomous aircraft, underwater systems, electronic warfare and manufacturing infrastructure. Europe now has several companies able to raise U.S.-style growth rounds, but Helsing is still comfortably ahead of the rest. Prefer verified numbers to headlines? Shield AI remains firmly in the top tier, with roughly $3 billion raised and one of the largest recent financing packages in defense technology. The company's Series G brought in $1.5 billion of equity at a $12.7 billion post-money valuation. Shield AI also arranged $500 million of fixed-return preferred equity connected with its acquisition of simulation company Aechelon Technology, plus an additional delayed-draw facility. That distinction is worth keeping clear. The headline transaction was described as a $2 billion strategic financing package, while the conventional Series G equity component was $1.5 billion. The financing came from investors including Advent International, JPMorganChase, Snowpoint Ventures and others, with Blackstone supplying the preferred-equity component. Shield AI now sits behind Anduril and Helsing in cumulative funding, but a $1.5 billion equity round still puts the company among a very small group capable of raising more than $1 billion at once. How did Saronic raise so much money so quickly? Saronic has become one of the most heavily funded defense startups in only a few years, reaching roughly $2.6 billion largely because investors are financing its autonomous shipbuilding capacity as aggressively as its technology. The company closed a $1.75 billion Series D at a $9.25 billion valuation. That followed a $600 million Series C at a $4 billion valuation and an earlier $175 million Series B. Saronic says the latest capital will help expand Port Alpha, its planned next-generation shipyard, as well as existing production capacity in Texas and Louisiana. The company is developing autonomous vessels ranging from smaller Corsair craft to the 180-foot Marauder. This is why Saronic has climbed the funding ranking so quickly. Building ships at meaningful volume consumes far more capital than developing a normal software product, and investors are financing the manufacturing system alongside the vessels themselves. The company's $1.75 billion Series D alone exceeded the lifetime funding of almost every defense startup outside the top five. Are defense tech funding rounds getting much bigger? Yes. Defense tech rounds have moved sharply upward, and billion-dollar financings are now recurring often enough that they can no longer be dismissed as one exceptional Anduril deal. Anduril raised $5 billion. Helsing raised $1.8 billion. Saronic raised $1.75 billion. Shield AI raised $1.5 billion in Series G equity. Hadrian raised $1.37 billion. Quantum Systems raised $1.2 billion. Castelion raised $800 million of equity inside a larger financing package. The broader market data points the same way. Defense News, citing PitchBook, reported that defense-tech venture deal value reached $49.1 billion in 2025 under PitchBook's broad definition, up from $27.2 billion the year before. CB Insights, using a narrower methodology, put defense-tech equity funding at $17.9 billion, up from $7.3 billion. Different research firms draw the category differently, so those market totals should not be treated as interchangeable. Both show the same acceleration. PitchBook also reported that the median defense-tech deal size doubled from $10 million in 2024 to $20 million in 2025. At the top of the market, the change has been much more dramatic. Anduril's $5 billion Series H is currently in a class of its own, but at least five other companies have recently raised more than $1 billion in a single equity financing. Helsing, Saronic, Shield AI, Hadrian and Quantum Systems all cleared that mark. Castelion followed with $800 million of equity, while True Anomaly raised $650 million in a Series D that included a smaller debt component. ICEYE is another case where the headline needs to be read carefully. The company announced a Series F of more than €1 billion when secondary transactions were included, but the actual primary funding into the company was €450 million, or about $520 million. Those details matter in a funding ranking. A secondary transaction lets existing shareholders sell shares and does not give the startup the same amount of new operating capital. Defense manufacturing startups are raising much more money because production capacity has become one of the market's biggest bottlenecks. Hadrian makes that visible. The company raised $1.37 billion of Series D equity at a $7.87 billion valuation to expand its network of highly automated factories. The financing followed a $260 million Series C and came as Hadrian pushed into much larger defense and industrial manufacturing programs. Hadrian is not alone. Saronic is financing shipyards. Castelion is scaling missile production. Mach Industries is expanding manufacturing for autonomous systems, propulsion and weapons. Neros is increasing drone production. Firestorm is building systems intended to make drone manufacturing more deployable and flexible. PitchBook found that defense-related manufacturing investment rose to $4.7 billion across 39 deals in 2025 from $2.6 billion across 24 deals a year earlier. The customer-side logic is pretty simple. A military can test an impressive prototype quickly; getting thousands of units on time is much harder. Investors are increasingly financing the factories needed to solve that second problem. Is space defense becoming one of the biggest defense tech categories? Space has quietly become one of the deepest funding clusters in defense tech, with several companies approaching or exceeding $1 billion in capital. ICEYE has raised roughly $1.3 billion. True Anomaly has surpassed $1 billion in total capital. K2 Space sits close to $1 billion, while HawkEye 360 and Apex are also near the top of the broader ranking. Further down, CesiumAstro, Loft Orbital, Capella Space, Albedo, Umbra, Unseenlabs, LeoLabs, Turion Space, Aalyria, Digantara and Scout Space add another large group of funded companies. These businesses do very different things. ICEYE sells synthetic-aperture radar intelligence. HawkEye 360 tracks radio-frequency emissions. True Anomaly builds spacecraft for space security. K2 and Apex make satellite platforms. LeoLabs tracks objects in orbit. CesiumAstro develops phased-array communications. Taken together, space defense now looks much more like a complete technology stack than a small niche inside the wider defense market. Are missile and counter-drone startups getting serious funding? Missiles and counter-drone systems are now attracting hundreds of millions of dollars per company, and Castelion has pushed the category into billion-dollar territory. Castelion's Series C included $800 million of equity and a $250 million committed revolving credit facility. The company said the financing would accelerate production of Blackbeard, its lower-cost hypersonic strike missile, and valued Castelion at $13 billion. Cambridge Aerospace has raised roughly $636 million for lower-cost interceptors. Epirus has accumulated more than $500 million around high-power microwave counter-drone systems. Allen Control Systems has reached roughly $242 million, while Hidden Level, Fortem, Aurelius Systems and D-Fend are pursuing radar, kinetic, laser, autonomous and cyber-based approaches. The interesting part is the variety of technologies being financed. Investors have not settled on one universal answer to the drone threat, so capital is flowing into several competing layers of detection and interception. Defense software startups generally raise less capital than missile, satellite or autonomous-vehicle companies, although the strongest software businesses can still reach several hundred million dollars. Onebrief is one example. Its $200 million Series D included both primary and secondary shares and valued the military planning software company above $2 billion. Rebellion Defense has raised roughly $224 million, while Defense Unicorns, Govini and Second Front Systems have each raised well above $100 million. Vannevar Labs, TurbineOne, Yurts, Reveal Technology, Rune Technologies and Integrate occupy the next layer. Those figures look small beside a $1.75 billion Saronic round, but the businesses have very different cash requirements. Software companies do not normally need shipyards, rocket-motor lines, aircraft factories or satellite constellations. Funding rankings are useful for measuring financial resources. They are much less useful if someone tries to turn them directly into a ranking of product quality or commercial success. Is Europe really catching up with the US in defense tech funding? Europe is producing genuine defense tech heavyweights now, but the United States still has a much deeper bench of heavily funded startups. Helsing has reached roughly $3.3 billion, Quantum Systems around $1.7 billion and ICEYE about $1.3 billion. STARK, Cambridge Aerospace and TEKEVER have also moved into the hundreds of millions. The U.S. still dominates the wider top 20. Anduril, Shield AI, Saronic, Hadrian, Castelion, True Anomaly, Mach Industries, CHAOS Industries, K2 Space and Skydio are all American. Market-wide data shows the same gap. CB Insights data reported by Defense News put U.S. defense-tech equity funding at $14.2 billion in 2025, compared with $2.48 billion in Europe. European funding was growing, but the U.S. received almost six times more capital under that methodology. What has changed is Europe's ceiling. Raising $1 billion or more in a defense-tech round is now possible for a European company, which was much harder to imagine only a few years ago. Does raising more money mean a defense tech startup is more successful? No. Raising more money tells us how much equity capital investors have been willing to put behind a defense startup; it does not tell us by itself which company has built the best business. The problem becomes obvious when we compare categories. A company building autonomous ships needs facilities, vessels, inventory and heavy manufacturing equipment. A satellite company pays for spacecraft and launches. A missile company needs testing and production infrastructure. A military software company can often grow with much less capital. Skydio gives us a useful example from the other direction. The company raised a relatively modest $110 million Series F and said the small size of the round reflected falling capital needs as its core business generated more cash internally. The company valued itself at $4.4 billion in that financing. So a smaller round can sometimes reflect a healthier funding position rather than weaker investor demand. We use cumulative equity funding to answer a specific question: which startups have attracted the most outside equity capital? Revenue, contracts, margins, production output and customer adoption need separate analysis. Are defense tech valuations rising as fast as the funding rounds? Yes. Valuations for several leading defense tech startups have jumped almost as dramatically as the amounts being raised. Anduril reached a $61 billion valuation in its latest financing, roughly double the $30.5 billion mark from its previous round. Helsing's Series E valued the company at $18 billion. Castelion reached $13 billion. Shield AI reached $12.7 billion. Saronic was valued at $9.25 billion. Quantum Systems' $1.2 billion Series D valued it at around $8 billion, while Hadrian's Series D valued the company at $7.87 billion. K2 Space reached $6.8 billion and Skydio $4.4 billion. Several of these companies have repriced in a matter of months rather than over many years. That creates a much harder test for the next stage of the market. Investors are already valuing the leaders as future large defense suppliers, so continued valuation growth will increasingly depend on contracts, production and revenue catching up with those expectations. Which defense tech startups have raised big rounds lately? Defense tech has produced a dense run of large financings lately, with Mach Industries, Hadrian, Neros, Castelion, Helsing, Quantum Systems and others all adding major new rounds. Mach Industries announced another $600 million for its Series C in September, bringing its valuation to $3.7 billion. Hadrian raised $1.37 billion in Series D equity. Neros raised $250 million at a $2.5 billion valuation. Castelion raised $800 million of equity. Earlier in the year, Helsing added $1.8 billion, Quantum Systems raised $1.2 billion, True Anomaly raised a $650 million financing package and ICEYE added $520 million of primary Series F capital. K2 Space also raised $500 million at a $6.8 billion valuation. The frequency is the unusual part. Large rounds are appearing across drones, satellites, missiles, factories, autonomous vessels and military AI rather than clustering around one hot product category. Are young defense startups raising money much faster than before? Some of today's defense startups are reaching several hundred million dollars or even $1 billion in funding only a few years after being founded. True Anomaly says it surpassed $1 billion in total capital four years after its 2022 founding. CHAOS Industries said it reached $1 billion in funding only three years after it was founded. Saronic moved from a $55 million Series A to a $1.75 billion Series D within a remarkably short period. Neros is another example. Its latest $250 million Series C valued the company at $2.5 billion and brought its reconstructed cumulative funding to around $370 million. This pace would have looked extraordinary in the earlier defense venture market. Today, investors are willing to fund production capacity much earlier when they believe a company has found a military capability with large procurement potential. The result is a faster separation between the startups that can access late-stage capital and the much larger group still raising conventional Series A and Series B rounds. Is most defense tech money going to late-stage startups? Most of the dollars at the top of the defense tech market are going to later-stage companies, even though plenty of new startups continue to raise Seed and Series A rounds. Anduril's latest financing was a Series H. Helsing reached Series E. Shield AI reached Series G. ICEYE raised a Series F. Saronic, Hadrian, Quantum Systems, True Anomaly and CHAOS Industries have all reached Series D. The early-stage part of the market looks very different. Distributed Spectrum raised $25 million in Series A funding. Aurelius Systems raised $40 million. AndrenaM raised $18 million. Scout Space raised $18 million. Integrate raised $17 million. PitchBook has described the defense-tech market as increasingly barbelled, with capital concentrating around very early companies and a group of large scale-up businesses. Our ranking shows the second side of that pattern very clearly: once a company breaks into the top tier, individual rounds can become hundreds of millions or billions of dollars. Are defense tech startups finally reaching the stock market? A public-market exit path is starting to appear, but private funding still dominates the biggest defense tech companies. HawkEye 360 became the clearest recent example. The space-based signals-intelligence company sold 16 million shares at $26 in its IPO, raising $416 million. Reuters reported that the offering initially valued the company at roughly $2.42 billion, while its shares rose sharply on the first trading day. Other companies in the wider dataset have reached public markets through IPOs or business combinations, while a few have been acquired. Yet Anduril, Helsing, Shield AI, Saronic, Hadrian, Quantum Systems and most of the other funding leaders remain private. Private capital is currently deep enough to let a defense company raise $1 billion or even $5 billion without listing. That gives the biggest startups more freedom over when, or whether, they need public markets. Are big defense companies buying these startups, or are the startups staying independent? The best-funded defense startups are currently being financed to stay independent for much longer than the old "build and sell to a prime" model would suggest. There have been acquisitions. Capella Space, D-Fend Solutions and PredaSAR are examples in our dataset. Startups also buy other startups: Shield AI acquired Aechelon, while Anduril has repeatedly used acquisitions to expand its technology portfolio. But the biggest capital flows are going into independent companies that are building their own production systems and competing directly for government programs. Saronic is building shipyards. Anduril is building large-scale manufacturing infrastructure. Hadrian is building automated factories. Castelion is scaling missile production. Quantum Systems is expanding industrial capacity across autonomous systems. Investors are effectively giving these companies the balance sheets needed to grow into much larger defense suppliers before considering an exit. What does the defense tech funding ranking really tell us about the market today? Defense tech funding has clearly entered a new phase: the market now contains a small group of startups with multibillion-dollar balance sheets and the capital to build factories, ships, aircraft, missiles, satellites and autonomous systems at serious scale. Our dataset puts cumulative funding across 97 companies at roughly $44.7 billion, with close to half concentrated in the top five and around 80% concentrated in the top 20. Anduril remains the extreme case at roughly $11.3 billion. Helsing, Shield AI and Saronic form the next tier. Hadrian and Quantum Systems have already moved well beyond $1 billion, while Castelion, ICEYE, True Anomaly, Mach Industries and CHAOS Industries complete a growing group of billion-dollar-funded challengers. The strongest current pattern is the move from financing product development to financing industrial scale. Recent money is paying for factories, shipyards, inventory, acquisitions, satellite production, missile production and manufacturing capacity. That changes what the funding ranking means. Investors are increasingly making very large bets that some of these startups can become durable defense suppliers rather than small technology vendors. For now, Anduril stands far above the rest in total funding, while the number of credible challengers beneath it is growing quickly. The bigger unresolved question is no longer whether venture-backed defense companies can raise enough capital. Several clearly can. What we still need to see is how many can turn those billions into sustained production, major procurement programs and businesses large enough to justify the valuations investors are already paying. Our methodology This analysis ranks defense tech startups by cumulative equity funding raised and uses that ranking to examine how capital is concentrating across the defense tech market. The underlying page is New Market Pitch's defense tech market page. We reconstructed fundraising histories round by round wherever the public record allowed it, using company and investor announcements, regulatory filings where relevant, official press releases, reputable financial and defense-industry reporting, venture databases and other credible sources. The ranking focuses on equity capital. Conventional debt, credit facilities, grants and other non-equity financing are excluded from cumulative equity totals, and we separate primary capital from secondary share sales when the transaction structure is disclosed. That distinction is especially important in large recent financings. ICEYE's Series F included secondary transactions, Castelion paired equity with a revolving credit facility, Shield AI used multiple financing components, and True Anomaly's Series D package included a smaller debt component. When sources disagreed, we gave greater weight to direct company or investor disclosures, regulatory documents and authoritative reporting. Older rounds still count because the ranking is cumulative, while recent financings were checked separately so prior totals were not left stale after a new raise. Where the public record remained incomplete, we kept the uncertainty visible with approximate or minimum figures rather than forcing a precise-looking total. The goal is a defensible round-by-round reconstruction, not a cleaner number that cannot be reconciled.

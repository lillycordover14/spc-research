Boston Dynamics workers oppose the company’s robot dog use by ICE&nbsp;&nbsp;The Boston Globe

[page text]
Until now, opposition to US Immigration and Customs Enforcement purchasing four Boston Dynamics robotic dogs has come mainly from advocacy groups. But now, hundreds of company employees are calling for the Waltham-based robotics manufacturer to stop the deal. 
Boston Dynamics is 
providing ICE with its Spot robots
 through a third-party Connecticut company, Radeco. The $1.3 million deal includes a modified version of the original robot, with military grade add-ons such as a robotic arm, thermal camera, and sensors for explosive materials. Radeco’s modified robot is nicknamed Cerberus, after the mythical three-headed dog that guards the gates of hell.
In a letter to top executives, the contents of which were shared with the Globe, employees said they are “uncomfortable” with the deal, and warned it could hurt the company’s reputation and workforce. 
“We cannot be proud of working at BD when that work is being used by a government agency that brutalizes civilians,” the letter, sent to executives in September, reads. 
A Boston Dynamics robot dog called "Spot" is seen during a technology conference. 
ANDREJ IVANOV/AFP via Getty Images
While the contract is not directly with Boston Dynamics, the company will be heavily involved in technical support. Employees wrote that the deal presents them as “working directly with ICE.”
“Many of us have gotten messages from friends and family, asking us in disbelief and horror if this news is true, and how we can live with ourselves if it is,” the letter reads. 
A Boston Dynamics spokesperson acknowledged that employees had sent the letter to senior executives. In a statement, the company said the robot’s purpose is to keep people safe, and that DHS must follow the company’s terms and conditions, which 
prohibit weaponization
 of the robots or using them for harm or intimidation. 
The spokesperson said half the roughly 300 letter signers did not use their names
 
and that they represent a minority of the company’s 
1,400 workers
. 
A spokesperson for the Department of Homeland Security told the Globe this week that the robot “provides law enforcement officers with an additional tool to safely assess potentially dangerous situations before placing personnel or the public at risk.” 
The employees’ letter says the risks for Boston Dynamics are “simply too great to continue with this deal.” 
“We fear we will lose teammates over this decision, and worry about the risk it creates to achieve our shared company goals,” it reads. 
The company’s former interim chief executive, Amanda McMaster, responded to the workers’ petition in an email, saying she is “standing behind the decision to move forward with this and other potential sales to government customers.”
The Globe also reviewed the text of that email.
McMaster cited the company’s anti-weaponization guidelines, and vowed to make sure the dog robot is not misused. 
“We believe this decision is in line with our purpose of ethically advancing intelligent machines in service of humanity,” she wrote, asking workers to “accept this decision, even if you do not agree with it.” 
The deal has drawn criticism from advocacy groups around the state. Barbara Katzenberg and other members of the De-Ice Action Network have staged protests outside of Boston Dynamics’ headquarters. 
Barry Chin/Globe Staff

In agreeing to let ICE have the modified robots, Katzenberg, 76, said Boston Dynamics is “hoping and believing that ICE will stand by their terms and conditions and ethical principles.” She added, “We believe that’s naive.”

    Column: Hollywood has an ‘iPhone face’ problem

    “An actor’s ability to imitate dark, complex emotions such as deep anger, grief and euphoria is what makes their talent feel solid and their character feel real to audiences.”

Taffi Rose
, 
Columnist
                 
             •  
            
October 21, 2026
 

 

Mirin Mercer
 

Cinema has always been a medium and outlet for creative expression, giving people entertainment and stories that have impacted and influenced generations. There is a reason that acting has become immortal in the light of so many artistic forms. 

An actor’s ability to imitate dark, complex emotions such as deep anger and grief is what makes their talent feel solid and their character feel real to audiences. Without their expressions, are they really acting, or are they simply reading a script? 

In Ti West’s “Pearl,” Mia Goth’s performance as the titular character is characterized by raw, unfiltered rage that consumes her entirely. It was so powerful and terrifying that anyone who watches the movie can say they, too, felt Pearl’s unrelenting determination to be a star. Through Goth’s iconic screams and the end of the movie — where we see Pearl’s painful smile as tears run down her face — we feel her anger, but most importantly, we can see it. Goth showed audiences that being able to imitate emotion is vital to acting. If she had an unmoving face, there would have been a huge disconnect between audiences and Pearl’s character.

Despite this, it seems that our culture has begun to desire a frozen face with a stiff forehead, hollowed out cheeks and a facelift. It’s the new trend of the beauty world, and in Hollywood, it’s infiltrating what we watch. With every new up-and-coming movie, I can’t seem to tear my gaze away from an actor who looks unreal ⁠— I immediately get sucked out of the atmosphere the movie is attempting to rock me into. 

New age movies are being impacted by actors who have an “iPhone face.” Having an “iPhone face” refers to actors who look too modern, like they’ve seen an iPhone. They appear sculpted and out of place in any type of period piece⁠ — in these cases, having noticeable Botox and lip filler. It is the phenomenon that makes a 1920s heroine look like she just stepped out of a dermatologist’s office. It makes it seem as though the actor is not playing a character, but is rather playing themselves after having been handed a script. 

An actor’s face is one of the foundations of performing with substance and weight. Without its complexity, the art simply feels forced and hollow.

Actress Kim Cattrall, known for her role in the “Sex and the City” franchise, has openly admitted to using Botox and seeks out other cosmetic enhancements. While she says that she doesn’t want to go too extreme with these procedures because she wants to maintain a semblance of herself, some still point out that she no longer has much expression in her face. 

In recent photos, John Travolta, the iconic actor who starred in “Pulp Fiction” as Vincent Vega, has been rumored to have had cosmetic procedures such as Botox that have smoothed out his forehead and overall aging face. Many of his fans claim that he is no longer expressive and that, because of these alleged procedures, it has led to him having a plastic look. While these are only rumors, the point is not just what an actor did or did not have done. It’s what audiences now see on screen.

Why does it seem that more and more actors feel the need to inject themselves with Botox, lip fillers, get veneers or have facelifts? The answer is the industry behind them. Hollywood punishes aging faces — especially women’s — and rewards the “camera-ready” look. 

Still, actors are beginning to look more alike after these procedures, and they begin dulling their own talent and uniqueness ⁠— paralyzing the story the movie is trying to tell and the character they’re playing. If anything, I can only imagine the best actors would be those with worn faces wrinkled by years of performing. 

Actors are meant to rely on their faces to tell a story that words cannot and audience members are then meant to lean in, searching for the character’s thoughts. Audience members are being robbed of seeing unique, natural faces on their screen. In a way, they can no longer feel connected to the character nor to the movie if they cannot find a semblance of themselves in the story.

If studios keep casting only faces that can’t move, audiences will stop searching them for meaning. When there is no longer any expression in our movies and everyone wears the same face, I wonder how this art will be perceived and consumed. Will acting hold any weight anymore if we cannot see the depth of the story actors are meant to breathe life into?

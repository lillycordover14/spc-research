Rubio Visit Passes Without F-414 Progress, Prolonging Uncertainty for Tejas Mk2 and AMCA Timelines&nbsp;&nbsp;Defence.in

[page text]
US Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s recent diplomatic trip to India concluded without any public progress on the stalled General Electric (GE) F-414 engine agreement.

The absence of a breakthrough during the October 20 meeting in Mumbai with External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar has deepened the uncertainty surrounding the production timelines of the Indian Air Force’s (IAF) future combat aircraft, namely the Tejas Mk2 and the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA).

While official readouts highlighted broad dialogues on trade, energy, and critical technologies—with US officials advocating for increased American defence sales to reduce New Delhi's reliance on Russian hardware—the crucial F-414 engine deal was missing from the public discourse.

Whether the topic was addressed behind closed doors remains unknown, but industry experts note that the public silence highlights a stark disconnect between strategic diplomatic partnerships and the harsh realities of commercial defence negotiations.

A Critical Void in IAF Capabilities​
The stakes for the IAF are exceptionally high. The air force is grappling with a concerning depletion of its combat fleet. While the sanctioned strength stands at 42 squadrons, the current operational force has dwindled to roughly 30 squadrons.

A timely induction of new fighters is essential to counter the growing twin-front security threat posed by the collusive military partnership between China and Pakistan.

The IAF relies heavily on the upgraded 4.5-generation Tejas Mk2 and the 5th-generation AMCA—which has a targeted first flight timeline of September 2028—to bridge this capability gap.

Both of these indigenous platforms are fundamentally dependent on the GE powerpack.

Three Years of Waiting​
The foundation for this deal was laid in June 2023, when GE and Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) signed a memorandum of understanding during Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s state visit to Washington.

The agreement proposed the joint production of GE’s 98-kilonewton F-414 engines in India. Despite the US Congress clearing the pathway in August 2023, a finalized technological and commercial contract has remained out of reach.

In September, HAL Chairman Ravi K. confirmed that while technical evaluations were complete, the company was still awaiting a formal commercial proposal from GE by October.

As the month draws to a close, the absence of this final agreement leaves India's most important fighter jet programmes severely delayed.

Complex Negotiations and Price Escalation​
The situation is further tangled by two parallel negotiations occurring simultaneously.

The first involves GE and HAL regarding the mass local manufacture of the engines.

The second is a separate dialogue between GE and the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO) for a limited batch of approximately 15 engines meant specifically for AMCA prototypes and the naval Twin Engine Deck Based Fighter (TEDBF).

According to industry reports from June, these AMCA-specific negotiations have hit a major roadblock due to severe price hikes.

Initial estimates placed the cost of each engine between Rs 70 and 80 crore, but the asking price has reportedly surged to over Rs 200 crore.

Additionally, GE is said to be seeking an $800 million upfront investment from India to establish a dedicated manufacturing facility.

Trapped in a 'Double Whammy'​
Defence insiders point out that India is virtually locked into using the American engine for its next-generation fighters.

Attempting to switch to a different engine at this advanced stage would require massive structural redesigns and lead to catastrophic timeline delays.

This lack of alternatives gives GE immense bargaining leverage over pricing, intellectual property rights, and the actual depth of technology transfer.

As a private corporation accountable to its shareholders, GE is under no strict obligation to offer favourable terms just to advance Washington’s diplomatic goals.

As one retired IAF veteran noted, India is facing a "double whammy"—forced to pay significantly more than budgeted while potentially failing to achieve the technological independence it initially sought.

Rubio’s visit was widely viewed as a prime opportunity to inject high-level political momentum into the stalled talks. However, that opportunity appears to have slipped by, leaving the IAF's vital fighter modernization plans hostage to prolonged corporate bargaining with no clear resolution in sight.

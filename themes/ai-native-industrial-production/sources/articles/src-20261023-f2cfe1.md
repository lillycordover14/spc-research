Yield ding on corn: Not all about tar spot
It isn’t exactly the harvest from hell, but there are unique challenges this year in getting corn in the bin — and lessons to learn.
Curt Arens
,
senior editor of Nebraska Farmer
,
Nebraska Farmer
October 23, 2026
schedule
6 Min Read
Tar spot exploded onto the scene across Nebraska cornfields late this summer, but it isn’t the only disease or challenge when it comes to leaning corn, dusty fields, and gray and black leaves this harvest season. 
Curt Arens
link
At a Glance
Tar spot exploded late in the growing season across Nebraska. 
Disease pressure in many parts of the state was significant, and it has caused a lot of leaning corn and dusty conditions. 
Yield reductions experienced by many producers may be due to disease and other weather-related factors. 
A cloud of dust. That sums up corn harvest in Nebraska.
Greg Greving farms with his sons, Shane and Jeremy, near Chapman, Neb. Last week, they had finished seed-corn harvest and were busy harvesting popcorn. Disease pressure, including tar spot, has hit the central Platte River Valley hard this season, causing popcorn and field corn to lean over and reducing yields across the board.
Greving is hearing field corn yields that are good, but they are still 20 to 30 bushels less than last season. He attributes lower yields and excessive dust to diseases such as tar spot and a challenging growing season.
“In over 50 years, I’ve never seen the combines so dirty after working the fields,” Greving said. “Normally, we would change the engine air filter once or twice per harvest, but this year, we’ve been changing it two or three times a day.”
On Oct. 19, USDA’s National Agricultural Statistics Service reported corn harvest at 37% complete in Nebraska, jumping 10% over the previous week. The five-year average is 48% harvested.

, up to 188 bushels per acre. Projections have jumped, but farmers in many parts of Nebraska, like Greving, are experiencing plenty of yield and harvest challenges.
Dreaded tar spot
It’s difficult to argue about the presence of tar spot. Last year, it showed up early, so that’s what producers were expecting, said farmer Brandon Christiansen of Plainview, Neb.
“This year it came on later, and then at the end of the season, it just blew up,” he said.
But not all problems in every field can be attributed to tar spot alone. There were plenty of troubles.
Nebraska Extension plant pathologist Tamra Jackson-Ziems spent part of her summer scouting fields with colleagues for ScoutNebraska, a new social media platform that hosts live, in-field scouting reports every week, designed for farmers, crop consultants and ag professionals to stay up to date on crops. So, she has seen it all, in every part of the state.
“There is so much variation this year, field to field,” Jackson-Ziems explained.
In addition to tar spot, southern rust and other diseases, higher-than-normal nighttime temperatures may have played a role in reduced corn yields.
“We’ve also had some intense heat and warm night temperatures early in the summer, followed by an enormous amount of cloud cover and high relative humidity over the past few weeks,” Jackson-Ziems said. “While farmers may be alarmed at the amount of gray to black fungi on corn leaves, some of that is disease pressure, but also the beneficial fungi breaking down the residue, increased by moisture we had at the end of the growing season. So, yield differences may be attributed to a combination of factors.”

There are other alarming reports. “Some reports have come in about corn kernels germinating inside the husk,” Jackson-Ziems said. “Some husks are tighter than others, so moisture causes small green shoots.”
Other stalk and grain quality issues include ear rot diseases, stalk rot and crown rot. These, along with a multitude of diseases, can compromise the integrity of the stalk. That means the plants may have shut down early, and stalks may not withstand excessive wind without lodging.
“If that’s the case, farmers will want to prioritize those fields for harvesting earlier where standability might be an issue,” Jackson-Ziems said.
Fungicide treatment timing, method and coverage made a difference. Plus, for the first time, Jackson-Ziems observed tar spot in southwest Nebraska. It wasn’t on the radar for farmers in that region because of the arid nature of the weather.
But she believes that it will now continue to be an issue, just as it has been expanding from eastern Corn Belt states into Nebraska. It is now working west and north to other parts of the state.

“It is probably only a matter of time when it will be confirmed in eastern Colorado, too,” Jackson-Ziems added.
Tough season
“We had a lot of wind this season early on, but we were lucky in that it hit us when the corn was not yet at a stage where there would be green snap,” Greving explained.
He said that farmers are happy they have pickup reels on their corn heads because it is a necessary tool.
Popcorn fields that are leaning are under considerable scrutiny, testing for quality issues. Because fields seemed to mature rapidly and dry down so fast, less standability and dust have been the result, Greving added.
For Christiansen, it was late wind events that leaned corn over. “It seems that the best-yielding corn leaned over, and the poorer corn withstood the winds,” he observed.
This year, he applied fungicide two times during the growing season, but a little later than in 2025. He utilized what are considered the best and most expensive fungicides.
“We can learn a lot from farmers in the East who have been dealing with this kind of disease pressure longer than we have,” Christiansen said. “You can really see which genetics stand up better.”
He recently ordered pickup attachments for his corn header to help handle the leaning corn in his fields.
For Christiansen, the experience offers simple takeaways. “High-yield farmers in the East are applying fungicide several times during the growing season, and that is our future,” he said. “But if that’s the case, we don’t need to use the most expensive fungicide on the market. We can get by with less expensive options.
“This year, after harvesting my irrigated corn, the fields where we had applied fungicide yielded better than check strips, but not that much better.”
Christiansen is convinced that Mother Nature had the final say.
“The crop came up uneven in many places,” he said. “So, we were already limited at that point. We had a crazy weather spurt this season, where we were so dry early and then had a month that was sopping wet. And if we are irrigating, the pivot creates the environment for all the disease pressure, because we are keeping the fields wet 24/7. We are going to have disease.”
According to Christiansen, the best thing farmers can do is get back to the basics — keeping the crop healthy while thinking all season long about the return on investment for every input they apply.
Healthy crop
“Soil fertility and keeping the crop healthy all season are the keys to high yields,” Christiansen said. “That means applying fungicide when we need to and making sure we have the right genetics in the field.”
While tar spot is the big enemy now, Christiansen noted that in the past, gray leaf spot and other diseases have been a problem. One day, tar spot will not be an issue because corn genetics research will catch up, and farmers will figure out how to effectively manage it.
“But there will be other problems, diseases and insects that are unforeseen now, and they will cause us just as much trouble down the road,” he added.

Op-Ed: Nicaragua mining – the golden bridgehead threatening US national security

Nicholas Vafeas
 | October 23, 2026 | 12:09 pm                

News
 
Mexico and Central America
 
Gold

(The opinions expressed here are those of the author, Nicholas Vafeas)

In the wake of 
Daniel Ortega’s July declaration
, formalizing the end of competitive elections, Western commentary focused almost entirely on the death of Nicaraguan democracy. From one geopolitical perspective, this is catastrophic. From another, it creates something of an oasis of mineral opportunity. But from a national defense perspective, it represents something far more immediate: the formal establishment of a sovereign, state-backed resource bridgehead inside the Western Hemisphere. 

For much of the last century, the Western Hemisphere has operated under a geopolitical reality that placed the United States as the dominant power, and countries that strayed too far outside its political and economic orbit discovered fairly quickly that Washington has a long reach. China has spent the better part of two decades testing the boundaries of that reality, and Nicaragua presents a textbook case.  

The critical minerals debate has become obsessed with pure geology, but mineral security is not determined by geology alone. It is determined by who controls access to that geology, under what political conditions, and for how long. Managua’s new institutional framework completely flips that calculation on its head. By removing competitive elections from the equation, long-term political continuity (and foreign state access) suddenly becomes much easier to price.  

China has already accumulated an 
extraordinary mineral footprint
 in the country, and it’s not difficult to understand the attraction. Nicaragua sits on the highly prospective Central American volcanic arc, home to high-grade epithermal gold systems like the 50km La India district, with an estimated Net Present Value of $1.4 billion. (For perspective, Japan’s Hishikari mine, one of the world’s richest epithermal deposits, occupies only a few kilometres of strike).  

But it’s worth remembering why gold holds value. In national defense terms, it is far more than commercial bullion. Physical gold extracted within an isolated autocracy represents off-grid, sanctions-proof war liquidity. A direct financial mechanism to keep the Ortega regime afloat and insulated from Western economic pressure. Whats more, epithermal gold systems rarely produce gold in isolation. They carry critical defense co-metals like antimony, tellurium, and silver. All materials essential for infrared sensors, night-vision optics, and military munition fuses. 

Recognising this potential, by early 2026, Chinese state-aligned firms had reportedly been granted mining concessions covering more than one million hectares, equivalent to 
roughly 8.5% of Nicaragua’s territory
. The expansion has been remarkably rapid, with Chinese firms moving from a limited presence only a few years ago to becoming some of the largest holders of mineral rights in the country.  

To put it plainly, controlling 8.5% of a Central American state carries a massive physical, logistical, and intelligence footprint. Mining concessions bring heavy earth-moving equipment, surface survey rights, dual-use airstrips, heavy telecommunications infrastructure, and potential deepwater logistics flanking both the Caribbean and Pacific coasts. Positioned barely 1,000 miles from the U.S. border and sitting directly alongside the Panama Canal, this embeds a state-backed adversary presence firmly inside 
USSOUTHCOM’s
 primary Area of Responsibility. 

This is not a hypothetical Chinese resource strategy waiting to happen, but a lived reality. Political risk does not disappear, it changes ownership. In general terms, mining investors and private equity firms are typically wary of political instability, because elections introduce what financial models can never fully price: political discontinuity. Concessions agreed under one administration can suddenly find themselves under scrutiny by the next. 

Ortega’s decision changes that, effectively handing China a golden key. If the current political system remains intact, Chinese companies holding mineral concessions no longer need to consider the possibility of an opposition government coming to power in 2026, 2031, or beyond. 

Of course, that does not make Nicaragua politically low-risk. Quite the opposite actually, since it creates a highly asymmetric risk environment. For Western companies dependent on transparent institutions, independent courts, ESG mandates, and Western finance, the jurisdiction becomes unworkable. But for Chinese firms trading capital for regime survival, policy continuity becomes unusually strong.  

As seen across Africa and elsewhere, China’s advantage has never simply been that Chinese companies are willing to tolerate more risk. Rather, Chinese firms can sometimes operate in political environments where the risks facing Western investors are fundamentally different from those facing them. And Nicaragua is quickly becoming an extreme example. 

It’s an asymmetry that is rooted in capital architecture. Western mining firms answer to public stock markets and quarterly profit targets, making 30-year political risks nearly impossible to justify to a corporate board. Chinese state-owned companies answer to five-year state plans. Because they aren’t chasing short-term stock returns, they can ride out local political chaos to lock in decades of control over vital resources.  

Nicaragua doesn’t need to become the next Congo
  

There is a temptation to judge Nicaragua’s strategic importance by slapping mineral labels on it. But in reality, it almost doesn’t matter. Nicaragua does not need to become another Democratic Republic of Congo, Indonesia, or Chile to matter to China. Chinese resource security is built through portfolios.  

Those familiar with the game of Monopoly will know that winning is not simply about owning the best property. It’s about owning ALL the property to restrict everyone else’s movement. That is optionality. In military circles, there is a brilliant term for this: “Anti-Access/Area Denial”, which basically involves
 
preventing an adversary force from deploying, and then restricting its freedom from any further action. 

In much the same way, uncontested control over large swathes of Nicaragua’s geology gives China extraordinary optionality while executing a pre-emptive resource lockout. If an Indo-Pacific crisis severs Asian critical mineral supply chains, the U.S. Defense Logistics Agency and Western defense primes would naturally look to near-shore resources in the Western Hemisphere. By buying up “Nicaragua Street,” Beijing makes sure that when Western defense strategists look to secure their own backyard, the prospective geology is already locked down under 30-year state-backed concessions. 

This mineral footprint connects directly to broader strategy. Since Managua severed diplomatic relations with Taiwan and signed a 
Free Trade Agreement with Beijing
 in 2024, diplomatic recognition, trade, infrastructure, and mining have moved as a unified system. Viewed together, mineral access is both a beneficiary of that political relationship and the economic anchor that solidifies it.  

Because Nicaragua is increasingly isolated from Western institutions, Chinese capital becomes the regime’s primary lifeline. It is a beautifully crafted feedback loop. The further Managua moves from democratic governance, the harder Western investment becomes. The harder Western investment becomes, the more valuable Chinese capital becomes. And as that capital becomes economically embedded (backed by sanctions-proof gold reserves) Nicaragua becomes increasingly capable of withstanding Western diplomatic and economic pressure. 

The western hemisphere matters
  

This exposes the core flaw in Western critical mineral strategy. If critical minerals are increasingly treated as a matter of economic and national security, analysis can no longer stop at resource geology and processing plants. It has to extend into the political systems, physical footprints, and sovereign relationships that dictate whether that resource can actually be secured in a crisis. China has understood this. By converting political alignment into physical, long-term territorial control in Central America, China has rewritten the rules of hemispheric defense, leaving Western strategists trying to play a market game on a board where the key spaces have already been taken.

Nicholas Vafeas is the founder and director of BluMelt Mineral Consulting Limited, an independent consultancy specialising in geological assurance, critical mineral intelligence and investment de-risking.

Boston Dynamics workers petition against ICE deal for Spot robots&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
Nearly 300 Boston Dynamics employees signed an internal petition this fall asking leadership to kill a $1.3 million robot sale tied to ICE. The company sold anyway, and the fight over who gets to use Spot just moved from the lab floor to the loading dock.

Boston Dynamics builds the four-legged robot that can climb stairs, open doors and do a backflip on command. Immigration and Customs Enforcement wants four of them to search tunnels at the border. According to WBUR, which obtained the petition and first reported on it this week, dozens of Boston Dynamics employees wrote to company leadership in early September asking it to cancel the sale, arguing it "presents us as working directly with ICE" at a moment when the agency has, in the employees' words, worked to sow fear across immigrant communities.

The petition picked up close to 300 signatures, WBUR reported, though a Boston Dynamics spokesperson noted that nearly half came in marked anonymous. The Waltham, Massachusetts company employs more than 1,500 people, so the petition is a vocal minority, not a majority revolt. But a vocal minority willing to put its name, or lack of one, on a letter against a paying customer is still a problem for any sales team.

The deal itself closed in September. ICE finalized a $1.3 million contract for four quadruped Spot units, according to the Boston Globe, which also reported on the petition. The package isn't the stock robot dog that does parkour in Boston Dynamics marketing videos. It comes loaded with a robotic arm, a thermal camera, and sensors built to detect radiation and explosive materials, kit meant for Homeland Security Investigations agents checking tunnels, crawl spaces and the tops of closets along the border for drugs, trafficking victims or contraband.

Here's the detail that complicates the employees' case: Boston Dynamics doesn't sell to ICE directly. The robots are going through Radeco, a Connecticut-based reseller, which means the company's own sales team can point to an arm's-length relationship even while cashing the check indirectly. That distinction matters to lawyers. It clearly didn't matter to the 300 people who signed anyway.

Boston Dynamics has leaned on a different defense in public: that the contract runs through ICE's Homeland Security Investigations division, which chases transnational crime, not its Enforcement and Removal Operations arm, the one that actually detains and deports people. A company spokesperson put it bluntly in a statement reported by multiple outlets covering the deal: "Regardless of any political implications or personal feelings about the organizations involved, if our robot can help keep people safe, we believe it should."

That line doesn't address what employees are actually worried about, which isn't a legal technicality about which ICE division writes the purchase order. It's optics, and optics don't read org charts.

This isn't the first time Boston Dynamics workers have pushed back. In May, employee pressure got the company to cancel a separate sale to a police equipment supplier that wanted to bolt flash-bang grenades onto a Spot unit, according to Semafor's reporting at the time. That one was a cleaner call: weapons mounted on a robot sold for law enforcement use is exactly the scenario Boston Dynamics has publicly sworn off.

Back in 2022, Boston Dynamics joined five other robotics makers, Agility Robotics, ANYbotics, Clearpath Robotics, Open Robotics and Unitree, in an open letter promising not to weaponize their general-purpose robots or help anyone else do it. The letter was specific about what it didn't cover, too. The companies wrote that they weren't taking issue with existing technologies that governments and their agencies already use to defend themselves and enforce the law. Surveillance, inspection, reconnaissance: all fair game under that pledge. The ICE contract is a surveillance and inspection sale. No weapons, no flash-bangs, nothing that trips the 2022 promise. Which is exactly why the May flash-bang case was easy to win and this one isn't.

Hyundai owns Boston Dynamics, and that ownership adds its own wrinkle. The automaker's Hyundai Rotem subsidiary builds tanks and unmanned weapons systems for the South Korean military, a fact critics have raised before when scrutinizing how seriously the company's parent takes robot ethics pledges. Boston Dynamics operates with real independence day to day, but it answers to a parent that has no comparable qualms about defense contracting elsewhere in its portfolio.

What this exposes is a gap every robotics vendor selling into government work is going to have to answer eventually: a weaponization pledge sounds clean until a government buyer asks for cameras and sensors instead of guns, and the pledge has nothing to say about that. Surveillance hardware pointed at immigrant communities raises harder questions than a mounted grenade launcher ever did, precisely because it's legal, funded, and defensible on a spec sheet. Boston Dynamics drew its line at weapons. Its own employees just told the company that line is in the wrong place.

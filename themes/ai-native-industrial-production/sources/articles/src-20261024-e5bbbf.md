Tesla, Nvidia to Bankroll $2.8 Billion Samsung Substrate Buildout as AI's Next Shortage Takes Shape

Tesla (TSLA) and Nvidia (NVDA) are preparing to pour a combined 3.7 trillion won, or roughly $2.8 billion, into Samsung Electro-Mechanics so the Korean parts maker can expand output of the advanced package substrates that AI processors cannot run without, according to a Korean press report cited by Reuters.

The split: Nvidia would put up about 2.6 trillion won (approximately $2.0 billion) and Tesla about 1.1 trillion won (approximately $829.9 million). The money would underwrite dedicated production lines at Samsung Electro-Mechanics' Sejong complex, south of Seoul, with Tesla reportedly locking in a five-year supply of flip-chip ball grid array, or FC-BGA, substrates running from 2029 through 2033 for its autonomous-driving silicon and Optimus humanoid robots.

The reported outlay lands on top of a strategic alliance the two companies have already made public. On Oct. 21, Samsung Electro-Mechanics and Nvidia announced a partnership to lift South Korea's capacity for high-end substrates, with dedicated lines planned in Sejong and Busan. Supplies under the multiyear agreement begin in 2026, with Samsung drawing on existing Korean capacity while the new lines are built. The company would supply substrates for Nvidia's AI processors as well as the high-speed networking chips used in data centers.

Samsung expects the volume it ships to Nvidia to climb at a double-digit annual rate, and by 2028 Nvidia could account for a substantial share of its total package-substrate capacity. The company has called the Sejong buildout its largest single-product investment ever. The 4.27 trillion won (approximately $3.2 billion) commitment, disclosed in a Sept. 28 regulatory filing, equals 43.6% of Samsung Electro-Mechanics' equity at the end of last year and runs from September 2026 through May 2028, with mass production targeted for September 2028. Tesla's 1.1 trillion won is part of the 4.27 trillion won budget rather than an addition to it. The Sejong project sits inside a wider July pledge to invest 8 trillion won in the Chungcheong region, which includes Sejong, and 15 trillion won in Busan by 2040. A further 2.51 trillion won (approximately $1.9 billion) is earmarked for FC-BGA production in Vietnam.

Nvidia's operations chief framed the spending as a matter of speed. "The pace of the AI infrastructure buildout requires us to expand manufacturing capacity on a global scale," Executive Vice President of Operations Debora Shoquist said.

A component most investors never see

FC-BGA substrates are the layer that carries power and electrical signals between a high-end processor and the broader circuit board. They are far less famous than the GPUs Nvidia designs, but those GPUs are inert without them. As AI accelerators grow larger and draw more power, substrates must carry more wiring layers and tighter tolerances, with stricter control of warping and flatness to hold yields and reliability. That difficulty has narrowed the field of suppliers able to deliver at the required size and quality, turning substrates into another pinch point in a supply chain already strained by chips, electricity and data-center space.

Samsung Electro-Mechanics has said customers are increasingly seeking long-term contracts and even offering financial support for new plants as bigger AI processors and heavier use of high-bandwidth memory strain substrate capacity. The company's second-quarter results show the shift: operating profit more than doubled to 440.4 billion won (approximately $332.3 million), up 106.7% from a year earlier, on revenue of 3.46 trillion won (approximately $2.6 billion), also up 24%. Net profit rose 139.2% to 328.3 billion won. The company attributed part of the gain to higher sales of high-value substrates for server CPUs and AI accelerators.

For Nvidia, paying to create capacity rather than waiting for vendors to add it is a way to protect a buildout measured in tens of billions of dollars. A single missing component can idle a data center. The same logic applies to Tesla, whose ambitions in robotaxis, the Cybercab and Optimus have pushed it to compete with the largest technology companies for the same manufacturing resources that go into chipmaking.

The effort reaches well beyond one plant

The Samsung arrangement sits inside a broader supply-chain push Nvidia outlined at its GTC event in Berlin. The chipmaker is working with Samsung Electro-Mechanics, Japan's Ibiden and Taiwan's Unimicron Technology (3037.TW) with the goal of lifting package-substrate capacity for its products to 2.5 times current levels within three years. "We are in the midst of the largest infrastructure buildout in human history. Demand is extraordinary. Capacity is the constraint," Nvidia Chief Executive Jensen Huang said at the event.

Nvidia also plans to double total semiconductor test capacity in 2027 from 2026 levels, supported by expansions in the United States, Taiwan and Singapore, with its Singapore test operations due to reach high-volume production in February 2027. The geographic split is not a clean onshoring story: Amkor, headquartered in Tempe, Arizona, is adding interposer capacity in Korea, while Taiwan's KYEC is building its new test capacity in the United States.

The investment case extends past the headline chipmakers. Nvidia's GPUs became the face of the AI boom's first phase; the second is exposing the unglamorous plumbing required to deploy them by the million. High-bandwidth memory was one constraint, electricity another, and advanced packaging has become a significant bottleneck in its own right. Substrates are now attracting commitments in the billions of dollars.

For Samsung Electro-Mechanics, customer money and multiyear purchase pledges lower the capital burden of expansion and improve visibility on orders, while giving the company a larger foothold in the high-value AI substrate market. For Tesla and Nvidia, the outlay buys insurance against shortages that could otherwise slow chip, vehicle and robot programs. The risk cuts both ways: capacity added in a hurry can become excess if AI demand cools, and a single hidden component that fails to arrive can leave a very expensive data center sitting dark.

South Korea's Won Nominal Effective Exchange Rate Hits 94.83, Two-Year High as USD-KRW Enters 1,310 Range
Add preferred source
The South Korean won's nominal effective exchange rate climbed to 94.83 (2020=100), its highest level since October 2024, as the USD-KRW exchange rate approached the 1,310 range. The gauge, which had fallen to 81.99 in June, rebounded alongside the won's sharp second-half appreciation, while the real effective exchange rate also hit a two-year high of 92.73 in September. Market participants are divided: some see room for further declines toward 1,250 won, citing record current account surpluses and foreign-currency repatriation led by semiconductors, while others expect downside rigidity to strengthen around 1,270-1,280 won.

The South Korean won has risen to its highest level against the currencies of major trading partners in more than two years, as the USD-KRW exchange rate fell into the low 1,320 range. Market forecasts are split between those who see room for further declines and those who argue that level burdens from the sharp drop must be taken into account.

According to the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and the Bank of Korea's Economic Statistics System, the won's nominal effective exchange rate stood at 94.83 (2020=100) as of October 20 — the highest reading since October 9, 2024 (94.91). The nominal effective exchange rate measures a currency's value weighted by trade shares with major trading partners; a higher number means the won has strengthened.

Looking at the trend, the won has traced a clear path of weakness in recent years. After hovering around 100 from 2020 through mid-2021 during the COVID-19 period, the nominal effective exchange rate fell to the mid-to-high 90s and slid to the 91 level immediately after the martial law incident. It plunged to the high 80s in March-April of last year amid a combination of US tariff issues and the impeachment political turmoil, dropped to the 86 level on year-end supply-demand instability, fell to the 84 level in March this year amid fallout from the Middle East war, and hit a low of 81.99 in June when the USD-KRW exchange rate neared 1,600 won due to foreign stock rebalancing.

The reversal began in the second half. The monthly average USD-KRW exchange rate (as of 3:30 p.m.) fell rapidly from 1,528.0 won in June to 1,488.9 won in July, 1,404.4 won in August and 1,358.8 won in September, and has recently approached the 1,310 range. Accordingly, the nominal effective exchange rate recovered to the 90 level (91.99) in September and continued its upward trend. The real effective exchange rate, which also reflects inflation differentials with trading partners, jumped from 83.03 in June to 92.73 last month, marking its highest level since November 2024 (93.43).

Bank of Korea Governor Shin Hyun-song said that despite the recent recovery, the nominal effective exchange rate remains below its long-term average, assessing that the won is still in undervalued territory.

The market generally sees room for further declines, though views differ markedly on the strength of downside pressure. Some argue that the lower bound should be left open to the 1,250 won level, citing record current account surpluses and improved dollar supply-demand. Others analyze that, given the steepness of the recent drop, downside rigidity will increase in the high 1,200 range.

Cha Young-hoo, a researcher at Eugene Investment & Securities, said that the won's continued strength despite a series of external headwinds and large-scale net selling of South Korean stocks by foreign investors is evidence that the currency has significant energy to fall further if external factors stabilize. He explained that semiconductor shareholder returns and mega-project-related investment alone are expected to generate an annual foreign-currency repatriation effect of $210 billion (~277.6 trillion won), exceeding the annualized net outflow of $130 billion (~171.8 trillion won) in securities and direct investment over the past three years. He forecast that for at least the next year, the won-strengthening effect from capital inflows into South Korea will outpace foreign-currency re-outflow pressure.

Moon Da-woon, a researcher at Korea Investment & Securities, meanwhile, said that "downside rigidity will increase around 1,270-1,280 won," adding that "the upper end could easily rebound to around 1,380-1,390 won, the level reached after the September Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting, depending on external conditions such as wariness over the US midterm elections, geopolitical risks and uncertainty over the Federal Reserve's monetary policy."

The impact of the sharp exchange-rate decline on South Korean inflation and the profitability of exporting companies is also cited as a variable. If won strength continues, import prices will fall, easing upward pressure on consumer prices, while the won-converted revenue of exporters with a high share of dollar-denominated settlements could shrink. Some observers note that the exchange rate has already approached a level consistent with economic fundamentals, and that South Korean inflation could limit the extent of real-value appreciation.

Market participants see the Federal Reserve's rate path, the outcome of the midterm elections and the pace of the semiconductor industry recovery as key variables that will determine the future direction of the exchange rate.

Opinion - Can India Replicate the F-35 Model? Why the AMCA Demands a Predictable Production Roadmap&nbsp;&nbsp;Defence.in

[page text]
The recent landmark framework agreement between the United States Pentagon and Lockheed Martin offers a stark lesson in military procurement and industrial strategy.

By committing to a minimum of 850 F-35 Lightning II fighter jets over a ten-year period encompassing Lots 21 to 30, the United States has not just secured its aerial dominance; it has provided a masterclass in how to sustain a massive defence industrial base.

For India, currently navigating the complex developmental phases of its own fifth-generation fighter, the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA), the American approach highlights a critical missing piece in New Delhi's procurement puzzle: predictable, long-term production roadmaps.

The F-35 framework, announced in October 2026, aims to sustain a production rate of 156 aircraft annually for both domestic and international customers.

While this is not yet a finalised contract—it remains subject to congressional approval and funding—it sends an unmistakable signal to the global supply chain.

This decade-long visibility allows component manufacturers to invest in advanced tooling, expand their workforce, and optimise supply chains, ultimately driving down unit costs and ensuring steady deliveries.

In contrast, India's approach to fighter aircraft procurement has historically been characterised by staggered, piecemeal orders.

Whether it is the indigenous Tejas programme or imported platforms, the tendency to order aircraft in successive, fragmented batches creates a ripple effect of inefficiencies.

When manufacturers are unsure of the long-term demand, they are naturally reluctant to invest heavily in expanding production lines or building robust supplier ecosystems. This hesitancy translates into delayed deliveries and elevated costs.

To be fair, the AMCA programme is at a vastly different stage of its lifecycle compared to the mature F-35.

The Indian fifth-generation fighter is still awaiting its maiden flight, with the government actively seeking a private-sector partner to co-develop and produce the prototypes alongside the Aeronautical Development Agency.

It is entirely logical that the Indian Air Force (IAF) and the Ministry of Defence cannot place massive orders for an aircraft that has yet to prove its mettle in the skies.

Furthermore, the financial realities of India's defence spending cannot be ignored. The Union Budget for 2026-27 allocated approximately ₹2.19 lakh crore for the capital outlay of the defence services.

While substantial, this capital budget must accommodate a myriad of requirements across the armed forces, from submarines and tanks to air defence systems and infrastructure.

With a significant portion of the overall defence budget consumed by salaries and pensions, committing a massive chunk of funds to a single decade-long mega-order is a daunting fiscal challenge.

However, the core takeaway from the F-35 framework is not that India must immediately sign a cheque for hundreds of unproven stealth fighters.

Rather, the lesson is that industrial capacity and cost-efficiency are directly tied to the predictability of demand.

Once the AMCA successfully completes its developmental trials and demonstrates its capabilities, the government must resist the urge to place a timid initial order.

If India truly wishes to cultivate a self-reliant aerospace ecosystem capable of delivering fifth-generation fighters at scale, it must transition towards multi-year procurement commitments.

Providing a clear, legally backed roadmap for future batches of the AMCA will give private industries the confidence required to build the necessary infrastructure.

A predictable production line is the lifeblood of military aviation; without it, the AMCA risks remaining a brilliant engineering achievement rather than the backbone of a formidable air force.

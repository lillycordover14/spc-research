Hundreds of employees at the US robotics firm Boston Dynamics signed a petition calling for executives to halt a sale of the company’s robot dogs to US Immigration and Customs Enforcement—but executives stood by the deal, while saying the robots would not be weaponized.

On September 25, the Department of Homeland Security 
contract awarded $1.3 million
 to a Connecticut company called Radeco to provide ICE with modified versions of Boston Dynamics’ quadruped robots. But Boston Dynamics workers sent the 
petition letter
 with more than 300 signatures—half of them anonymous—to the company’s executives in early September and shared copies with 
The Boston Globe
 and 
WBUR public radio station
.

“Many of us have gotten messages from friends and family, asking us in disbelief and horror if this news is true, and how we can live with ourselves if it is,” according to the petition. “The risk for Boston Dynamics is simply too great to continue with this deal.”

The petition described the signatories as employees who normally “love showing off what we do to our friends, family and the world” and who “love challenging ourselves to innovate and change the perception of what robots can do.” The 300 signatures come out of a total Boston Dynamics workforce of approximately 1,400 people.

Spot’s Cerberus upgrade

The ICE contract covers the purchase of four 
Boston Dynamics Spot robots
 that were modified by Radeco to be marketed as a 
robot platform named Cerberus
. The contract describes these Cerberus robots as being intended to “detect radiation, toxic gas leaks and explosive threats in urban environments or disaster zones,” along with enabling other remote observation and inspection missions.

A centerpiece of the upgraded robot is an oxygen sensor that is intended to “ensure the air quality is breathable when personnel are placed in confined spaces such as tunnels.” The Cerberus robot dog also has a camera equipped with 30-times zoom capability, 360-degree cameras, and infrared thermal imaging.

The Cerberus robot dog would come with an optional Spot robotic arm that enables it to manipulate objects and open doors, along with providing additional camera views when extended. The ICE contract describes the robot as being generally capable of navigating complex industrial environments on its own and executing “repeatable autonomous missions with minimal human intervention.”

Such a purchase was justified in the ICE contract as supporting the Homeland Security Task Forces’ mission to carry out “counter-illicit cross-border tunnel operations” and dismantling “cross-border human smuggling and trafficking networks” being conducted by “criminal cartels, foreign gangs and transnational criminal organizations throughout the United States.”

“Spot robots are tools that help keep people out of harm’s way,” a Boston Dynamics spokesperson told Ars in a statement. “Regardless of any political implications or personal feelings about the organizations involved, if our robot can help keep people safe, we believe it should.”

From fighting transnational crime to deportations

Boston Dynamics has also emphasized that the robots would primarily be used by ICE’s Homeland Security Investigations unit that is focused on combating transnational criminal organizations, according to WBUR.

However, the 
Brennan Center for Justice
 has documented the Homeland Security Investigations unit as frequently being involved in deporting undocumented workers and conducting workplace raids during the first administration of President Donald Trump. These operations occurred between 2018 and 2021, before being halted by US Secretary of Homeland Security Alejandro Mayorkas early in the term of President Joe Biden.

During Trump’s second term that began in January 2025, the Homeland Security Investigations unit once again diverted thousands of agents to immigration enforcement and deportations after one of Trump’s executive orders redefined the unit’s primary mission, according to the 
Immigration Policy Tracking Project
.

The Boston Dynamics employee petition specifically referenced an ICE and Homeland Security Investigations workplace raid that detained 475 primarily South Korean workers at the Hyundai Motor Group Metaplant America facility in Georgia on September 4, 2025. Hyundai Motor Group became the parent company of Boston Dynamics after 
acquiring the US robotics company
 in June 2021.

“We cannot be proud of working at Boston Dynamics when that work is being used by a government agency that brutalizes civilians, including some of our own colleagues at Hyundai,” according to the employee petition. “All elements of the ICE organization, not just Enforcement & Removal Operations, have worked to sow fear among all people living in this country.”

That 
single largest immigration enforcement operation
 of its kind sparked public outrage in South Korea and led to South Korean leaders questioning the country’s business investments in the United States. However, Hyundai has maintained its operations at the US electric vehicle production at the Metaplant America factory and still plans to deploy 
thousands of Boston Dynamics’ Atlas humanoid robots
 starting at its US facilities.

Boston Dynamics says Spot won’t be weaponized

The Boston Dynamics leadership team has stood by the ICE contract with Radeco, which would mainly involve Boston Dynamics providing technical support for the modified robots, according to The Boston Globe.

The company also responded to the employee petition via an email from Amanda McMaster, former interim CEO for Boston Dynamics. That email was obtained and quoted in part by both The Boston Globe and WBUR.

“We believe this decision is in line with our purpose of ethically advancing intelligent machines in service of humanity,” McMaster wrote in her email. She urged workers to “respect and accept this decision, even if you do not agree with it.”

McMaster also pointed to the company’s 
terms and conditions
 for sale of the Spot robots that prohibit “intentional use of the Product to harm or intimidate any person or animal, as a weapon, or to enable any weapon.” The company previously 
signed an open letter
 opposing the weaponization of “advanced-mobility general-purpose robots.”

The Boston Dynamics spokesperson told Ars that the company has “worked productively” with the Department of Homeland Security and Homeland Security Investigations unit for years, and said the latter have abided by the company’s terms and conditions of sale.

The robotics company said it would also “take immediate action” if any customer misused Spot, although it said “there have been no cases of a customer violating those terms and conditions” while working with the Department of Homeland Security and other “public safety agencies.”

“That would include specific steps like voiding the warranty, refusing all future software updates, terminating their license, taking any appropriate legal action available to us, and likely never selling to that customer again,” said the Boston Dynamics spokesperson.

Future workforce concerns

The Boston Dynamics employee backlash to the ICE contract comes as the company is developing a new advanced robotics and AI facility in Waltham, Massachusetts, near its headquarters. The $100 million project includes creating 1,250 new jobs by 2033, according to a 
company press release
.

The ICE contract represents “a complicated and emotionally charged topic for many people, including some of our own employees,” said the Boston Dynamics spokesperson. “As our company continues to grow at a rapid pace, we will continue welcoming a highly diverse group of employees with views on every side of the social and political spectrum.”

But as Boston Dynamics ramps up its production capabilities and deployment of robots such as Spot and Atlas, the employees who signed the petition expressed concerns that the company’s association with the ICE contract will threaten both workforce hiring and retention.

“We fear we will lose teammates over this decision, and worry about the risk it creates to achieve our shared company goals,” according to the petition. “This decision makes Boston Dynamics a less attractive employer—it will make it more challenging to build the team we need as we scale.”

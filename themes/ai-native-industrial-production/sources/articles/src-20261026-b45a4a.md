Faith Hedgepeth Trial

    Closing arguments presented in Faith Hedgepeth murder trial

    After the defense unexpectedly rested its case Wednesday, closing statements are proceeding sooner than expected. The jury may begin deliberations today. 

Aidan Lockhart
, 
Enterprise Managing Editor
                 
             •  
            
October 22, 2026
 

 

Lisa Coltrain, Homicide and Arson Resource Prosecutor for the North Carolina Conference of District Attorneys, points toward defense while consulting the jury in closing statements during the Faith Hedgepeth murder trial on Thursday, Oct. 22, 2026. Photo courtesy of WRAL YouTube livestream.
 

Content warning: This story contains mentions of violence, sexual assault and death. 

Prosecutors gave their closing arguments Thursday in the murder trial of Faith Hedgepeth, much earlier than expected after the defense rested its case Wednesday without presenting any evidence. The prosecution’s arguments lasted over two hours.

Hedgepeth was a 19-year-old UNC sophomore who was 
killed
 in her Durham apartment on Sept. 7, 2012. According to her death certificate and later autopsy reports, she died from blunt force trauma to the head. 

The trial seeks to determine whether 33-year-old Miguel Salguero Olivares is guilty of first-degree murder, first-degree burglary, first-degree rape and first-degree sexual offense in connection with Hedgepeth’s killing. Investigators say Salguero Olivares’ DNA was linked to the murder weapon nine years after Hedgepeth’s murder.

On Wednesday, defense attorneys for Salguero Olivares surprised the courtroom by declining to present any evidence. Judge Keith Gregory sent the jury home early, and the charges that jurors will deliberate on were finalized.

The State is bringing charges for both first- and second-degree murder, with two theories for the first-degree murder charge. Jurors may convict Salguero Olivares of first-degree murder under premeditation or deliberation, meaning he acted with deliberate intent, or they may convict him of first-degree felony murder, meaning the death happened while he was committing a dangerous felony even if he did not have deliberate intent to kill.

If the jurors feel there is insufficient evidence to find Salguero Olivares guilty of first-degree murder, they will have the option to convict him of a lesser second-degree murder charge.

The prosecution also moved to remove all lesser offenses under the first-degree burglary and first-degree rape charges, meaning that if the jurors do not feel the state has proven first-degree burglary or rape, they should return a verdict of not guilty with no lesser alternative.

Charges outlined to jurors, deliberations to begin soon

Gregory explained that the jurors are to determine whether the defendant is guilty of first-degree murder, second-degree murder or not guilty of either. He explained the difference between first- and second-degree murder, as well as the two charges of first-degree murder being brought against Salguero Olivares — first-degree murder under premeditation or deliberation and first-degree felony murder.

Gregory said the jury may find Salguero Olivares guilty of either or both first-degree murder charges, or return a verdict of not guilty.

He further outlined every aspect of each charge, also including the first-degree sexual offense, first-degree rape and first-degree burglary charges brought against the Salguero Olivares.

Gregory then gave final instructions to the jurors, stating that they should consider all evidence presented during the trial, and that all 12 of the jurors must agree on a verdict.

The jurors were dismissed to the juror room at 2:58 p.m.

Judge instructs jurors on deliberation process

Following the conclusion of the defense’s closing arguments, Judge Gregory gave deliberation instructions to the jurors.

Gregory instructed the jurors on how they should evaluate the truthfulness of witnesses, including witnesses with an interest in the outcome of the trial, and consider the reasonableness and consistency of testimony. He also instructed the jurors on how to evaluate the testimony of expert witnesses.

He reiterated the burden of proof — beyond a reasonable doubt — which jurors must hold the prosecution to. He instructed the jurors to “apply the law as I give it to you and not as you think it is or as you might like it to be.”

Gregory reminded the jury that the defense is not required to prove the defendant’s innocence, and that, rather, the burden of proof is on the prosecution.

Gregory also provided the jurors with the legal definition of intent: a mental attitude seldom provable by direct evidence.

DNA probabilities, evidence handling remain defense’s focus

Rainsford said he is concerned about the hypotheticals used in likelihood calculations included in the N.C. State Crime Laboratory’s reports on DNA evidence.

“They’re running hypotheticals on whether it might be somebody’s DNA,” Rainsford said. 

He said he takes issue with some of the DNA hypotheticals not making the assumption that Hedgepeth was a part of the DNA mixture tested.

Rainsford also said that he has concerns about the handling of crime-scene evidence and how much evidence was tested. He said he tried to figure out how many officers entered and exited the crime scene during the investigation by asking witnesses, but that the number seemed like a “closely-guarded secret.”

He said that if the State didn’t care about there allegedly being blood on Rosario’s hand, they wouldn’t have tried to convince the jury that it was Rosario’s red-slushee vomit during their closing argument. He said that details like these aren’t noise, but rather questions that should be answered when trying to convict someone of murder.

Rainsford addresses crime-scene note, DNA evidence

Rainsford expressed doubt as to the prosecution’s claim, made during closing arguments, that Salguero Olivares may have written the note at the crime scene which read “I’M NOT STUPID / BITCH / JEALOUS” because he was learning English at the time and may have meant to write “I’M NOT STUPID, JEALOUS BITCH.”

Rainsford said this claim doesn’t make any sense. If the defendant was able to spell “JEALOUS” and use a contraction, it is illogical that he wouldn’t know how to form the sentence.

He also said that the fact that the comforter was found covering Hedgepeth’s body is consistent with someone who was horrified with what had happened, not someone who was in a rage.

Rainsford also mentioned “tunnel vision” on the part of criminal investigators. He said that the presence of sperm alone does not mean that rape or a sexual offense occurred.

“You can’t just make this quantum leap that there was sexual assault,” Rainsford said. “You don’t get to just do that. You have to look at the evidence.”

Rainsford casts doubt on State’s assumptions

Rainsford attempted to poke holes in the State’s theory of how the gas cap on Hedgepeth’s car was opened. Garcia-Lamarca suggested during her closing argument that, in a rage after being embarrassed by Hedgepeth and Rosario’s rejection, Salguero Olivares opened the gas cap.

Coltrain emphasizes reason, concludes State’s closing arguments

Coltrain urged the jurors to recognize their duty to apply reason and common sense to their deliberation.

“When you apply that reason and common sense, noise quiets down, and the actual evidence screams at you,” Coltrain said.

She moved on to discuss how Salguero Olivares could have been placed at the scene of the crime. She said that Luis Lainez Lopez, a man who lived at Rosario and Hedgepeth’s apartment complex, testified that he had a cookout the night Hedgepeth was killed. Lainez Lopez testified that a distant cousin of his wife, named Abel, arrived with a man who spoke spanish that Lainez Lopez did not know.

Coltrain suggested that this man was Salguero Olivares. She began to detail the State’s version of events as she presented images from the crime scene. Multiple jurors and members of Hedgepeth’s family in the audience were crying.

“He beat her, he beat her, he beat her,” Coltrain said. “That young girl didn’t have a chance.”

She discussed the handwriting on the white paper bag left at the scene of the crime which read “I’M NOT STUPID / BITCH / JEALOUS.” She said that Salguero Olivares was learning English during 2012, and that it would make sense that if he were writing on the bag, he may have intended to write “I’M NOT STUPID, JEALOUS BITCH.”

Coltrain urged the jury to find Salguero Olivares guilty.

“He turned that beautiful woman into this corpse,” she said.

Coltrain concluded the State’s closing arguments at 12:08 p.m.

Coltrain addresses potential argument against Rosario

Coltrain said she believes the defense will attempt to distract the jury by claiming Rosario is responsible. She said when common sense is applied to any of the defense’s arguments, they fall apart.

Coltrain said that the person who killed Hedgepeth would have to have been covered in blood. She then presented the jury with graphic images of the crime scene, emphasizing the amount of blood in the photos.

“They would be covered in blood,” Coltrain repeated multiple times as the photos were presented.

Coltrain emphasized that there was no blood found on Rosario’s clothes except for her own. She also showed a photo of the bathroom sink, which appeared to have spots of diluted blood on it. Coltrain asked the jury to consider how, if Rosario was responsible, she could have washed all of the blood off her body and left a single spot on her hand.

She claimed there was zero evidence of animosity between Rosario and Hedgepeth.

“Nothing about that makes any sense,” Coltrain said.

Coltrain continues discussion of investigation, anticipates defense’s argument

Coltrain said that the CHPD officers were doing their best with the resources they had. She said the defense is going to try to cast doubt about the quality and integrity of the investigation.

Coltrain claimed that even if CHPD officers went into the apartment, rolled around on the floor, spit on the Bacardi bottle and rubbed the white paper bag all over their bodies, it still wouldn’t matter because Salguero Olivares’ DNA would still be at the crime scene. She said that over 14 years, there are going to be mistakes, but that those mistakes don’t take the DNA out of Hedgepeth’s body.

“They tried to make sense out of senselessness for nine years, and they never gave up,” Coltrain said.

Coltrain said she anticipates the defense will argue that there was no sexual offense or rape because there was no trauma to Hedgepeth’s genitalia. She said that this argument makes no sense because an expert witness testified during the trial that it is fairly common for victims of sexual assault to have no physical injuries.

Coltrain emphasized that the evidence, including photos of the crime scene, show that Hedgepeth was raped.

“There is no woman on God’s green earth who pulls out her tampon and throws it on the floor,” Coltrain said. “This was a rape. This was a violent rape.”

Focus shifts to investigation

Coltrain began to discuss the investigation of Hedgepeth’s killing by the Chapel Hill Police Department and North Carolina State Bureau of Investigation. She stated repeatedly that no shortfall in the investigation can negate the DNA evidence.

“The perfect investigation does not exist,” Coltrain said.

Police have to be able to use some common sense, she said. Police didn’t interview every single person in the area or test every single item in the apartment, Coltrain said, but once again emphasized that investigators didn’t need to.

“He left his DNA like confetti all over that room,” she said.

Prosecution attempts to explain motive, instructs jury on first-degree murder charges

Garcia-Lamarca moved on to explain the two first-degree murder charges the State is bringing against Salguero Olivares: premeditated first-degree murder and first-degree felony murder.

She claimed that Salguero Olivares premeditated the killing of Hedgepeth because he used a deadly weapon and had time to change his mind as he walked in the apartment, grabbed a bottle and hit her repeatedly. She suggested he was embarrassed and upset after being rejected by Hedgepeth.

“When he saw Karena leave, he knew Faith was alone,” Garcia-Lamarca said.

Prosecution outlines charges, presents a theory of events

“We cannot prove the why,” Garcia-Lamarca said to the jury.

Regardless, she attempted to suggest a motive. 
Garcia-Lamarca said the State believes Salguero Olivares saw Hedgepeth and her roommate, Karena Rosario, entering their apartment. She said the State believes
 Salguero Olivares may have attempted to hit on the women in the parking lot, and became angry when Hedgepeth rejected him. 

Garcia-Lamarca walked the jury through the elements of first-degree sexual offense and first-degree rape. She attempted to discredit the alternative theory, which has been repeatedly suggested by the defense, that Salguero Olivares and Hedgepeth may have had a consensual sexual relationship prior to her killing that would explain the semen found in her body. 

She said that if this was true, semen would have been found on the inside of Hedgepeth’s tampon, but it was not. Combined with the state Hedgepeth was found in, Garcia-Lamarca said there is no doubt that sexual offense and rape occurred.

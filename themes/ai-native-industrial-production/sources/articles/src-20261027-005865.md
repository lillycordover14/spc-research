PCB demand for physical AI soars as on-demand maker CircuitHub expands

                                          By
                                                                    

Matt Hamblen

Oct 27, 2026 6:46pm

            CircuitHub says it builds and ships PCBs 2x faster than Chinese counterparts. (CircuitHub)
          

A critical component of physical AI infrastructure is the printed circuit board, but it has been difficult in the US for designers of electric vehicles, humanoids and wearables to get quick turnaround of PCB prototypes for use in their new concepts.  It’s also been difficult to bring those concepts up to scale with PCBs as the demand for physical AI applications soars.

As a reminder, PCBs usually carry all the components for a successful application that are soldered together on a non-conductive substrate, usually FR-4, a combination of fiberglass and epoxy. The components include the essentials from AI chips for compute to sensors to memory to motors to communications and power.  Some PCBs are tiny, placed inside ring and wrist wearables or the fingers of a humanoid.

There are hundreds of contract electronics manufacturers in the US that could potentially provide help for physical AI designers, but many if not all are capacity constrained, according to analysts and industry officials. 

One company, CircuitHub believes it has found a way around the constraints with automated, software-driven factories for on-demand electronics manufacturing. It provides The Grid platform to produce from one to 10,000 PCBs with a standard three-day turnaround, which can be half the time it takes to order from a manufacturer in China. 

CircuitHub recently announced its Massachusetts facility is operating at full scale and the company is increasing capacity fivefold, with plans to open the world’s first AI native electronics factory in the UK in 2027.   The company also boasts its prices are on par with those of Chinese companies, and it counts among customers a large US EV carmaker and a large AI lab. 

The company was founded in 2013 and has just under 100 employees. Earlier this year it raised $28 million for a total of $48 million in investor funding. “We’re a startup but we aim to grow fast,” said Andrew Seddon, founder and CEO, in an interview with Fierce.  The company has worked with 20,000 engineers on electronics designs and believes it has a leading position in contract engineering services.

What gives CircuitHub an edge is its use of automation with levitating movers that zip around a factory carrying each board and its parts between robots that print paste, place parts, solder and inspect.  Every board produced takes its own route through the factory, so there is no stopping of the production line for changeovers to save time.  Each job works its way through the factory along its own pathway, so multiple custom jobs can be produced at once.

Traditionally, technology at the PCB assembly level has been “super labor intensive,” Seddon explained. In some assembly operations, 90% of the cost of goods sold is labor.  “The Grid system applies automation to the processes for electronics assembly and we coordinate it like factory-scale robots.”

In recent years, the demand crunch for production began during Covid, then the chip supply change shortage hit and 2025 marked the biggest trend of VCs flowing dollars into AI hardware companies.  “The infrastructure buildout as a result is a huge domestic balloon.”

Seddon said he knew how difficult it was to get a PCB for a specific application produced in a hurry when he worked as a technical design engineer for movies in Hollywood early in his career.  (He designed a HeadCam for the 2009 Robert Zemeckis film, 
A Christmas Carol.
) Dealing with those delays was “deeply unsatisfying,” he said, and also an inspiration to find a better way. 

“For thirty years the West accepted a trade-off: design here, build in China, and wait. The Grid ends that trade-off. Every board takes its own route through the factory, so a single prototype is as easy to build as a production batch, and it’s built in Massachusetts in half the time. We’ve been quietly running it for five years, and demand is already far ahead of what we can build.”

CircuitHub competes in a crowded US electronics manufacturing industry and its more direct competitors are digitally operated, quick-turn PCB assemblers such as MacroFab and Screaming Circuits rather than global manufacturing giants like Jabil and Flex.

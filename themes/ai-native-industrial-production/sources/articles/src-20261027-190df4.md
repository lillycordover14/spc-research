Analysis - Like F-22 and Su-35, Can 290kN Twin-Engine Thrust Put AMCA Mk2 into Global Heavyweight Fighter Class?&nbsp;&nbsp;Defence.in

[page text]
In defence aviation, engine thrust numbers are often discussed with great enthusiasm, yet they are rarely translated into what they actually mean for the airframe itself.

A particularly interesting inflection point has emerged in recent conversations regarding India’s Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA) programme and subsequent next-generation projects.

The proposition of a twin-145kN engine configuration is gaining traction as a long-term conceptual goal.

With a combined thrust of 290kN, this powerplant could comfortably lift an aircraft in the same weight category as some of the heaviest fighters operating today.

However, the final classification of such an aircraft depends as much on its core design philosophy as it does on its engines.

Decoding the Thrust-to-Weight Ratio​
A combined thrust of 290kN from two 145kN afterburning engines is a formidable figure.

Yet, raw thrust alone does not define a fighter's capabilities. The critical metric is the thrust-to-weight (T/W) ratio, which essentially reveals the designer's intent and the aircraft's primary operational mission.

To understand the potential of a 290kN airframe, we must benchmark it against existing heavy fighters with similar power outputs.

The American F-22 Raptor, powered by twin F119 engines, has a theoretical maximum take-off weight (MTOW) of roughly 38,000 kg and maintains a T/W ratio of around 0.83.

Similarly, the Russian Su-35, utilising twin AL-41F1S engines that produce an estimated 284kN combined, sits at an MTOW of near 34,500 kg with a T/W ratio of 0.84. Its stealthier sibling, the Su-57, operates with a T/W ratio between 0.85 and 0.86 at MTOW.

These ratios are not coincidental. Traditional air-superiority fighters are designed to be heavy, yet they operate within a T/W "sweet spot" of 0.8 to 0.86 at maximum weight.

This range allows the aircraft to execute sustained turns, maintain a high climb rate, and carry substantial internal fuel and weaponry without compromising aerodynamics.

Applying this established T/W metric to a hypothetical 290kN twin-engine fighter places its MTOW squarely in the 34,300 kg to 37,000 kg bracket—firmly within the traditional heavy-fighter class.

The Role of Mission Philosophy​
However, the transition from fifth to sixth-generation fighter concepts broadens this weight estimate significantly. The ultimate MTOW is dictated by the specific mission requirements demanded by the air force.

If the airframe is designed strictly for pure air superiority—prioritising extreme agility with a lighter internal payload and fuel load—the T/W ratio could be pushed closer to a highly aggressive 1.0.

This would result in a sleeker, lighter aircraft with an MTOW dropping to around 29,500 kg.

Conversely, if the design leans towards a multirole, strike-heavy platform, the engineering requirements change drastically.

The need for larger internal weapons bays to accommodate heavy stand-off munitions and greater internal fuel capacity for extended operational range would drag the T/W ratio down towards 0.75. This design path would swell the MTOW towards the 39,400 kg mark.

Sixth-generation programmes globally are currently navigating these exact engineering compromises.

Designers must balance the need for extreme range and persistence—often required to operate alongside loyal wingman drones—against the aerodynamic penalties of increased weight, all while maintaining low observability (stealth) and high manoeuvrability.

If a future Indian platform adopts a balanced air-superiority approach with sixth-generation characteristics, a realistic MTOW estimate settles in the 33,000 kg to 35,000 kg range.

Contextualising the AMCA Mk2​
It is important to view these figures as physics-based extrapolations rather than official specifications.

Currently, India's indigenous propulsion roadmap for the AMCA Mk2 is focused on developing a joint-venture engine in the 110 to 130kN class for the near to medium term.

The discussion surrounding a 145kN engine is not a reflection of the immediate AMCA Mk2 baseline, but rather a window into the ultimate end goal of India’s aerospace ambitions.

A twin-145kN setup, driving a 33 to 35-tonne aircraft, represents a future where Indian defence manufacturing steps confidently into the realm of sixth-generation, heavyweight fighter capabilities.

            The Los Angeles startup is building robotic composites manufacturing cells in Torrance, California, targeting cycle times under 15 minutes for unmanned systems, robotics and aerospace parts.

Helicon Industries
 (Los Angeles, Calif., U.S.) has emerged from stealth with $16.5 million in seed funding to build automated, high-volume manufacturing systems for composite parts, starting with carbon fiber. The startup is preparing for production at a new facility in Torrance, California, and aims to shorten composite part lead times from months to weeks.

According to 
The Robot Report
, the oversubscribed round was led by AlleyCorp (New York, N.Y., U.S.) and followed earlier angel investment. 
JEC Composites
 lists other investors, including Josh Buckley, Mike Chalfen, Gokul Rajaram, Eileen Tanghal, Fredrik Hjelm, Charlie Songhurst and Lucas Vaz.

Helicon was co-founded by CEO Alex Niehaus and CTO Edmundo Sanz-Gadea. Sanz-Gadea studied aerospace at TU Delft and worked on autonomous drones and robotics before founding the company. He told The Robot Report that composites manufacturing is still largely manual and hard to automate, especially the handling of flexible carbon fiber textiles. He added that conventional mold-based prepreg, vacuum infusion and resin transfer molding (RTM) workflows fit low-volume, high-cost programs. They do not fit the much larger volumes now sought by drone, robotics and aerospace companies.

The company is moving into a 36,000-square-foot space in Torrance, where materials, equipment and processes are all being selected for automation. “The goal is to use processes that can be automated much more easily and run with much faster cycle times — under 15 minutes, compared with hours for traditional layup and autoclave processes,” Sanz-Gadea says. Robots are intended to pick and place material, remove finished parts and handle downstream steps such as trimming and quality control. Helicon is also working with multiple material suppliers. “There’s not really a shortage of carbon fiber itself,” Sanz-Gadea points out. “The bottleneck is the ability to turn that material into finished parts at scale domestically.”

As JEC observes, Helicon’s website shows manufacturing simulations for mostly unmanned air, land and maritime platforms, ranging from a 10-inch quadcopter to a humanoid robot and an autonomous underwater vehicle. The site cites lead times of 2-6 weeks and minimum order quantities of 10-100 units. The Robot Report notes that Helicon is pursuing certifications for aerospace and defense programs and is also seeing interest from the robotics, logistics and maritime markets. Most robotic systems today use aluminum, and Sanz-Gadea says Helicon’s materials offer higher stiffness at 40% lower weight. 

Helicon is now hiring across engineering, software, robotics, manufacturing and operations. “The ambition is much bigger than making one type of carbon fiber part,” Sanz-Gadea says. “We want to build the manufacturing capacity to produce advanced composites at a real industrial scale.”

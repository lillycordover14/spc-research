Nuclear Deterrence Explained&nbsp;&nbsp;Diplomacy and Law

Introduction
Nuclear deterrence is a military and political strategy intended to prevent an adversary from attacking or taking another unwanted action by convincing it that the consequences of nuclear retaliation or escalation would outweigh any anticipated benefits. Its purpose is to influence an opponent's decisions before an attack occurs. Unlike conventional military defense, which may depend on defeating enemy forces in combat, nuclear deterrence relies heavily on the prospect of destruction so severe that initiating conflict becomes an unacceptable risk.
The strategy rests on a difficult proposition: weapons capable of causing catastrophic destruction may discourage war because their consequences are so dangerous. For deterrence to work, an adversary must believe that a state possesses the military capability to retaliate and that its political leadership might authorize nuclear use under the circumstances concerned. Possession alone does not guarantee this result. The credibility of threats, the survivability of nuclear forces, and the opponent's interpretation of political intentions all influence deterrence.
Nuclear strategy developed during the Cold War, when the United States and the Soviet Union acquired arsenals capable of devastating one another. The emergence of second-strike capability and mutually assured destruction changed strategic calculations by making the consequences of major war potentially unacceptable to both sides. Yet the absence of direct nuclear war between the superpowers does not establish that deterrence was solely responsible for preventing it. Diplomacy, conventional military balances, political restraint, and the particular circumstances of successive crises also influenced their decisions.
Nuclear deterrence remains a major feature of international security. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute identified nine nuclear-armed states in its 2026 assessment, although estimates of their arsenals vary in reliability and transparency (SIPRI, 2026). Several non-nuclear states also rely on extended deterrence commitments from nuclear-armed allies. These arrangements raise questions about strategic stability, alliance credibility, accidental escalation, and the relationship between nuclear threats and international law.
1. How Nuclear Deterrence Works
Nuclear deterrence operates by changing the expected consequences of aggression. A government contemplating an attack must weigh the political or military advantages it hopes to obtain against the possibility of retaliation. If nuclear retaliation is regarded as sufficiently likely and destructive, the anticipated costs may exceed the expected benefits. The objective is to prevent the initial decision to attack rather than defeat the aggressor after hostilities begin.
Consider a hypothetical confrontation between two nuclear-armed states. One believes it could achieve a limited territorial objective through conventional military action. Before proceeding, however, its leadership must consider whether the opposing government could interpret the operation as threatening its security and respond with nuclear weapons. Even when the probability of nuclear retaliation appears relatively low, the severity of the possible consequences may alter the decision.
Conventional forces can also deter aggression by demonstrating that an attack would fail or require costly fighting. James J. Wirtz distinguishes conventional deterrent threats, whose execution may depend on uncertain battlefield outcomes, from nuclear threats that are generally more difficult for an adversary to contest or neutralize (Wirtz, 2018). This distinction does not make nuclear retaliation inevitable or its execution entirely uncontestable. Interception capabilities, counterforce attacks, operational failures, and doubts about political resolve can all affect the credibility of a nuclear threat.
Nuclear deterrence represents one application of 
hard power in international relations
, through which states seek to influence others by threatening costs or employing material capabilities. Its effectiveness cannot be measured by the destructive capacity of an arsenal alone. A state may possess substantial nuclear forces and still fail to prevent limited aggression if its adversary believes those forces are unlikely to be employed in the circumstances concerned.
1.1 Credibility, Capability, and Political Resolve
Credibility is central to deterrence theory. A threat influences an adversary only when that adversary believes the threatened action could occur. Nuclear forces provide the material basis for retaliation, but political resolve affects whether their use appears plausible. Communication connects those elements by allowing an opponent to understand the circumstances under which retaliation might follow.
A government defending its own territory against a nuclear attack may have a more credible reason to retaliate than one threatening nuclear use over a comparatively minor political dispute. The adversary's calculations depend on the interests at stake, the military balance, and its interpretation of the defending government's willingness to accept substantial risks. Credibility consequently varies across situations rather than existing as a permanent attribute of nuclear possession.
States communicate their intentions through official nuclear doctrines, military deployments, exercises, diplomatic warnings, and public statements. Some identify conditions under which nuclear weapons might be used. Others deliberately preserve uncertainty about their responses. Strategic ambiguity may complicate an opponent's planning, but excessive ambiguity can also produce misunderstanding, particularly when governments interpret the same signals differently.
Classical deterrence theory frequently assumes that leaders assess alternatives according to expected costs and benefits. Thomas Schelling examined how threats, commitments, and the deliberate manipulation of risk could influence bargaining between adversaries in Arms and Influence (1966). Actual political decisions are less predictable. Incomplete intelligence, domestic political pressures, ideological commitments, and mistaken judgments can cause leaders to underestimate dangers or misunderstand signals that another government believes are unmistakable.
1.2 Deterrence by Punishment, Denial, and First-Use Policies
Deterrence by punishment seeks to prevent aggression by threatening consequences that would follow an attack. In nuclear strategy, the threatened punishment may involve retaliation against military capabilities or other assets whose destruction would impose unacceptable costs. The adversary must believe that the defending state could deliver that retaliation despite any military measures intended to prevent it.
Deterrence by denial operates differently. It seeks to convince an attacker that military action will not achieve its objectives. Strong conventional defenses, missile defenses, and the capacity to defeat an invasion can support denial. Nuclear and conventional capabilities may also operate together, particularly where a state fears that an unsuccessful conventional defense could place its survival at risk. The distinction concerns how aggression is discouraged, not an absolute separation between weapon categories.
Nuclear doctrines differ over first use. A no-first-use policy declares that nuclear weapons will not be employed before an adversary uses them. China reaffirmed its commitment to no first use in its November 2025 white paper, China's Arms Control, Disarmament, and Nonproliferation in the New Era (State Council Information Office, 2025). Other nuclear powers retain options for nuclear first use under circumstances defined, sometimes ambiguously, by their security policies. Such differences influence the credibility, scope, and perceived danger of nuclear threats.
Deterrence must also be distinguished from compellence. A deterrent threat attempts to prevent an adversary from taking an unwanted action, whereas compellence seeks to induce a change in existing conduct. Nuclear threats may be used for either purpose, but compelling an opponent to withdraw forces, abandon territory, or accept political demands creates different problems of credibility and escalation. An adversary that has already acted may face substantial political costs from reversing its decision, making coercive threats harder to execute successfully.
2. Second-Strike Capability and Mutually Assured Destruction
Second-strike capability is the ability of a nuclear-armed state to retaliate after absorbing an adversary's nuclear first strike. Its strategic significance lies in the survival of enough weapons, delivery systems, and command capabilities to impose unacceptable damage despite an initial attack. A state possessing credible second-strike forces denies its adversary confidence that a surprise assault could eliminate the threat of retaliation.
This capability changes the calculation of a potential attacker. If the aggressor cannot destroy the defending state's nuclear forces before they respond, even an apparently successful initial attack may expose its territory and military infrastructure to devastating retaliation. The possibility of such an outcome creates incentives to avoid nuclear war regardless of which side possesses the larger arsenal.
Mutually assured destruction, commonly abbreviated as MAD, describes a strategic condition in which opposing nuclear powers retain the capacity to inflict unacceptable destruction on one another. It became closely associated with American-Soviet strategic relations during the Cold War, particularly as survivable retaliatory forces became central to strategic planning. The underlying logic was that neither side could confidently initiate a nuclear war without risking consequences that would outweigh the anticipated gains.
MAD should not be confused with nuclear deterrence itself. Deterrence is the broader strategy of influencing an adversary through threats and anticipated consequences. Mutually assured destruction describes a particular relationship of reciprocal vulnerability, rather than a formal agreement between adversaries or a doctrine universally accepted by nuclear-armed states. Governments may seek to deter attacks without maintaining equivalent arsenals or explicitly accepting mutual vulnerability as a desirable condition.
2.1 Survivable Nuclear Forces and the Nuclear Triad
Second-strike capability requires more than the possession of nuclear warheads. Retaliation depends on whether weapons and their delivery systems survive an initial attack, whether the government retains the ability to authorize their employment, and whether communications remain functional. An arsenal concentrated in vulnerable locations may provide a less credible retaliatory capability than a smaller force designed to survive a surprise strike.
The nuclear triad distributes strategic forces among land-based intercontinental ballistic missiles, submarine-launched ballistic missiles, and nuclear-capable strategic bombers. These systems have different operational characteristics and vulnerabilities. Hardened or mobile land-based missiles can complicate targeting, ballistic missile submarines can remain concealed at sea, and bombers offer operational flexibility because they may be deployed, recalled, or redirected before weapons are released.
The United States maintains a nuclear triad, while other nuclear-armed states rely on different combinations of delivery systems. A complete triad is not an indispensable requirement for credible deterrence. What ultimately matters is whether an adversary believes sufficient retaliatory forces would remain operational following an attack. The capacity to preserve and employ those forces is more consequential than the number of delivery categories maintained.
Command and control presents a separate challenge. Systems must prevent unauthorized nuclear employment while ensuring that legitimate orders can be communicated during severe disruption. Communications failures, damaged infrastructure, and uncertainty about the survival of national leaders can undermine the credibility of retaliation. Survivability consequently includes political and organizational arrangements as well as weapons technology.
2.2 MAD, Minimum Deterrence, and Mutual Vulnerability
Mutually assured destruction assumes that each side remains vulnerable to retaliation despite attempts to protect its territory or destroy an opponent's arsenal. It does not require identical numbers of weapons. A state with substantially fewer warheads may still threaten unacceptable damage if its weapons can survive an attack and reach targets the adversary considers sufficiently valuable.
Minimum deterrence follows a related but narrower logic. Rather than maintaining numerical parity with a stronger adversary, a state may seek sufficient forces to make aggression prohibitively costly. The threshold for credible retaliation depends on political expectations, the destructive capabilities available, and the defenses maintained by an adversary. No universally agreed number of nuclear weapons establishes an adequate minimum deterrent.
The distinction between counterforce and countervalue targeting is relevant here. Counterforce strategies concentrate on an adversary's military capabilities, particularly nuclear forces and supporting infrastructure. Countervalue strategies have historically emphasized cities, population concentrations, industrial centers, and other assets whose destruction would impose severe societal and economic costs. The categories describe strategic targeting concepts; they do not establish whether a particular target is lawful under international humanitarian law.
Mutual vulnerability can also create incentives to seek technological advantages. Missile defenses, precision-strike capabilities, and intelligence improvements may appear attractive because they promise to reduce the damage an adversary could inflict. Yet systems intended to strengthen one state's security can weaken another state's confidence in its retaliatory forces. An opponent that fears losing its second-strike capability may expand its arsenal or change its deployment posture, generating further strategic competition.
3. The Cold War Origins of Nuclear Strategy
The emergence of nuclear weapons changed military strategy during the closing stages of the Second World War. The United States used atomic bombs against Hiroshima on August 6, 1945, and Nagasaki on August 9, demonstrating a destructive capacity far beyond that of ordinary conventional explosives. These attacks also established the humanitarian consequences against which subsequent theories of nuclear warfare would be assessed.
In The Absolute Weapon: Atomic Power and World Order, published in 1946, Bernard Brodie argued that the principal purpose of military establishments had changed with the arrival of atomic weapons. Preventing war acquired unprecedented strategic importance because a conflict involving such weapons could impose damage that military victory would not adequately compensate. His contribution helped establish deterrence as a central problem of nuclear strategy (Brodie, 1946).
The Soviet Union's first successful atomic test in 1949 ended the American nuclear monopoly. Subsequent development of thermonuclear weapons, long-range bombers, and ballistic missiles increased the destructive capacity and geographic reach of both arsenals. Competition over nuclear capabilities became closely connected to the wider 
balance of power in international relations
, although the nuclear balance could not be understood through numerical superiority alone.
American strategic doctrine developed in response to these changes. Massive retaliation, associated particularly with the Eisenhower administration and Secretary of State John Foster Dulles, emphasized the possibility of a powerful response to aggression. The Kennedy administration subsequently developed flexible response, seeking a wider range of military options rather than relying so heavily on the threat of massive nuclear retaliation. The change reflected concern that an overwhelming nuclear response to a limited provocation might lack credibility.
Arms-control negotiations eventually became part of the strategic relationship. The 1972 Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty limited specified defensive missile systems, reflecting concerns that extensive defenses could undermine confidence in retaliatory capabilities. The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks addressed aspects of the offensive nuclear competition. These arrangements did not eliminate rivalry, but they demonstrated that adversaries could recognize a shared interest in restricting particular sources of strategic instability.
3.1 The Cuban Missile Crisis and Nuclear Risk
The Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 brought the United States and Soviet Union into a confrontation over Soviet nuclear missiles deployed to Cuba. American reconnaissance revealed missile installations under construction, prompting President John F. Kennedy to authorize a naval quarantine and demand their removal. The Soviet leadership, under Nikita Khrushchev, faced mounting pressure over how to preserve its position without triggering direct military conflict.
The crisis exposed the danger of decisions based on incomplete information. American officials did not fully understand the Soviet nuclear capabilities already present in Cuba, including tactical nuclear weapons. Subsequent historical investigations established the significance of information that was unavailable to American decision-makers during the confrontation. Edward T. Toton and James Scouras examine the crisis as an example of a complex deterrence system in which intelligence gaps, military actions, and organizational failures could generate unexpected escalation (Toton and Scouras, 2019).
The confrontation was resolved through diplomacy. The Soviet Union agreed to withdraw its missiles, while the United States publicly committed not to invade Cuba. A confidential understanding also involved the subsequent withdrawal of American Jupiter missiles from Turkey. Those missiles were removed in April 1963, as documented by the US Department of State's Office of the Historian. The resolution combined military pressure, private bargaining, and political concessions that could not be reduced to deterrence alone.
The experience encouraged further measures to reduce nuclear danger. Washington and Moscow established a direct communications link in 1963, initially operating through teleprinter technology rather than a voice telephone. The Partial Test Ban Treaty was also concluded that year. The crisis demonstrated that even governments with strong reasons to avoid nuclear war can approach catastrophic escalation when military preparations, uncertain intelligence, and political confrontation interact.
4. Extended Deterrence and the Nuclear Umbrella
Extended deterrence occurs when a nuclear-armed state seeks to prevent aggression against another country by extending the protection of its military capabilities. Unlike deterrence directed exclusively at attacks against national territory, it requires an adversary to consider the possibility that attacking an ally could provoke retaliation by a nuclear power.
The United States provides extended nuclear deterrence to NATO allies and maintains related security commitments in East Asia, particularly toward Japan and South Korea. These arrangements are frequently described as a nuclear umbrella. Their purpose is to strengthen allied security and influence adversaries that might otherwise calculate that military action against a non-nuclear state would involve comparatively limited risks.
Extended deterrence presents a difficult credibility problem. Would a nuclear power risk devastating retaliation against its own territory to defend another country? The answer cannot be established simply by examining alliance declarations. Geography, strategic interests, deployed forces, domestic politics, and the nature of the attack influence whether the protective commitment appears credible.
Alliance commitments can also affect proliferation incentives. States that trust a nuclear-armed ally's protection may have less reason to develop independent nuclear weapons. Conversely, doubts about the reliability of external guarantees may encourage demands for alternative security arrangements. The relationship is not automatic, because domestic politics, technological capabilities, historical experience, and regional rivalries also shape nuclear choices.
4.1 NATO Nuclear Sharing and Allied Decision-Making
NATO provides an institutional example of extended nuclear deterrence. The Alliance identifies nuclear forces as a central component of its deterrence and defense posture, alongside conventional military forces and missile defenses. Its nuclear capabilities are principally associated with the United States, while the United Kingdom and France maintain independent national nuclear forces that contribute to Allied security (NATO, 2026b).
The 
North Atlantic Treaty of 1949
 establishes collective-defense commitments but does not require automatic nuclear retaliation. Article 5 obliges each member responding to an armed attack against an ally to take such action as it deems necessary, including the possible use of armed force. The choice of specific military measures remains subject to national decision-making.
NATO's Nuclear Planning Group provides the principal forum for consultation and decision-making on the Alliance's nuclear deterrence policy, planning, capabilities, and exercises. All NATO members except France participate. Nuclear-sharing arrangements also involve American nuclear weapons stationed in Europe and the contribution of allied aircraft capable of undertaking nuclear missions. NATO's September 2026 policy statement confirms that the United States retains absolute control and custody of its forward-deployed nuclear weapons (NATO, 2026b).
These arrangements illustrate the distinction between collective nuclear planning and national authority over nuclear employment. Consultation and coordinated military preparations help communicate political commitment, but NATO institutions do not independently command the national nuclear arsenals of member states. The strategic purpose is to make adversaries take the possibility of a collective response seriously before aggression occurs, even though the decisions governments would make during an actual crisis cannot be known with certainty.
5. Deterrence Stability and Nuclear Escalation
Strategic stability concerns the conditions under which nuclear-armed adversaries lack strong incentives to initiate a nuclear attack. It is closely associated with confidence that retaliatory forces would survive, but it also depends on political perceptions, crisis-management arrangements, and the broader military relationship between states.
Analysts commonly distinguish crisis stability from arms-race stability. Crisis stability concerns whether confrontation creates incentives to strike first rather than risk losing military capabilities. Arms-race stability concerns whether states perceive strong incentives to expand or modify their arsenals in response to an opponent's capabilities. A nuclear relationship may be relatively stable in one dimension while generating competition in another.
The security dilemma complicates both forms of stability. A government may strengthen its forces to protect itself, while an adversary interprets the same preparations as evidence of offensive intentions. The resulting countermeasures can increase mutual insecurity even if neither side initially seeks war. This problem connects nuclear strategy to the broader study of 
international security
, where military capabilities and political perceptions frequently interact in ways neither government fully controls.
Deterrence stability is not equivalent to the absence of conventional conflict. Nuclear powers may continue to threaten one another, compete through military deployments, or become involved in limited hostilities. The relevant question is whether their nuclear relationship discourages escalation toward catastrophic warfare or introduces additional pressures that make escalation more likely.
5.1 First-Strike Incentives and False Warnings
A first-strike incentive arises when a government believes that attacking before its adversary acts could produce a decisive strategic advantage. Such incentives are particularly dangerous when nuclear forces or their command systems appear vulnerable. If political leaders fear that waiting would deprive them of the ability to retaliate, they may face pressure to make decisions under extreme uncertainty.
Launch-on-warning arrangements seek to preserve the ability to respond after detecting an incoming attack but before the attack reaches its targets. This differs from a policy of retaliating with forces that survive the initial strike. The distinction affects the time available to verify warnings, assess their accuracy, consult political authorities, and determine whether a nuclear response is justified.
Historical experience demonstrates the importance of false-warning risks. On September 26, 1983, Soviet early-warning officer Stanislav Petrov assessed an apparent American missile attack reported by a satellite-based warning system as a false alarm. Subsequent accounts established that the warning did not correspond to an actual missile launch. Petrov's decision illustrates the significance of human judgment within nuclear warning systems, although the available historical record does not establish that a retaliatory launch would necessarily have followed a different assessment (National Park Service, 2020).
The organization of nuclear command and control must reconcile competing requirements. Governments seek to prevent unauthorized or accidental use while preserving the ability to retaliate if national leadership, communications, or military facilities are attacked.
These objectives can conflict. Highly centralized authorization may reduce unauthorized-use risks but create vulnerabilities if decision-makers become unreachable. Extensive delegation may preserve operational capability at the cost of weaker political control. The effectiveness of a nuclear command system depends on how it manages these competing risks under conditions of uncertainty and extreme pressure.
5.2 Limited War and the Stability–Instability Paradox
The stability–instability paradox describes the proposition that nuclear weapons may discourage major war while allowing limited conflict to continue. If two adversaries believe that a large-scale confrontation could lead to mutual destruction, they may avoid direct strategic warfare but remain willing to undertake smaller military operations below the perceived nuclear threshold.
This possibility challenges the assumption that effective nuclear deterrence produces peace across every level of conflict. A state may believe that its opponent would hesitate to employ nuclear weapons in response to a limited conventional attack. The attacker could consequently perceive opportunities for coercion or territorial gains that would appear too dangerous if a wider war were considered likely.
India and Pakistan illustrate the difficulties associated with this logic. Both possess nuclear weapons and have experienced serious military confrontations, including the 1999 Kargil conflict. Nuclear capabilities affected the strategic environment, but their precise influence on each government's decisions cannot be established solely from the absence of full-scale nuclear war. Conventional military calculations, international diplomacy, political objectives, and uncertainty about escalation also shaped the confrontation.
The possibility of limited nuclear employment creates a further problem. Some strategies contemplate the use of nuclear weapons against selected military targets to signal resolve or prevent conventional defeat. Their advocates may regard limited options as more credible than threats of unrestricted retaliation. Critics question whether political leaders could reliably contain escalation after nuclear use begins, especially when communications, military infrastructure, and public expectations have already been disrupted.
6. Deterrence Beyond the US–Soviet Nuclear Balance
Nuclear deterrence no longer operates within the relatively concentrated strategic rivalry that dominated much of the Cold War. According to SIPRI's 2026 assessment, nine countries possessed nuclear weapons at the beginning of that year: the United States, Russia, China, France, the United Kingdom, India, Pakistan, North Korea, and Israel. SIPRI estimated their combined inventory at approximately 12,187 warheads, including retired weapons awaiting dismantlement (SIPRI, 2026).
These figures are estimates rather than a complete set of officially disclosed inventories. Transparency differs substantially between nuclear-armed states, and Israel does not publicly acknowledge possessing nuclear weapons. The United States and Russia continue to hold the largest arsenals, but their strategic calculations increasingly involve other nuclear powers.
China presents an additional major-power deterrence relationship, while regional rivalries in South Asia and Northeast Asia create different patterns of vulnerability and escalation. A strategy designed primarily around two superpowers cannot be transferred unchanged to every nuclear confrontation. Political objectives, alliance commitments, geographic proximity, and differences in nuclear capabilities affect how deterrence operates.
The distinction between the five nuclear-weapon states recognized under the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty and the broader group of nuclear-armed countries is legally significant. Article IX(3) of the treaty defines a nuclear-weapon state by reference to the manufacture and explosion of a nuclear weapon or other nuclear explosive device before January 1, 1967. This classification determines a state's position within the treaty regime; it does not mean that other countries lack nuclear weapons.
Different nuclear relationships also overlap. American commitments to allies influence Chinese and North Korean calculations, while developments in China's arsenal affect American strategic planning. India must consider its relationship with Pakistan alongside its security concerns regarding China. Measures directed at one adversary may consequently influence the perceptions and responses of another, complicating efforts to preserve strategic stability.
6.1 Regional Nuclear Rivalries
India and Pakistan maintain nuclear forces under conditions shaped by territorial disputes, conventional military competition, and geographic proximity. Their relationship differs from the historical American-Soviet confrontation because warning times, political circumstances, and the possibility of limited conventional hostilities present particular challenges.
India's Cabinet Committee on Security publicly articulated its nuclear doctrine on January 4, 2003. The policy emphasized credible minimum deterrence and a no-first-use posture, with nuclear retaliation envisaged following a nuclear attack against Indian territory or Indian forces. It also retained the option of nuclear retaliation in response to a major biological or chemical weapons attack against India or its forces (Prime Minister's Office of India, 2003).
The qualification is significant because India's declared policy is not an unconditional commitment never to employ nuclear weapons before an adversary has used them. Its doctrine combines an emphasis on retaliatory deterrence with an expressly reserved response to certain non-nuclear attacks. Subsequent academic analysis has examined the credibility and interpretation of these commitments without establishing that India's publicly declared policy determines every possible operational decision.
Pakistan has not adopted an equivalent no-first-use commitment. Its nuclear strategy has been influenced by India's conventional military advantages and the perceived need to deter attacks that Pakistani authorities might regard as threatening national security. Specialist analyses distinguish Pakistan's declared and publicly discussed positions from operational arrangements that remain uncertain. The absence of a precisely defined public threshold for nuclear use complicates assessments of escalation.
North Korea presents another deterrence relationship. Its nuclear and missile capabilities are intended, among other objectives, to discourage attacks against the regime and strengthen its strategic position. American extended deterrence commitments to South Korea and Japan introduce additional considerations because a conflict could involve several governments with different interests and thresholds for escalation.
Conventional military operations, nuclear signaling, and uncertainty about intentions can interact in ways that make crisis management difficult. A government attempting to demonstrate resolve may find that its actions are interpreted as preparations for a larger attack. Deterrence in such circumstances depends partly on each side's ability to communicate its intentions without creating additional incentives for escalation.
China's nuclear strategy has traditionally emphasized retaliation rather than initiating nuclear use. Its November 2025 white paper reaffirmed a no-first-use commitment and described its nuclear capabilities as intended for self-defense and deterrence. These declarations establish China's official policy, but they do not independently resolve questions about operational planning or the implications of its expanding arsenal (State Council Information Office, 2025).
Assessments of China's strategic posture must distinguish stated doctrine from military capabilities and external interpretations of their potential employment. The modernization of an arsenal may affect perceptions of its survivability, effectiveness, or potential uses, but those implications cannot be determined solely from changes in weapon numbers (SIPRI, 2026).
6.2 Technology, Retaliatory Vulnerability, and Arms Control
Technological developments can change the credibility of nuclear retaliation without changing the underlying logic of deterrence. Advanced conventional precision-strike weapons may threaten military infrastructure previously considered difficult to attack. 
Missile defenses may reduce confidence in an adversary's ability to deliver retaliation, while improved surveillance can make mobile or concealed forces more vulnerable. The strategic consequences depend on the actual capabilities deployed and the opponent's assessment of their effectiveness.
Cyber operations and attacks against space-based systems raise additional concerns. Nuclear forces rely on communications, early warning, navigation, and command infrastructure that may also support conventional military operations. An attack intended to disrupt conventional capabilities could be interpreted as preparation to disable nuclear retaliation. Such uncertainty creates escalation risks even when the attacking state does not intend to initiate nuclear warfare.
Advanced decision-support technologies may improve the processing of warning information, but they also raise questions about reliability, erroneous classification, and the speed of military decisions. Their consequences cannot be determined independently of the systems in which they are employed. Human judgment, procedures for checking warnings, and the security of communications remain significant considerations in assessments of nuclear command and control.
Arms-control agreements have historically sought to manage some of these risks. Verification, limits on deployed systems, notifications, and communication arrangements can increase predictability without eliminating strategic rivalry.
The New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty, known as New START, imposed limits on specified American and Russian strategic nuclear forces and established monitoring arrangements. Its implementation nevertheless encountered serious difficulties before expiration. Russia announced the suspension of its participation in February 2023, following earlier problems involving inspections and consultations.
The United States rejected the legal validity of that suspension and identified Russian failures to comply with specific treaty obligations (US Department of State, 2023). This was the American government's legal position concerning the suspension and its consequences. The disagreement must be distinguished from the treaty's subsequent expiration, which ended its binding legal framework.
New START expired on February 5, 2026, without a legally binding successor. United Nations Secretary-General António Guterres confirmed that the United States and Russia no longer had binding bilateral treaty limits on their strategic nuclear arsenals (United Nations, 2026).
The absence of those limits does not mean either state must immediately expand its arsenal. It removes an established framework for reciprocal legal restraints at a time when strategic competition increasingly involves several nuclear powers.
7. Nuclear Deterrence and International Law
Nuclear deterrence raises legal questions because it relies on the possibility of using weapons whose effects may be catastrophic. International law distinguishes the possession of nuclear weapons, their deployment, a communicated threat to employ them, and actual use. Each form of conduct must be assessed under the rules applicable to the state and circumstances concerned.
The United Nations Charter establishes the principal legal framework governing threats and uses of military force in international relations. Article 2(4) prohibits threats or uses of force inconsistent with the Charter, while Article 51 recognizes the inherent right of individual or collective self-defense if an armed attack occurs. The Security Council also possesses powers under Chapter VII to authorize enforcement measures. These rules apply independently of the military advantages that a government believes nuclear weapons might provide.
The legality of a particular nuclear threat cannot be established merely because its stated purpose is deterrence. The threatened action must be assessed according to the rules governing the contemplated use of force. This distinction is especially important where nuclear doctrines contemplate retaliation in circumstances that might not satisfy the conditions for lawful self-defense.
The broader doctrine of 
self-defense in international law
 requires attention to necessity, proportionality, and the legal basis for responding to an armed attack. Necessity and proportionality under the law governing resort to force must be distinguished from proportionality under international humanitarian law. The former concern whether defensive force is required and whether its scale and effects remain proportionate to the legitimate defensive objective. The latter concerns whether expected incidental civilian harm from a particular attack would be excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated.
International humanitarian law also requires parties to distinguish military objectives from civilians and civilian objects, take feasible precautions, and comply with restrictions on weapons and methods of warfare that cause unnecessary suffering. Even when resort to force is lawful under the Charter, military operations remain subject to these rules.
Nuclear weapons present particular difficulties because of their blast, thermal, radiation, and potentially extensive environmental effects. Strategic necessity cannot independently displace the applicable legal requirements. Nor does the strategic classification of a target as counterforce or countervalue establish its legality. The lawfulness of an attack depends on the applicable rules and the circumstances of the contemplated operation.
7.1 The UN Charter and the ICJ's 1996 Advisory Opinion
The International Court of Justice addressed these questions in its advisory opinion on the Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons, delivered on July 8, 1996. The proceedings arose from a request by the United Nations General Assembly, rather than a contentious dispute between two states. Although the advisory opinion was not a binding judgment between litigating parties, it remains an authoritative judicial interpretation of the international law examined by the Court.
The Court considered the United Nations Charter, international humanitarian law, relevant treaties, and customary international law. In paragraph 105(2)(A), it unanimously concluded that neither customary nor conventional international law specifically authorized the threat or use of nuclear weapons. In paragraph 105(2)(B), adopted by eleven votes to three, it found no comprehensive and universal prohibition of nuclear threats or use as such under the law examined (ICJ, 1996).
The Court separately addressed the Charter. Paragraph 105(2)(C), adopted unanimously, established that a nuclear threat or use contrary to Article 2(4) and failing to meet the requirements of Article 51 would be unlawful. The legality of a threat was consequently connected to the legality of the force contemplated. A government could not make an otherwise unlawful use of force legally acceptable merely by characterizing its intended action as a conditional deterrent warning.
Paragraph 105(2)(D), also unanimous, required nuclear threats and use to comply with the international law applicable in armed conflict, particularly international humanitarian law, together with relevant treaty obligations. In its reasoning, the Court emphasized the principles protecting civilians and prohibiting unnecessary suffering. It recognized the exceptional destructive characteristics of nuclear weapons and the difficulties of reconciling their effects with humanitarian requirements.
The Court's most contested conclusion appeared in paragraph 105(2)(E). By seven votes to seven, resolved through President Mohammed Bedjaoui's casting vote, it concluded that the threat or use of nuclear weapons would generally be contrary to the rules applicable in armed conflict, particularly international humanitarian law.
Nevertheless, given the state of international law and the factual material before it, the Court could not conclude definitively whether a nuclear threat or use would be lawful or unlawful in an extreme circumstance of self-defense in which the survival of a state was at stake (ICJ, 1996).
This unresolved question did not constitute judicial authorization to employ nuclear weapons when national survival was threatened. Nor did it establish a general exception to humanitarian law. The judges' separate and dissenting opinions revealed substantial disagreement over the consequences of the applicable rules, but those individual views must be distinguished from the Court's operative conclusions.
The advisory opinion remains significant because it rejected the proposition that nuclear weapons could be placed outside the ordinary requirements of international law simply because of their strategic function. Deterrence may influence political calculations, but it does not create a general exemption from the Charter or humanitarian rules. The legality of a particular threat or use depends on the applicable obligations and the circumstances in which the conduct is contemplated.
7.2 Non-Proliferation, Disarmament, and Treaty Prohibitions
The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, opened for signature in 1968 and in force since March 5, 1970, establishes obligations concerning nuclear proliferation, safeguards, peaceful nuclear activities, and disarmament. Articles I and II restrict transfers and acquisition of nuclear weapons, while Article III establishes safeguards obligations for non-nuclear-weapon states parties. The treaty differentiates between states recognized as nuclear-weapon states under its terms and those accepting non-nuclear status.
Article VI requires every party to pursue negotiations in good faith on effective measures relating to the cessation of the nuclear arms race and nuclear disarmament, as well as a treaty on general and complete disarmament under strict and effective international control. The obligation is not confined to the five nuclear-weapon states recognized by the treaty. Its interpretation has considerable significance for assessments of continued reliance on nuclear deterrence.
In its 1996 advisory opinion, the International Court of Justice unanimously concluded that an obligation exists to pursue in good faith and bring to a conclusion negotiations leading to nuclear disarmament in all its aspects under strict and effective international control.
The Court explained in paragraph 99 that the obligation involved a defined result pursued through negotiations conducted in good faith. This conclusion does not establish a fixed deadline for disarmament or prescribe a particular numerical reduction schedule (ICJ, 1996).
The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, adopted on July 7, 2017, and in force since January 22, 2021, establishes a different set of obligations. Article 1 prohibits states parties from developing, acquiring, possessing, using, or threatening to use nuclear weapons, among other activities. It also prohibits specified forms of assistance and the stationing, installation, or deployment of nuclear weapons in territory or places under the jurisdiction or control of a state party.
These are binding treaty prohibitions for states parties, not identical obligations automatically imposed on every country. Broader arguments concerning the treaty's contribution to customary international law must be distinguished from obligations arising directly through treaty consent. The fact that states disagree about the treaty's wider legal significance does not diminish the binding character of its provisions for those that have accepted them.
The relationship between deterrence and the NPT remains disputed, particularly in relation to nuclear sharing. NATO maintains that its arrangements comply with the treaty because the United States retains custody and control of its forward-deployed nuclear weapons in peacetime and because the arrangements predate the NPT. The North Atlantic Council reaffirmed the Alliance's position on the compatibility of nuclear sharing with the treaty in April 2026 (NATO, 2026a).
Critics question whether arrangements contemplating a possible transfer of nuclear-weapons control during armed conflict can be reconciled with Articles I and II of the NPT. The disagreement concerns the interpretation of the treaty's transfer and acquisition prohibitions, the legal significance of arrangements made before the treaty entered into force, and the position that might arise under wartime conditions. NATO's institutional interpretation does not constitute a judicial determination, while the existence of criticism does not by itself establish a treaty violation.
A related disagreement concerns the role of nuclear weapons in maintaining international security. Supporters of deterrence argue that credible nuclear forces reduce the likelihood of major aggression and provide protection where conventional capabilities may be insufficient. Advocates of nuclear disarmament emphasize the catastrophic humanitarian consequences of nuclear use, the possibility of accidents or miscalculation, and the danger that reliance on nuclear protection perpetuates proliferation incentives.
These positions involve competing strategic assessments as well as legal and humanitarian arguments. International law determines the obligations applicable to states, but it cannot by itself establish whether a particular nuclear posture will successfully deter an adversary. Conversely, a government's belief that deterrence serves its security interests does not resolve the legality of threatened conduct. Evaluating nuclear deterrence requires keeping those questions distinct.
Conclusion
Nuclear deterrence seeks to prevent aggression by convincing adversaries that military action could provoke consequences too costly to accept. Its operation depends on credible threats, political resolve, communication, and the survival of sufficient retaliatory capabilities. Second-strike forces and mutual vulnerability explain why nuclear-armed adversaries may hesitate to initiate major conflict, but mutually assured destruction represents only one form of deterrence relationship.
The historical experience of the Cold War demonstrates the influence of nuclear weapons on strategic calculations, alongside the importance of diplomacy, conventional forces, and political restraint. The Cuban Missile Crisis revealed that mutual awareness of nuclear danger does not eliminate the possibility of catastrophic escalation. Regional rivalries and technological developments present additional difficulties because the capabilities and assumptions supporting one deterrence relationship may not apply to another.
The central uncertainty concerns how reliably deterrence can prevent the conduct it is intended to discourage. Nuclear weapons may influence an adversary's behavior without being used, yet the absence of nuclear war does not conclusively prove that nuclear threats prevented it. Strategic stability must also be assessed against the possibility of accidents, mistaken judgments, coercion, and escalation during conventional conflicts.
International law imposes obligations that operate independently of deterrence theory. The prohibition on unlawful threats or uses of force, the requirements of international humanitarian law, and treaty commitments concerning nuclear weapons continue to constrain state conduct. Nuclear deterrence remains a major instrument of national and alliance security, but its continued operation rests on the uncertain ability of governments and military systems to manage weapons whose actual employment could produce consequences far beyond the political objectives they were intended to protect.

    A timeline of North Carolina’s recent election law changes

    The N.C. General Assembly and state board of elections have enacted and proposed a variety of changes to North Carolina’s voting procedures.  

Daphne Stifelman
, 
Staff Writer 
                 
             •  
            
October 27, 2026
 

 

Aubrey Word
 

In the 2026 midterm elections, North Carolina voters have encountered new election procedures that affect where they vote, how they submit mail-in ballots and how early votes are counted. Here’s a breakdown of those changes:

November 2024: Griffin vs. North Carolina State Board of Elections

Associate Justice Allison Riggs defeated Court of Appeals Judge Jefferson Griffin by 734 votes for a seat on the N.C. Supreme Court in the 2024 general election. Griffin then filed protests with county boards of elections in every North Carolina county. 

Griffin challenged the eligibility of over 60,000 ballots cast by voters with allegedly incomplete registrations, overseas voters who did not include a copy of their photo ID with their ballots and overseas voters who had never lived in North Carolina. 

In April 2025, the N.C. Supreme Court ordered that voters with incomplete registrations should have their votes counted and overseas voters who did not submit photo ID had to fix their ballots. The court also discounted the votes of overseas voters who inherited N.C. residence from their families. 

The following month, Griffin 
conceded
 the race. 

December 2024: Senate Bill 382 

Senate Republicans overrode then-Gov. Roy Cooper’s veto of SB 382, which transferred administration over the N.C. Board of Elections from the governor, a Democrat, to the Department of State Auditor Dave Boliek, a Republican. It also gave the State Auditor authority to appoint members of the state board and the chairs of county boards of elections.

State and county boards of elections conduct elections, operate polling sites and handle other aspects of election administration. 

N.C. Rep. Allison Dahle (D-Wake), vice chair of the N.C. House Election Law Committee, and N.C. Rep. Renée Price (D-Caswell, Orange) called the auditor’s authority to make appointments to state and county election boards a “power grab” by Republicans. 

Gov. Josh Stein filed a petition Wednesday asking the N.C. Supreme Court to overturn a lower court decision that upheld the law. 

Brooks Fuller, policy director at Common Cause North Carolina, said the law poses a risk of elections board officials adopting the same views on election laws as the State Auditor who installed them. 

Fuller said that Boliek and his department are skeptical of things like robust access to voter ID and use of different IDs to vote, early voting on college campuses and measures that would make it easier to vote by mail.

“And with that skepticism, you see significantly heightened scrutiny on voters,” Fuller said.

N.C. Sen. Jonah Garson (D-Caswell, Orange, Person) said the cure period, a time when voters can correct mistakes on their ballots, is very important in North Carolina, referencing the litigation that followed Riggs’ narrow win in 2024.

SB 382 mandated that voters have until the third business day after the election to submit documents correcting ballot deficiencies.

“The reduction of that provisional cure period is also an enormous, enormous thing in states, in a state where even statewide races regularly come down to just handfuls of those,” Garson said. 

August 2026: House Bill 958 Vetoed 

Gov. Josh Stein vetoed HB 958, which would have decreased the number of early voting days from 17 to 10. It also included a provision to allow ballots cast during early one‑stop voting to be challenged until the third day after the election. 

Should the General Assembly override Stein’s veto, the bill would expand the State Auditor’s administration over elections, requiring the auditor to randomly select counties for review of their election systems and controls after each general election, with all county boards being audited at least once every six years. 

August 2026: House Bill 834 Becomes Law

Dahle and Garson said they believe the provisions regarding vote-counting and voter ID in HB 834 stem from Griffin’s attempt to overturn the election of Riggs in 2024.

One of the changes in HB 834 requires county boards to begin counting early in-person and mail-in absentee ballots on Election Day, with each county board choosing a start time between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m.

Dahle said that delaying the counting of early ballots cast before Election Day could disadvantage Democrats, who she said tend to perform well during early voting, because it does not allow enough time to identify potential errors in the counting process. 

“You’ve added a whole bunch of extra work that needs to be done in a short amount of time. That then, if people don’t like the results, or if there’s a mistake made, I mean, it doesn’t allow you any time. And not that I know of any mistakes, but humans are humans,” she said. 

HB 834 expanded the cure period from the third business day after the election to the fifth business day. 

One of the ballot-curing provisions in the law says that if someone votes with a provisional ballot, which is used when the voter’s eligibility, registration or ID is under question, they must sign an affidavit on the ballot. If they do not, they have until the fifth day after the election to fix it. 

September 2026: Mail-In Ballot Sealing Instructions

The NCSBE updated its sealing instructions for mail-in ballots. Ballots must be inside a ballot envelope and then placed into an absentee return envelope. If either envelope is missing, unsealed or appears to have been opened and re-sealed, the county board will cancel the ballot and reissue another one. The NCSBE’s new rules also eliminate the ban on signature verification and direct county officials to verify that the signature on each absentee ballot belongs to the voter.

Common Cause reported that so far, 3.1 percent of mail-in ballots in this election have been spoiled, meaning they were marked incorrectly and a new ballot was reissued to the voter. In 2022, 2.2 percent of mail-in ballots were rejected. 

October 2026: Voters Face Relocation of Polling Sites During Early Voting

The Board of Elections in Granville County decided in June to relocate a polling site from the Public Works Complex in downtown Oxford to a location 5 miles from the town center. 

According to a letter by Southern Coalition for Social Justice urging the board to keep Oxford Public Works as an early voting site, more than 40 percent of all early voters in the county voted at the complex in 2024. 

The letter also said that a majority of Black voters use Oxford Public Works to vote early, with between 56 percent and 61 percent of Black voters using the site in every election from 2016 to 2024.

This comes after reports of the auditor’s office strategically intervening in early voting administration. In a text exchange between Larue Ulshafer, then-chair of the Granville County Board of Elections, and Dallas Woodhouse, who Boliek hired as a liaison to local elections boards, Woodhouse offered to help Ulshafer “shove our new Granville early voting plan down their throats” and “tell the Democrats where to go stick it.”

Earlier this month, students at UNC Greensboro and North Carolina A&T University, the largest historically Black college or university in the country, protested the lack of on-campus early voting sites. 

Fuller said that this change will make it more difficult for voters who rely on walking and public transit to reach the polls, many of whom are lower-income and do not have access to a personal vehicle. 

“Imagine if you had a voting site at the Student Union right in the center of campus, and then the Orange County Board of Elections came along and moved that early voting site to the Friday Center. That’s like technically still part of UNC’s campus, right? But you would never say that that’s equivalent access to campus for the vast majority of people,” Fuller said. 

N.C. Rep. Allen Buansi (D-Orange), a member of the N.C. House Election Law Committee, said that the relocation of voting sites has primarily impacted college students and elderly people who may have difficulty travelling long distances to the polls. 

“Whenever I hear about an infringement on an American citizen’s right to vote, regardless of their race, age [or] racial backgrounds, it always should be a cause for concern, and then when you do things that may disproportionately impact one direction or another, that concern becomes multiplied,” Buansi said. 

October and November 2026: Polls open 

The early voting period is from Oct. 15 through Oct. 31. Polls are open for early voting from 8 a.m. to 7:30 p.m. on weekdays, 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. on Saturdays and 12 p.m. to 5 p.m. on Sundays. On Election Day, Nov. 3, polling places will be open 6:30 a.m. to 7:30 p.m. 

During early voting, voters can vote at any early voting site in their county. On Election Day, voters must go to their assigned polling place. Any voter in line at their assigned polling place at 7:30 p.m. will be able to vote.

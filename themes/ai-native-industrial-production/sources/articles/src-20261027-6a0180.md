Boston Dynamics has individually notified South Korean component suppliers to prepare to produce parts for 10,000 humanoid robots by the end of next year as it gears up for mass production of its Atlas humanoid. Excluding Nvidia's chipsets, which serve as the robot's "brain," virtually all components will come from South Korean companies: Hyundai Mobis will make the joint actuators and hand grippers, Samsung SDI and LG Energy Solution will supply batteries, BH EVS will handle charging solutions, and CTR will produce control arms. The supply chain design reflects South Korean suppliers' mass-production cost and quality competitiveness built over decades in the auto industry, the exclusion of Chinese-made parts, and efforts to prevent technology leaks. Hyundai Motor Group plans to build a system producing 30,000 robots annually starting in 2028 and deploy them at its plant in Georgia, USA.

Boston Dynamics, the robot specialist affiliated with Hyundai Motor Group, has summoned a large roster of South Korean component suppliers ahead of mass production of its Atlas humanoid robot. With individual notices sent to suppliers to prepare to produce components for 10,000 units by the end of next year, the contours of the humanoid supply chain have effectively been finalized.

According to industry sources on the 27th, Boston Dynamics has finalized its Atlas component suppliers and notified each company the previous day. Purchase orders (POs) are expected to be issued soon. In this supply chain, with the exception of Nvidia's chipsets, which serve as the robot's brain, virtually all components will be handled by South Korean companies. Hyundai Mobis will manufacture the joint actuators and hand grippers, which account for more than half of a robot's cost. Hyundai Mobis will lead production of the headlamps and the main cameras that serve as the robot's eyes, with Namuga, IEL and others supplying alongside. Samsung SDI and LG Energy Solution will supply the batteries, BH EVS will handle charging solutions, and CTR will take on the control arms. The component makers plan to begin expanding production lines immediately.

Boston Dynamics will establish a production system capable of making 30,000 robots annually, including Atlas, starting in 2028. The robots produced will first be deployed at Hyundai Motor Group's Metaplant America (HMGMA) in Georgia, USA, to perform tasks such as parts sorting. Atlas is currently being trained at a robot training center in the same region.

Behind the selection of South Korean suppliers is the mass-production capability they have built over decades supplying parts to the automotive industry. A humanoid robot contains 5,000 to 10,000 parts per unit, so quality, cost and delivery management determine market competitiveness. Automobiles and robots overlap 60-70% in production processes and core technologies, allowing motor, reducer and controller technologies used in actuators to be repurposed directly. This is also why Hyundai Motor Group built the supply chain around first-tier partners that have already completed its verification. CTR has supplied chassis parts to Hyundai and Kia since its founding in 1971, while Namuga is a company that has made camera modules for Samsung Electronics smartphones. In the stock market on this day, Namuga hit its daily limit, while CTR and IEL rose 12-16%.

Cost control is also a key variable. Amanda McMaster, chief financial officer (CFO) of Boston Dynamics, recently said in a media interview that managing costs while rapidly scaling up is Atlas's strategy. The market-cited price for Atlas is around $130,000 per unit, five to six times higher than Tesla's Optimus ($20,000-$30,000) and Unitree's H2 ($29,900). That means securing price competitiveness is essential to capturing the market, so the role of the South Korean supply chain in lowering component costs is expected to grow.

Excluding Chinese-made components is another pillar of the supply chain design. Controllers, which issue real-time motion commands to each joint motor, are a representative category dependent on China; according to the Korea International Trade Association, imports of Chinese-made controllers totaled $316.965 million in January-July this year, up 14% from a year earlier. Chinese-made products also rank first in the force and torque sensor category at $2.571 million. China effectively monopolizes 90% of rare-earth refining and 94% of permanent magnets, key materials for robot motors. In the global market as well, Chinese-made products accounted for 86.6% of the roughly 22,000 humanoids shipped in the first half of this year.

U.S. regulatory moves are also encouraging the exclusion of Chinese-made parts. In March, the U.S. Congress introduced the "American Security Robotics Act" to block government purchases of robots from adversary nations, and in June it proposed the "Adversarial Robotics Proliferation Prevention Act" targeting imports as well. To supply Atlas to the U.S. market, Chinese-made components must be removed. Preventing technology leaks was also a consideration. Boston Dynamics handed its design drafts only to its group affiliate Hyundai Mobis, choosing to keep core technologies within the group and outsource only mass production.

South Korea's component industry sees a new growth path opening up. With Hyundai Motor Group planning annual production of 30,000 units starting in 2028, there is ample room for additional orders. "In the end, humanoids, like automobiles, are a battle over how cheaply and uniformly you can make them in large volumes," an industry official said. "For South Korean component makers that have met quality and delivery targets in the automotive supply chain for decades, this will be a new opportunity." Some note, however, that Atlas's high price point could hinder demand expansion in the early stages of mass production.

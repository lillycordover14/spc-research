From Straight-Ahead Jazz to Funk Fusion, Christian McBride & Ursa Major Covers New Ground on Points of Light (ALBUM REVIEW)

			
				By 
Jim Hynes

The prolific bassist, bandleader, producer, jazz ambassador, and “all things jazz” 
Christian McBride
 is constantly busy, whether leading one of his many bands, hosting podcasts or radio shows, or serving as Artistic Director at multiple events. He’s shown some patience, though, with this new project: leading a band of emerging contemporary jazz musicians under the banner Ursa Major, which he formed four years ago. The band has been gigging for that time, honing their chemistry before going into the studio to record this debut, 
Points of Light
. 

The resulting quintet is drummer 
Savannah Harris
, saxophonist 
Nicole Glover
 (tenor, soprano), guitarist 
Ely Perlman
, and pianist 
Mike King
 (piano, synths, Rhodes, organ)—young musicians McBride selected for their flexibility, fearlessness, and individuality. Harris brings the swing, funk, and freedom McBride was seeking. Glover came to his attention through the musicians’ grapevine as “one of the most heralded voices in jazz.” Perlman caught McBride’s ear at the Jazz Aspen Snowmass summer program, where he serves as Artistic Director, and King emerged when Glover and Harris unanimously recommended him among potential pianists. The album features original contributions from every band member, with the sole cover—George Duke’s 1992 single “No Rhyme, No Reason”—arising spontaneously from conversations in the control room.  McBride plays both acoustic and electric bass, sometimes on the same track. 

McBride, himself a reputable producer, turned to multi-instrumentalist, singer, rapper, and producer, 8-x GRAMMY-nominated, and fellow genre-hopping 
Terrace Martin
, who captured the band’s live feel in the studio before adding a layer of studio effects alongside GRAMMY-winning producer and multi-instrumentalist 
Quintin Gulledge
, without interfering with the sound, vibe, and camaraderie the group had forged onstage. Having heard this band live a few times, Martin brings an inexplicable missing ingredient that takes the sonics to a whole different level. His production work is masterful and remarkably cohesive, with sonics running from acoustic (2), hybrid acoustic-electric (2), funk (1), and fusion (3). At times this makes it uneven in balancing individual versus collective expression, but the latter generally wins out. McBride usually shuns the spotlight, functioning as a collaborator rather than an attention-getting leader. 

The program begins with Glover’s lone composition, the fusion-oriented “Sakura.” McBride plays both basses, King plays multiple keys, Martin steps in on Kong Kronos, and Glover leads with flowing tenor lines in an ethereal, meditative piece without a tangible rhythm, setting a mysterious mood without hinting at what follows. Accordingly, a scintillating hybrid and a nasty funk piece follow. King’s “Two Check” opens with Glover’s sweet melody, followed by McBride plucking his acoustic in trademark fashion. King delivers a blistering, structured acoustic piano solo before dueting with McBride. Harris ups the ante on the kit before the ensemble takes it out vibrantly. Perlman’s “Cows” is a funky jam led by McBride on electric bass, with in-the-pocket Harris, King on Rhodes and organ, Glover blowing aggressively, and Perlman tearing up the frets. Gulledge underpins the funk on clavinet, heard most effectively supporting McBride’s filthy bass solo. 

Three McBride compositions follow.  The acoustic mid-tempo ballad “Same Folks” has a cogent melody helmed by Glover and King. While one senses the solos could stretch out longer, the band favors restraint over showmanship and prioritizes a collective effort. Further evidence lies in the post-bop “Familiar Steps,” where each member solos freely but economically in one of the album’s more straight-ahead, traditional pieces. His “A Morning Story” goes more in a hybrid, contemporary direction, with Perlman taking a lyrical solo over Martin’s King Kronos. All others play acoustic instruments, making the piece a delicious blend of both.  Likewise, Martin (Mini Moog) and Gulledge (Oberheim) augment King’s primarily acoustic “Orquieda,” with Glover and King restating the melody.

The lone cover and closer, George Duke’s “No Rhyme, No Reason,” bookends the album as a moody, fusion-oriented ballad. It features Glover (soprano saxophone), McBride (acoustic bass), and King (piano, organ, and synths), with Martin chiming in on Vocoder and Oberheim synth, leaving us in a mood similar to Glover’s opener.  The album is put together like a sandwich with plenty of meaty, rollicking grooves within the bookends. It will grow on you and, to these ears, surpasses expectations.

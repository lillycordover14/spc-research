GUEST COLUMN: Will we ever again recognize musical genius?

                                                
    
                                                By
            
Guest writer

Oct 28, 2026 8:30 am

· Updated Oct 28, 2026 8:30 am

Editor's note: All opinions, columns and letters reflect the views of the individual writer and not necessarily those of the IDS or its staffers.

I am a poor pianist, and certainly not a composer. I wrote one rather unremarkable musical piece, which has never been performed in public. But I have spent a lifetime loving music, listening to it and learning about the extraordinary people who created it. Johann Sebastian Bach, Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, Ludwig van Beethoven, Stephen Sondheim, Burt Bacharach, Brian Wilson, Townes Van Zandt and the Beatles have all enriched my life beyond measure. Their music has provided not merely entertainment, but a continuing source of wonder at the remarkable capacities of the human imagination.

Recently, several friends have begun sending me musical compositions, including one so-called “tango opera,” created using artificial intelligence. One friend's daughter, who generated one of these pieces, even works for a very large AI company. I do listen, and some of these compositions are quite good. Certainly not Bach or Mozart, but pleasant, sometimes inventive and occasionally impressive. The melodies are coherent, the harmonies sophisticated and the arrangements surprisingly accomplished. Yet, these compositions have left me increasingly troubled.

Today, while listening to the Beatles' “
Across the Universe
,” written by John Lennon, I began thinking about the extraordinary human imagination necessary to create such a beautiful song. Its melody, lyrics and haunting repetition of "Jai guru deva om" reflect something remarkable about Lennon's creative mind. The song possesses a quality that is difficult to describe and perhaps impossible to explain. It seems at once simple and profound, intimate and universal. Its beauty is undeniable, but part of its wonder lies in knowing that another human being conceived it.

And that led me to a disturbing question: Will I ever again know whether someone possesses the extraordinary talent necessary to create beautiful music?

AI can already generate convincing melodies, harmonies, arrangements and lyrics. Its capabilities will undoubtedly improve with time. Soon, if not already, someone with little musical training may produce a composition indistinguishable from the work of a gifted songwriter. One might enter a prompt into a computer, select among several results, make minor adjustments and present the finished composition as an original work. To the listener, the distinction between genuine musical invention and technological production may be impossible to discern.

Of course, beautiful music remains beautiful regardless of origin. A melody that moves me does not become less moving merely because a machine generated it. Indeed, AI may introduce millions of people to the pleasures of musical creation, allowing individuals without formal training to express ideas they otherwise could never translate into music. That is no small achievement, and I would not dismiss it.

But appreciating a work of art and admiring the human genius responsible for it are two different experiences.

When I listen to Bach, I marvel not merely at the music, but at the mind capable of imagining it. When I hear Wilson's “
God Only Knows
,” I admire the remarkable musical ingenuity that brought it into existence. When I listen to Sondheim, I am astonished by the marriage of language, melody, wit and psychological insight. These are encounters with human genius, not simply pleasing arrangements of sounds.

Part of our appreciation comes from understanding that such achievements were not inevitable. Someone had to imagine what had never existed before. Mozart could have written a different melody. Lennon might never have conceived “Across the Universe.” Wilson might never have discovered the harmonies that made his finest compositions so extraordinary. Their creations emerged from particular human minds, shaped by experience, emotion, discipline, intuition, and perhaps something that deserves the mysterious designation of “genius.”

AI complicates this relationship between creator and audience. If a computer can produce a thousand beautiful songs in an afternoon, what becomes of our admiration for the person who presents one of them? How do we distinguish the gifted composer from the skillful operator of sophisticated software? And if we cannot make that distinction, will we gradually lose the habit of recognizing exceptional musical talent?

Perhaps future composers will document their creative processes. Perhaps audiences will place greater value on musicians who demonstrate their abilities through live performance, improvisation and the unmistakable evidence of individual artistry. Perhaps entirely new conventions will emerge to distinguish human compositions from machine-generated ones. Yet none of these possibilities completely resolves the uncertainty.

I do not fear that AIwill destroy beautiful music. Quite the contrary, it may produce an abundance of it, perhaps more than humanity has ever known. My concern is not that we will cease hearing extraordinary compositions, but that we will cease knowing when an extraordinary human being has created one.

That would be a profound cultural loss. For music is more than sound, and genius is more than the production of something beautiful. It is evidence of what the human mind, in its rarest moments, can accomplish. I would hate to live in a world where we could still hear another “Across the Universe” but could no longer recognize the genius of the person who imagined it.

Scott C. Clarkson is an IU alumnus from the class of 1979 and now serves as a federal judge.

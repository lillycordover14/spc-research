OPINION | India’s response to the two-front challenge: Deterrence on land, integration across domains

China’s pressure along the LAC and Pakistan’s confrontation along the LoC are not isolated military problems. India needs a sustained strategy that combines credible land power, technological superiority, air-land integration and maritime leverage to deter aggression without allowing either adversary to dictate the terms of escalation

Lt Gen Philip Campose (Retired)

Highlights

India faces a combined strategic threat where China and Pakistan can reinforce each other and exploit crises on separate fronts

China utilizes calibrated peacetime coercion and infrastructure development along the LAC to alter ground conditions

India must prioritise joint war-fighting systems, selective technological superiority, and enhanced air and maritime power for effective two-front planning

(File) Indian army soldiers patrol along the highly militarized Line of Control that divides Kashmir region between India and Pakistan, in Akhnoor sector

India’s security problem is not just the two separate land borders which neighbours contest as disputed. It is one combined and strategic system of pressure, in which China’s assertiveness along the Line of Actual Control (LAC) and Pakistan’s confrontation along the Line of Control (LoC) can reinforce each other. India must therefore build a military posture that can deter coercion on both fronts, retain the ability to fight if deterrence fails, and prevent either adversary from exploiting a crisis on the other.

The LAC and LoC present different operational problems. China brings scale, infrastructure, industrial depth and technological modernisation to a disputed Himalayan frontier. Pakistan combines a capable conventional military with cross-border terrorism, infiltration and nuclear-backed escalation risks. But their combined effect is greater than the sum of their parts: Chinese-origin equipment, defence assistance and possible intelligence support strengthen Pakistan, while a Pakistan-centred crisis could distract India from Chinese pressure in Ladakh or the northeast.

India’s traditional response—forward deployment, border infrastructure, diplomacy and calibrated military action—remains necessary. It is no longer sufficient. The eastern Ladakh crisis of 2020 and Operation Sindoor in May 2025 demonstrated that modern deterrence rests on intelligence, precision strike, air defence, electronic warfare, unmanned systems, resilient logistics and the integration of land, air, maritime, space and cyber capabilities.

The central question for Indian strategy is blunt: can India prevent China and Pakistan from exploiting each other’s actions, while remaining militarily effective if simultaneous crises develop?

China: Coercion without war

China’s challenge along the LAC begins with the absence of a mutually agreed, delineated boundary. Divergent perceptions of the line have long produced patrol friction. But Beijing has shown a willingness to repeatedly use military pressure not merely to defend its claims, but to alter conditions on the ground.

The 1962 war exposed India’s lack of preparedness at that time for high-altitude warfare. For decades afterwards, relative stability encouraged a belief that border mechanisms, diplomatic engagement and economic ties would contain rivalry. Doklam in 2017 challenged that assumption. Eastern Ladakh in 2020 destroyed it. And Arunachal Pradesh continues to draw media attention because of salami-slicing efforts amid a renaming onslaught.

The Galwan clash of June 2020, in which 20 Indian soldiers were killed, was a strategic rupture as much as a tactical tragedy. It demonstrated that even a crisis marked by disengagement talks could produce deadly violence, and that China was prepared to accept severe political costs to change the military reality along the frontier.

China’s advantage is not simply numerical. It lies in the combination of roads, railways, airfields, logistics hubs, surveillance networks and the ability to concentrate forces faster. The People’s Liberation Army (PLA) can also bring long-range firepower, cyber operations, electronic warfare and unmanned systems into a border confrontation. Its industrial base gives it a capacity to regenerate forces and munitions that India may find difficult to match.

The more likely danger is not a full-scale Chinese invasion. It is calibrated coercion during peacetime: patrol assertiveness, infrastructure construction, restricted access, incremental positioning and attempts to create new facts before India can respond. This is salami-slicing by other means—gains small enough to avoid war, but cumulative enough to weaken India’s position.

The October 2024 agreement on patrolling arrangements in Depsang and Demchok, followed by disengagement and the resumption of patrolling, was significant. It restored access consistent with pre-2020 practice and reduced immediate friction.  But it is not a boundary settlement, nor a guarantee against renewed coercion. Disengagement manages a crisis; it does not resolve the underlying strategic competition.

India must therefore ensure that any attempt by China to change the ground situation meets timely recognition, rapid reinforcement and a credible response. Roads, tunnels, bridges, advanced landing grounds, protected logistics and communications matter—but only if they produce faster mobilisation, sustainable deployment and usable combat power.

Pakistan: Violence below the threshold

The Western Front poses a different problem. Pakistan’s strategy has long combined conventional military capability with proxy violence, infiltration, and the calculated use of nuclear risk to constrain India’s response.

Kargil in 1999 revealed failures in surveillance and assumptions about Pakistani intent. The 2001–02 mobilisation, the 2016 surgical strikes and the 2019 Balakot operation followed a familiar cycle: terrorist violence creates domestic and strategic pressure for Indian action; Pakistan seeks to limit that action by raising the spectre of wider war.

The April 2025 Pahalgam attack and India’s Operation Sindoor marked a further stage in this cycle. India struck terrorist infrastructure and demonstrated a willingness to impose costs beyond the immediate site of an attack. The subsequent hostilities underlined the changing character of conflict in South Asia: that missiles, drones, precision weapons, integrated air defence and electronic warfare are no longer adjuncts to war; they are central to it.

Pakistan should not be underestimated. Its air force, missile forces, drone capabilities and air-defence systems have been strengthened substantially through Chinese-origin equipment and technological cooperation, as also defence cooperation with other countries like Turkey. Its nuclear arsenal complicates Indian escalation calculations, especially if conventional operations threaten assets Pakistan regards as critical.

India’s objective must therefore be precise: make terrorism an increasingly costly instrument of Pakistani state policy, without allowing an avoidable escalation to damage India’s wider strategic position. That requires an intelligence-led counter-infiltration grid, persistent surveillance, counter-drone capability, responsive reserves and the ability to conduct limited, politically authorised operations with clear military and termination objectives.

When two fronts converge

The two-front threat need not take the form of a simultaneous Chinese and Pakistani invasion. The more plausible danger is a sequence of connected crises.

China could increase LAC activity while India is engaged with Pakistan, forcing New Delhi to divide attention, air assets, intelligence resources and reserves across distant theatres. Pakistan could intensify infiltration or initiate a limited confrontation while China applies pressure in Ladakh. Neither action requires a formal military alliance to impose serious costs.

Four developments make this convergence especially consequential.

First, coordinated strategic pressure. Parallel action can achieve what formal coordination might: it stretches Indian forces, complicates political decision-making and creates uncertainty about where the next escalation will occur.

Second, Chinese military assistance to Pakistan. Pakistan’s dependence on Chinese combat aircraft, missiles, drones and air-defence systems gives Beijing direct influence over the military threat India faces on its western front.

Third, intelligence and technological support. Chinese technological and equipment assistance to Pakistan was clearly evident during Operation Sindoor. The precise extent of real-time intelligence or operational support may be difficult to establish from public information, but the possibility must shape Indian planning.

Fourth, battlefield learning. A conflict with Pakistan offers China a window into Indian weapons performance, tactics, electronic-warfare practices and command systems. Pakistan, in turn, can use Chinese technical assistance to improve its equipment and operational methods.

India must therefore plan against the combined effects of two adversaries, even without evidence of a joint operational plan. The issue is not merely whether India has enough troops on both borders. It is whether pressure on one front degrades its ability to act decisively on the other.

Peacetime is also a military contest

Periods of calm can be deceptive. China can use them to improve infrastructure, logistics, surveillance and mobilisation. Pakistan can replenish weapons, strengthen air defence and deepen military ties with Beijing. Peace does not pause the competition; it changes its form.

India must treat peacetime preparedness as an active element of deterrence.

Along the LAC, satellites, drones, ground sensors, electronic intelligence and human sources must provide continuous awareness of Chinese deployments, construction and logistics. India cannot respond to what it discovers too late.

Along the LoC, intelligence-led surveillance and close coordination among the Army, border forces and security agencies must target infiltration routes, launch pads, drone corridors and terror infrastructure. The threat is no longer confined to armed infiltrators; it includes unmanned systems, guided munitions and rapid cross-border strikes.

Both fronts require secure communications, electronic-warfare capability, counter-drone systems, dispersed logistics and adequate reserves of ammunition, fuel and critical spares. Recent conflicts have shown that modern militaries must operate when communications are jammed, bases are targeted and supply chains are under attack.

Peace is most valuable when it is underwritten by readiness. Complacency invites probing; credible preparedness discourages it.

Five priorities

First, deny China the ability to change the status quo.  India needs sustainable forward deployments, well-equipped reserves and the capacity to reinforce vulnerable sectors quickly. The aim is not to match every PLA deployment, but to prevent Beijing from securing a local advantage before India can respond. All-weather infrastructure, protected logistics, reliable communications and winter stocks are indispensable.

Second, preserve credible conventional deterrence against Pakistan.  India requires strong defensive formations, flexible offensive options and a modern combination of infantry, armour, artillery, Army aviation, engineers, air defence, drones and precision munitions. Military options must be tied to clear political objectives, escalation assessments and termination conditions. Nuclear coercion should not be allowed to eliminate all conventional options; neither should India assume escalation can be controlled automatically.

Third, build a genuinely joint war-fighting system. Land operations depend on air power for surveillance, mobility, air superiority and precision strike. The Navy can protect sea lanes, project power and impose maritime costs. These capabilities must be connected through joint planning, interoperable communications, common intelligence architectures and realistic tri-service exercises.

Theatre commands should be designed around operational logic, not institutional convenience. Integrated targeting, joint logistics, a common operational picture and unambiguous command responsibility are essential if India is to use scarce resources effectively across two theatres.

Fourth, pursue selective technological superiority. India cannot win an unrestricted numbers race with China. It should seek advantage in artificial intelligence-enabled decision support, electronic warfare, precision weapons, satellite communications, autonomous systems and integrated air and missile defence.

Drones should augment—not replace—infantry, armour, artillery and other conventional forces. Terrain still matters, especially in the Himalayas. But the side that detects, decides and acts faster will increasingly hold the initiative. Indigenous production, dependable supply chains and deep munitions reserves are strategic necessities, not industrial policy afterthoughts.

Fifth, make air and maritime power central to two-front planning. The Air Force must support both land fronts while defending Indian airspace and critical infrastructure. That demands combat-aircraft availability, airborne surveillance, tankers, precision weapons, resilient airbases and layered air defence.

The Navy offers India a strategic dimension Pakistan cannot easily match and China cannot ignore. India must protect its sea lanes and retain credible options to impose maritime costs in a wider conflict. China’s reliance on maritime trade and energy imports gives India potential leverage, though its use must be calibrated against escalation risks and possible Chinese naval reaction.

Conclusion:  Resolve without recklessness

India’s challenge is not simply to defend the LAC and LoC separately. It is to prevent China and Pakistan from exploiting their strategic relationship through coordinated pressure, military assistance or opportunistic action during a crisis.

Diplomacy, crisis communication and border-management mechanisms remain essential. The 2024 LAC disengagement shows that negotiated arrangements can reduce immediate danger – though not eliminating the permanent one. And that diplomacy is strongest only when backed by credible military preparedness.

India must avoid two errors – complacency and recklessness. Complacency must not allow incremental territorial, operational or technological losses to occur, or become the new normal. Recklessness in responding must not transform a limited provocation into a wider war that India did not seek – diplomacy must play its part.

The strategic objective is clear. Deter a two-front war. Retain the capability to fight one if deterrence fails. And deny either adversary the opportunity to exploit the other front.

That requires a land force that is mobile, protected, technologically enabled and logistically resilient; an Air Force able to operate across both theatres; a Navy able to add strategic depth; and a joint command system capable of turning national power into timely military effect. India does not need to fight two wars simultaneously. It needs to make certain that neither China nor Pakistan believes it can win by starting one. 

(Lt Gen Philip Campose (Retired) is a former Vice Chief of the Indian Army. He has authored a book titled ‘A National Security Strategy for India: The Way Forward’)

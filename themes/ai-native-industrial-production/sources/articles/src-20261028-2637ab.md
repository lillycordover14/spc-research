Japan plans to strengthen deterrence by denial in security strategy&nbsp;&nbsp;upi

[page text]
Oct. 28 (Asia Today) --
 Japan plans to make "deterrence by denial" a central element of its defense strategy, seeking to convince potential adversaries that an attack on the country would fail to achieve its military objectives.

The approach would involve expanding long-range missile and drone capabilities to repel invasions while strengthening the defense industry's ability to maintain weapons and ammunition production during prolonged conflicts.

The Yomiuri Shimbun reported Wednesday, citing multiple government officials, that Japan has begun incorporating the concept into revisions of its three principal national security documents scheduled for completion by the end of the year.

The documents are the National Security Strategy, which establishes the country's overall diplomatic and security direction, the National Defense Strategy, which outlines military priorities, and the Defense Buildup Program, which sets equipment procurement and investment plans.

Deterrence by denial aims to prevent conflict by convincing potential aggressors that committing troops and equipment would not produce the desired outcome.

To achieve this, Japan plans to accelerate the development and deployment of long-range missiles and unmanned systems capable of striking invading vessels from considerable distances.

The strategy would also focus on making it more difficult for potential adversaries to conduct military operations in waters surrounding Japan.

Another priority is establishing the capacity to produce ammunition and missiles continuously.

Even if Japan successfully repelled an initial attack, its defenses could become unsustainable if ammunition and other supplies were exhausted.

The government therefore intends to strengthen its ability to sustain prolonged military operations.

Cooperation with allies and friendly nations would also support the strategy.

By demonstrating that an attack on Japan could bring an aggressor into confrontation with several countries, Tokyo hopes to influence potential adversaries' calculations.

The approach also seeks closer strategic coordination with the United States.

According to the Yomiuri Shimbun, Washington's National Defense Strategy released in January called for strengthening deterrence by denial alongside allies and partners, particularly with China in mind.

Japan is consequently expected to deepen cooperation between its Self-Defense Forces and U.S. forces in the Indo-Pacific region.

Defense industry capacity becomes a strategic priority

Another major component of the strategy involves strengthening Japan's defense industry.

The Asahi Shimbun reported Wednesday that the Japanese government presented a proposal at a panel of experts Tuesday to support the defense industry as a means of strengthening both military and economic power.

The government wants an industrial system capable of increasing weapons production rapidly during wartime and maintaining output over extended periods.

Under the proposal, companies could produce ammunition, missiles and escort vessels for export during peacetime and redirect production toward Japan's military requirements during emergencies.

Civilian products, including drones, could also be adapted for defense purposes when necessary.

Maintaining commercial and export demand during peacetime would help sustain production facilities and skilled workers.

Japan is also considering establishing a new organization supported by the Defense Ministry and the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry.

The organization would help strengthen the defense manufacturing base, support technological innovation and assist with defense exports.

The proposal reflects a broader approach in which the ability to manufacture military equipment continuously is considered part of national defense capability, rather than focusing solely on the procurement of existing weapons.

Higher defense spending raises fiscal questions

Financing the strategy remains a major challenge.

The Asahi Shimbun reported that the government of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi intends to substantially increase defense spending in the year-end revisions.

Japan traditionally maintained defense spending at approximately 1% of gross domestic product.

In its 2022 revisions to the three national security documents, however, the government established a goal of raising combined defense-related spending to approximately 2% of GDP.

According to the newspaper, the United States is now seeking a higher level of around 3.5%.

Japan's government maintains that deterrence by denial is consistent with the country's exclusively defense-oriented policy, which limits military action to what is necessary for defense against attacks.

Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi said in a speech Friday that Japan would not strike first but needed to establish the capabilities required to counter threats facing the country.

The shift has nevertheless raised concerns about moving away from Japan's traditionally cautious approach to weapons exports toward policies intended to expand defense manufacturing and overseas sales.

Key issues in the upcoming revisions will include determining the military capabilities needed to repel invasions and sustain prolonged conflicts, along with the industrial capacity and financial resources required to support them.

Radeco, a Boston Dynamics partner that trains DHS and other agencies to operate Spot, will modify the four robots before supplying them to ICE

US robotics company Radeco, which provides unmanned robotic solutions for energy, defense and security applications, plans to supply ICE with Cerberus, a modified version of Boston Dynamics’ four-legged robot Spot. (via Radeco)

In Greek mythology, Cerberus is the three-headed dog that guards the gates of the underworld. Its eyes blaze, and its drool kills anything it touches. In pop culture, finance and the security industry, the name has become shorthand for airtight protection.
That name now belongs to a modified version of Spot, the four-legged robot from Boston Dynamics better known for playing fetch with people and looking after worker safety at industrial sites. The rebranding came with a new customer: US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), whose aggressive crackdown on immigrants under the second Trump administration has been marked by a string of deaths.
The deal has drawn protests from civic groups outside Boston Dynamics’ headquarters, where activists have demanded the company stop the sale, and hundreds of the company’s own employees have signed a petition backing that demand. It has also deepened concerns about the “weaponization” of commercial robots as AI-powered machines from the US and China, the world’s leading robotics powers, rapidly grow more capable.
Boston Dynamics did not sell the four robots to ICE directly. They were modified and named Cerberus by Radeco, a US company that provides unmanned robotic solutions for the energy, defense and security sectors. Radeco is an official Boston Dynamics partner and has spent years training the US Department of Homeland Security (DHS) and other agencies to operate Spot.
The Cerberus upgrade fits Spot with thermal imaging and 360-degree cameras; sensors that detect radiation, toxic gases and explosives; military-grade communications equipment; and a robotic arm that can open doors. According to US media reports, ICE plans to use the robots to support crackdowns on illegal immigration, smuggling and human trafficking through underground tunnels.
According to reports by Boston public radio station WBUR, The Boston Globe and tech news outlet Ars Technica, more than 300 employees of Boston Dynamics, a Hyundai Motor Group affiliate, submitted a petition to the company in September. Among other things, it cited ICE’s September 2025 raid on the construction site of a battery plant in Georgia jointly built by Hyundai Motor Group and LG Energy Solution, where hundreds of South Korean workers were detained.
“We cannot be proud of working at [Boston Dynamics] when that work is being used by a government agency that brutalizes civilians, including some of our own colleagues [. . .],” the petition read, according to WBUR.
The company’s leadership backed the Radeco-ICE deal. Amanda McMaster, then the company’s interim CEO, wrote in a letter to employees that customers must comply with the company’s terms and conditions of sale, which “prohibit any attempts to weaponize our robot or use it for purposes of harm or intimidation.”
“We believe this decision is in line with our purpose of ethically advancing intelligent machines in service of humanity,” McMaster wrote.
Boston Dynamics’ ethical principles state that it will not weaponize its robots, and that it will not partner with or authorize anyone seeking to use them as weapons. In 2022, after being acquired by Hyundai Motor Group, the company joined major US and Chinese robot makers, including Agility Robotics and Unitree Robotics, in a joint pledge against the weaponization of commercial robots.
In May, it was reported that a separate deal fell through after Boston Dynamics employees objected to a plan by a company supplying police departments to mount flash-bang grenades on Spot.
Spot has been used to search the interiors of dangerous blazes with thermal cameras alongside firefighters, look for survivors at disaster sites, open front doors in place of police officers during hostage situations, and inspect and detect explosives and biochemical agents. But those same capabilities could be used to support lethal operations on the ground, and the robot could even be modified to carry lethal weapons.
Reuters reported in September that China has been accelerating research since 2024 into converting commercial humanoid robot technology for military use.
Moon A-young, the head of the peace education group PEACEMOMO, has called for international norms on lethal autonomous weapons. She said Tuesday that Spot had already drawn controversy when it was deployed in training exercises at a French military academy in 2021.
“Current discussions on regulating lethal autonomous weapons revolve around lethality, and something like Spot sits right on that line. It doesn’t kill people directly, but when it’s deployed in environments where people could die, it can’t be exempted from regulation just because it’s called a commercial robot,” Moon said.
“With regulations lagging behind, companies and governments need institutional reviews before commercial and civilian robots are diverted into weapons,” she added.
Hyundai Motor Group said the matter had been handled by Boston Dynamics and that the group had no separate position.
Boston Dynamics is also continuing to upgrade its humanoid robot Atlas, which Hyundai Motor Group plans to deploy first on its US auto production lines in 2028. In August, Hyundai Motor Group signed a memorandum of understanding with the Republic of Korea Army and the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources at Army headquarters in Gyeryong, South Chungcheong Province, to expand the use of robots, physical AI and other advanced technologies.
By Kim Nam-il, senior staff writer

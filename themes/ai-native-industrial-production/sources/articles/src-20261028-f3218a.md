President Trump has a lot on his plate, and so too does the nation. Sunday, he took to Truth Social to address the people.

,” the president wrote at the top of his

A fireside chat it was not.
The 1,300-word-plus  screed was triggered by Friday’s episode of “Real Time With Bill Maher,” a mainstay on HBO since 2003. The comedian referred to Trump as a “lame duck” president with low approval ratings during a segment with his guest, former White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt.
Trump, 80, likely expected praise from his fellow curmudgeon Maher, 70, since the men had recently dined together at the White House. It was their second such date following a controversial get-together in March 2025, which Maher juiced for all it was worth in an effort to stand out from his fellow late-night hosts.

Maher’s chummy relationship with Trump has made him an outlier among his peers, many of whom have been in the president’s crosshairs. 
 
Advertisement
 
 
 
 
 
CBS 
canceled Stephen Colbert’s show
 after decades on the air following pressure from Trump’s Federal Communications Commission chair. Trump has bitterly complained about being treated unfairly by 
Jimmy Kimmel and Seth Meyers
. They, too, now have targets on their backs.
Maher has avoided the same fate by railing on Trump only half as much as his late-night peers, leaning into various MAGA grievances about the “woke” left and forging a fair-weather friendship with the second-term president.
If the Maher of 2003 could see himself now, he’d likely dedicate a searing monologue to the hypocrisy. The host was once positioned as an irreverent comedian whose political satire was guided by his no-BS take on Capitol Hill and American culture. Common sense over party fealty. Political correctness be damned. 
But as the political landscape has shifted, so too has Maher’s approach.
Now he plays the role of a staunchly nonpartisan crank on TV while dining with the president in person.
“Yeah, I did it again, b—!” Maher said to his audience Friday. “I know. I’m the bad guy because I go and say face-to-face to the man the things you write in articles that he’ll never read, but OK. You know what? F— you. I like these dinners.”
So brave. So calculated. 
Maher was dipping back into the attention he received in 2025, when he first dined with Trump and described the president as “gracious and measured.”
 
Advertisement
 
 
 
 
 
Their rendezvous was just the sort of thing the comedian would have mercilessly lampooned if he wasn’t in fact part of the joke. Fellow comedians such as Patton Oswalt have criticized the HBO host for placating Trump, while Larry David satirized Maher’s first date with the president in a New York Times essay titled “
My Dinner With Adolf.
”
The controversy was a boon for “Real Time,” boosting its waning relevance by putting Maher back in the news and reinforcing the tired charade that his fearlessness knows no bounds. 
This week’s public skirmish was a repeat performance, if not a somewhat pathetic display of both men’s need to agitate for attention.
Maher told his audience on Friday’s episode that he spoke with the president about deadly ICE incidents, Ukraine and how Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was “dragging” Trump down. In closing, he assured the audience he was no pushover by poking at the president’s recent favorable dealings with Russia (a diesel deal, lifting sanctions), then saying, “I could not have been treated better if I was Russian.”
Given the president’s delicate psyche, Maher knew anything short of absolute praise would be perceived as an insult. As for the knock about Trump’s slumping approval numbers? A sure trigger. 
In Sunday’s Truth Social manifesto, Trump referred to Maher as a “biased, dumb SLEAZEBAG” whose program is plagued by low ratings. The president also alleged that the comedian was drunk and had a drink in hand as he toured the White House on Oct. 15, and that Maher is a “major POTHEAD” who “looked like hell this time.”

So much for Trump’s initial comments about their meeting. He had told reporters that it was “a great dinner.” 
“Bill Maher, I tell you what, he likes me a lot more than people understand,” Trump said. “And I actually like him.”
Not unlike Trump, Maher also enjoys praise, and blames the “woke left” when he doesn’t get it.
The typically smug host appeared upset earlier this year at the 
Golden Globes
 when presenter Wanda Sykes 
roasted him
 and another nominee up for best performance in stand-up comedy on television, Ricky Gervais.
“There’s some people pissed off that a queer Black woman is up here doing the job of two mediocre white guys,” Sykes said. “... But first, I want to give them some love.” Addressing Maher directly, she said, “You give us so much. But I would love a little less. Just try less.” The camera panned to a stone-faced Maher, who appeared “butthurt” (a term he likes using to dismiss the gripes of folks he gripes about).
The bruised feelings didn’t stop there. Later, on Maher’s “Club Random” podcast, he vented about being passed over by Hollywood.

“I’ve been nominated for 33 Emmys, and they would never give it to me,” he told his guest Joel Edgerton.  “I speak freely, and this woke town f— hates that. That’s OK. I’ve made my peace with that, so I know how this goes.”
If Maher has been “
soft-canceled
” by the industry, as he claims, then Hollywood has a funny way of showing its disdain: HBO renewed “Real Time” through 2028. 
Perhaps he learned from his frenemy Trump that grievance politics sells, and it’s time to cash in.

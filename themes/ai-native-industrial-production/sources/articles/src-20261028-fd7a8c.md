Album Review: Points of Light by Christian McBride & Ursa Major
Christian McBride gives the front of his new quintet and keeps the bass chair for himself, and Ursa Major plays post-bop, funk, and synth-soul as one band that already knows how to listen to itself.
Shatter the Standards
 
and
 
Lillian Sharpe
Oct 28, 2026
1
Share
Courtesy of Mack Avenue Records.
When he was a kid, Ely Perlman studied guitar in the Jazz Aspen summer program alongside the bassist Christian McBride. The first time McBride called up Perlman for one of his bands in 2022, the guitarist was 21 and the youngest in a group the bassist had determined had to be young. Around him he had assembled Savannah Harris (27; drummer raised in Oakland by musician parents), Nicole Glover (28; a tenor player McBride’d been hearing good things about from the New York scene), and Mike King (about 29; piano player out of Theo Croker’s band). McBride is in his fifties by now, artistic director of the Newport Jazz Festival and host of 
Jazz Night in America
, and his name sells a show in its own right. What he was setting up was a practice as old as jazz: Art Blakey’s Jazz Messengers ran for decades, Miles Davis brought in Tony Williams as a teenager. The young ones learned what the music demanded of them by playing night after night next to somebody who already knew, and the knowing rubbed off. McBride stuck with this formula but placed himself at the bottom of it. He lays down the bass line, hands the melodies and most of the solos to somebody half his age. Ursa Major’s premise is that the bandstand is still a teaching tool when the teacher steps back to keep time. 
He started planning the band 
around 2018
 as an “all-in” group that could do in one band what he’s had to parcel out across Inside Straight, the New Jawn, A Christian McBride Situation, and Tip City. He asked the other four players to write tunes so they’d be writers as well as players in the band. Their first release was a 7-inch version of drummer Harris’s tune “More Is” (no solos on either side). When it came time to make a long player, McBride stayed on as associate producer but handed over the producer’s chair to Terrace Martin for 
Points of Light
, who’s made records with Kendrick Lamar, Snoop Dogg, Stevie Wonder, and Herbie Hancock. 
Melodies are Glover’s domain. She gets the first one right after McBride finishes playing solo at the top of “Two Check,” a pizzicato line on the upright bass with nothing underneath him. The rest of the band comes in and McBride drops into a walking line under the melody. Her solo is bluesy inflections and fast scalar passages over his walk and Harris’ swing ride. Mike King and Ely Perlman then have their respective solos, McBride walking underneath them all. Glover waits longer to come in on the ballad “Sakura,” floating in on a rubato piano phrase over a synthesized chord and a few low notes from some uncredited voice. When she finally arrives with the melody, she’s warm and keeps her vibrato in check. Same as the blues. The drums and bass settle into a slow ballad underneath her, and her solo grows more dynamic, louder, until Harris starts answering with fills and crashing the cymbals.
Harris’ fills grow into a second lead voice after McBride switches from the upright bass to electric bass on “Cows.” McBride’s round syncopated bass riff locks to her kick drum and backbeat while Quintin Gulledge (clavinet) and Perlman (guitar) chop out their own staccato parts that fit into one another’s gaps. She plays sixteenth notes on the hi-hat with a ghost note pattern on the snare drum. The rhythmic “pocket” stays still as Glover’s long solo ascends in register and volume; Harris then breaks free with snare chatter and rim shots and the hi-hat open, and the solo takes where the drummer pushes it. McBride told an interviewer that he could not have started his band without a drummer who “
could swing, could play free and could play funky
.” He and Harris steer “Orquieda” together on their acoustic instruments. He sets up an agile repeating figure on the upright bass with a percussive finger attack; Harris immediately locks in with soft kick drum hits and rim clicks. The solos are in the opposite order of the funk tune: Perlman’s (clean guitar) is first and Glover’s (with increasingly dense lines) is second; Harris’ accents grow louder behind her. 
Trading fours was how a young drummer learned if the horn players were listening—the two sides trading short musical phrases until each knew how the other thought. Ursa Major performs it at a fast tempo on “Familiar Steps.” Glover and Perlman play an angular theme in unison over McBride’s walking line; Glover solos, King plays quick bop lines in his right hand against syncopated chords in his left, and Perlman with linear runs and double stops. Then Harris trades four-bar phrases with the tenor, guitar and piano soloists in turn. The head returns and stops on a stop-time cadence. The same build appears on “Same Folks,” but the band never gets to trading fours. Glover and Perlman play an angular theme together; Glover’s solo pushes toward her upper register as Harris leans more on the snare drum, the liveliest stretch of the tune. 
After that, King plays bebop lines, Perlman plays a short solo and the head returns, as in any quintet, with no one trading with the soloists. King gets first word on “A Morning Story” with sparse chords on piano alone (in leisure time; no one joins him). When the band comes in, they play a slow waltz with so much air in it that Harris mostly plays brushes and taps the ride cymbal to keep time and McBride holds root notes, slipping small counter-lines under them. Glover plays a lyrical theme that runs into her solo. King takes the next solo high up on the keys (his left hand comping), and the melody itself carries the tune to its slow free ending (Glover restates the melody until it gives way to a held bass and piano chord). Martin’s Korg Kronos pads and Perlman’s comping behind the piano blend so closely that the ear can’t separate them.
The only tune Ursa Major didn’t write belongs to George Duke: “No Rhyme No Reason,” a single from his 1992 album of the same name by a pianist who came up with Cannonball Adderley’s band and Frank Zappa before he started making funk and R&B hits. The influence of producer Martin is clearest on it. After McBride plays solo on the upright, Savannah Harris lays down a slow neo-soul backbeat (snare drum and rim clicks). Then Martin’s vocoder sings Duke’s melody in a smooth, multi-voiced line (slightly bending the pitch at the ends of phrases). Glover plays the soprano saxophone (for the only time on the record), and her solo climbs and opens up. After her solo, Glover and the vocoder overlap their phrases until the drums leave. A Mini Moog, Oberheim, Prophet 5, and Mike King’s organ add a huge pad underneath it all—the keyboards melt together so completely you can’t tell one apart from the others. This is the price of letting a producer who loves synthesizers into an acoustic band. 
When Melissa McBride first heard Ursa Major, she told her husband it was his “
new CMB
.” (CMB is short for Christian McBride Band. McBride assembled it in 2000 with Ron Blake, Geoffrey Keezer and Terreon Gully.) They played the family music under his name, and the bassist is still the guy the band is named after. What are they like to lead 4 years after the band’s first gig (June 2022)? “They don’t say no to anything, which is a wonderful thing,” 
McBride said
.
Great (★★★★☆)
Favorite Track(s):
 “Cows,” “Familiar Steps,” “No Rhyme, No Reason”

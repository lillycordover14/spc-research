"Choosing growing substrate consciously"&nbsp;&nbsp;Vertical Farm Daily

[page text]
Growers across the horticultural sector are preparing to order substrates for the 2026/2027 growing season. With such a wide range of products on the market, choosing the right one requires solid knowledge, argues Simon Voogt in this contribution.
 
Simon guided many crops during his career as a crop consultant. He has since retired and returned to the Netherlands.
 
Many raw materials, including peat, stone wool, coconut coir, perlite, wood fiber, biochar, clay pebbles and compost, are offered either pure or as mixtures. Each has specific physical and chemical properties. The ratios in which they are mixed have a major influence on root system development, Simon emphasizes.
 
The root system is the foundation of any crop. The root zone requires specific parameters for each plant species, both chemical (EC, pH, macro- and micronutrients) and physical (pF value, water content % and oxygen level). "The longer the crop cycle, the more important these parameters become for root quality and, ultimately, for aboveground development and yields."

Peat
Peat has exceptional physical, chemical and biological properties for plant growth, which is why it has long been widely used. Its cation exchange capacity and water-holding capacity are very high. Its stability supports good nutrient uptake, making peat well suited as a base material for growing media.
 
In the popular press, peat use is often portrayed as unsustainable because it is said to contribute to climate change and other environmental problems, Simon notes (1).
 
European peatlands have been heavily degraded by centuries of drainage and peat extraction. Restoration techniques such as the Moss Layer Transfer Technique (MLTT) could turn these areas into new carbon sinks within a few decades. One example is the work of Kekkila BVB in Parkano, Finland (2), where reed canary grass is grown on former peat extraction sites. The grass can reach 2 meters in height. After harvest, it is shredded, heated and processed into a safe raw material for substrates.
 
Canadian peatlands remain largely untouched, the consultant stresses. "These areas currently sequester carbon faster than peat is harvested for container crops. When peat is harvested in Canada, restoration is standard practice. Long-term restoration using MLTT revives peat-forming ecosystems by improving water storage and water management." Simon concludes that peat is indispensable for horticulture.
 
In his work, Simon encountered many other substrates. He lists them below, along with their advantages.
 
Rockwool
 is made from basalt, a solidified lava, and offers an excellent balance between water and air. It has good aeration, is pH neutral and clean, and is consistent in quality.
 
Coconut coir
 is an organic product made from the husk of the coconut. It is a base material for substrates with very good, stable properties. The ratio of fine particles, long fibers and coarse particles can be adjusted according to how the substrate needs to handle water. That, in turn, depends entirely on climate (location), the chemical quality of the irrigation water, the crop and the season, the consultant explains.

Perlite
 is a natural mineral of volcanic origin that expands into lightweight, porous white granules when heated. It is added to substrate mixes to increase aeration and prevent waterlogging.
 
Wood fiber
 is produced from residues of the timber industry. When mixed in, it adds structure to the substrate and promotes drainage, which ensures sufficient air content. This benefits the oxygen supply to the roots.
 
Biochar
 is a reliable raw material for horticultural substrates, according to Simon. It is organic, contains 80% carbon and can therefore adsorb large amounts of positively (+) and negatively (-) charged ions, particularly positively charged ones. Biochar is plant-based and produced through thermochemical pyrolysis, a process in which material is heated to 300-900°C in the absence of oxygen. "That makes it completely sterile and clean," Simon stresses.
 
Compared with other substrate components, biochar is light in weight. Its good water-holding capacity also provides good aeration when mixed with materials such as peat.
 
Clay pellets, also known as hydro pellets, are another material Simon came across in the field. They are made from low-lime clay containing finely distributed organic matter, which is ground, granulated and heated to 1,200°C.
 
According to the consultant, clay pellets are indispensable for houseplants and potted plants. "The lightweight pebbles provide excellent drainage and are mostly used in decorative pots and planters. They are also used in crops such as orchids and anthurium."

Substrate tailored to the crop
Over his career, Simon saw many crops, including numerous fruiting vegetables and soft fruit. Growers do well to match their substrate closely to the crop.
 
In the Netherlands, 
tomatoes and cucumbers
 are grown for 12 months, with or without supplemental lighting. The crop cycle is long, and production is high across several seasons. This means large fluctuations in radiation, relative humidity, outdoor temperature and evaporation. The amount of water these crops need each day varies enormously, so a vital substrate with good properties is essential, Simon emphasizes.
 
"During my career as a consultant, I was able to guide very large commercial growing projects, both in the Netherlands and abroad." The following substrates worked well in those projects:
 
 
sphagnum peat mixed with perlite (30-35%).
 
pure rockwool (10-12 cm high)
 
sphagnum peat mixed with wood fibers (20-25%).
 
sphagnum peat mixed with biochar (20-25%)
 
coco fibers mixed with perlite (25-30%)
 
pure perlite in containers
 
 
The mixing ratios of all these blends depend on water quality and the climate in which the crops are grown, Simon adds. He also points to an advantage all of these substrates share: after steam disinfection, they can be reused for a second growing year.
 
For 
sweet peppers
, Simon found that a mix of coir with perlite, wood fiber or biochar is suitable. Pure stone wool also works well but is more expensive to purchase.
 
Strawberries
 can be grown well on coir. Mixing with perlite, biochar or wood fiber is not necessary, in his view. "Coir generally contains 20-25% air-filled pores," he notes (3). "Strawberries need a root system that is evenly distributed throughout the entire volume of the substrate. The roots are particularly sensitive to large swings in the water-to-air ratio."
 
Raspberries, blackberries and blueberries
 perform well in a coir- and peat-based substrate mixed with biochar or wood fiber, Simon observed among the growers he advised. Mixing ratios depend on the growing location. "Particularly hot conditions, such as in Mexico, Arizona and California, require large amounts of water per day. During peak hours with extremely high light intensity (outdoors > 1,000-1,500 watts), larger and more frequent irrigation cycles are needed. Even at night, two irrigation cycles are required."
 
Blueberries are best grown in a substrate with a root zone pH between 4.0 and 4.5, which is an acidic environment. All other soft fruit crops do well at pH levels between 6.0 and 7.2, a fairly neutral environment.
 
For the crops mentioned above, which Simon guided on large-scale projects in Mexico, Arizona and California, a mix of 70% coir and 30% chips and fibers was used. Raspberries were grown in 7.5-liter pots with 3 canes per pot, blackberries in 10-liter pots with 3 canes per pot, and blueberries in 25-liter pots with 1 plant per pot. Strawberries were grown in gutters with 2.5 liters of substrate per plant.

Medicinal crops
The pharmaceutical sector sets extremely high standards, Simon stresses. "For cannabis cultivation, my recommendation is stone wool. This substrate is completely sterile and the best option for growing high-quality, safe and effective medicinal cannabis."
 
Cannabis can be grown year-round, with or without supplemental lighting. Mother plants, as well as the vegetative and generative phases, require specific values, he notes. Depending on the season, a different EC and pH may be needed. In stone wool, these are easy to measure.
 
"Cannabis plants are extremely sensitive to powdery mildew and botrytis. Both fungi can be prevented with good climate control and an appropriate irrigation strategy, so chemical control agents are not needed," Simon says.
 
Irrigation strategy
Before investing in a new growing medium, it is important to know its physical properties. The experienced consultant advises growers to ask their substrate supplier for a pF curve and a chemical analysis (pH and EC) of the substrate. "These values are especially important at the start of a new crop. The line in the pF curve shows how much moisture is available in the substrate at a given suction pressure. pH directly affects nutrient availability and plant health. Poor pH control can lead to lower yield and quality."
 
Finally, from the start and throughout the entire crop cycle, it is important to monitor the oxygen content of the irrigation and drain water closely. This applies to every crop.

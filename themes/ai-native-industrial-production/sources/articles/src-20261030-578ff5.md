Chai Discovery's Co-Founder and CEO Joshua Meier is using generative AI to design antibodies from scratch for big pharma, after a US$400m round at US$3.8bn
Using gen AI, 
Chai Discovery’s
 Co-Founder and Chief Executive Officer, 
Joshua Meier
, is spearheading the designing of antibodies from scratch for big pharma. The move follows a US$400m funding round that valued the business at US$3.8bn. 
Historically, traditional methods for designing antibodies have generated hit rates below 0.1%, which includes earlier AI approaches. By contrast, Chai Discovery reports that its Chai-2 model achieved a 16% success rate in fully de novo design.
The figure represents roughly a hundredfold increase. The company has also stated that its successor model, Chai-3, will double that success rate.
Headquartered in San Francisco, US, the startup was founded in 2024. In July 2026, investors valued the business at US$3.8bn after a US$400m Series C round led by Index Ventures.
Our latest models can design high-affinity binders to targets that have stumped prior computational approaches
Joshua Meier, Co-Founder and CEO, Chai Discovery
Delivering de novo molecular design
Drug discovery in the pharmaceutical industry traditionally requires years spent screening physical libraries to discover a single viable candidate. Chai offers an alternative that designs computational molecules in hours.
The founding team Joshua, 
Jack Dent
, 
Matthew McPartlon
 and 
Jacques Boitreaud
 come from leading tech and AI backgrounds like OpenAI, Meta FAIR, Stripe and Google X.
The initial model from the business, Chai-1, focused on predicting molecular structure. Chai-2 was unveiled in 2025 and shifted the technology to generative design, building antibodies against defined target epitopes from scratch.
A subsequent preprint reports that these engineered antibodies have drug-like properties and hit difficult targets. Joshua explains that the technology overcomes historical limitations in computational biology.
Our latest models can design high-affinity binders to targets that have stumped prior computational approaches.
Joshua Meier, Co-Founder and Chief Executive Officer at Chai Discovery
Expanding pharmaceutical partnership agreements
The computational results attracted interest from major pharmaceutical companies during 2026. Eli Lilly signed an agreement in January 2026 after evaluating Chai designs, which led Chai to build a custom model trained on Lilly proprietary data.
In June 2026, a second agreement placed the Chai miniprotein design suite on the Lilly TuneLab platform for select biotechnology companies. Pfizer licenced the platform in June 2026 as well, embedding a version trained on internal data into research operations and gaining early access to Chai-3.
Novartis signed a partnership agreement in July 2026, which was followed by argenx. According to Fiona Marshall, President of Biomedical Research at Novartis: “AI is already reshaping how we discover medicines.”
The business model of Chai resembles enterprise software rather than traditional biotechnology. The company licences models directly into partner discovery workflows, generating in silico candidates in hours and removing the need for conventional library screening.
Financial terms for the agreements remain undisclosed as Chai has not published revenue figures.

Scaling platform deployment across pipelines

Investor backing has matched the speed of the commercial agreements. The Series C represented the third round for Chai in under a year, raising total funding to more than US$600m.

The valuation of the company rose from US$1.3bn in December 2025 to US$3.8bn in July 2026. Investors include OpenAI, Thrive Capital, General Catalyst, Menlo Ventures, Sequoia Capital and Kleiner Perkins.

Chai-3 claims better therapeutic binding, multi-specific molecule design and generalisation across target classes. The company has not disclosed clinical programmes of its own, meaning its standing rests on whether partner candidates advance through preclinical and clinical trials.

The business compressed a process that typically takes a decade into two years: three models, four major pharma partners and a US$3.8bn valuation. Its message to drug manufacturers is to design the molecule computationally first, rather than screening millions of candidates.

The next 12 months will show whether Chai-3 performs inside partner programmes and whether licensing revenue supports the valuation. If these hit rates translate into medicines, antibody discovery will look very different.

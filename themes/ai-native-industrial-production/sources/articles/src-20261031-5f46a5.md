Can New York voters figure out who’s driving the state to destruction?&nbsp;&nbsp;New York Post

[page text]
Gov. Kathy Hochul participates in a volunteer phone bank with Cait Conley, the Democratic nominee for New York's 17th Congressional District, on, Oct. 29, 2026, in Peekskill, New York.						

							James Keivom for NY Post						

In the latest sign that the Empire State’s long-term decline is turning critical, nearly half of likely voters across New York say they’re worse off financially than they were a year ago — a grim comment on the state of the state after decades of Democratic dominance.

In the 
same Marist poll
, only 13% report being better off — and the broader numbers for all voters aren’t much different.

More ugly context: Marist found back in February that a full 86% of the state’s residents believe 
New York is no longer affordable
 for an average family — with a third of the population planning to move away in the next five years, up from an already-grim 27% back in 2011.

No wonder: US News & World Report 
ranks the Empire State as 45th out of 50
 for affordability, while the nonpartisan 
Tax Foundation puts us dead last
 for tax competitiveness and the Citizens Budget Commission ranks us 
No. 1 for tax burden
.

Our cost of living consistently ranks among the highest nationally. 

And our ever-growing tax burden pays for an ever-
decreasing
 quality of public services, from schools to transit to road quality and public safety.

Meanwhile, Gov. Kathy Hochul is 
clearly signaling
 tax hikes next year to help fund Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s socialism-in-one-city, not to mention the state’s coming 
$32 billion budget hole
.

(The gov already broke her “no tax hikes” vow this year when she flipflopped to support Mamdani’s pied-à-terre gouge.)

Somehow, Albany can never find anything to cut; spending 
rose to an all-time high of $269 billion
 in the current fiscal year.

Good luck soaking the rich much more: New York’s top 1% of earners already pay 46% of state income taxes — and are 
fleeing, justifiably,
 from rulers who see them as no more than human piggy banks.

And mere middle-class folks don’t feel any more love.

The problems have accelerated on Hochul’s watch; she’s hopeless when it comes to reining in the ideologues and the special interests slowly eating New York’s future.

And no, President Donald Trump has nothing to do with the Empire State’s decline — and every candidate obsessing about him is avoiding facing New York’s challenges.

The public seems to get it — that’s why Republican challenger Lee Zeldin 
came so close to ousting Hochul
 four years ago, and why Bruce Blakeman has a shot now.

And has one despite Democrats’ huge edge in enrollment, and despite the vast campaign war chests the governor keeps building — by appeasing those special interests and by pretending she’ll protect the rest of us from the ideologues.

Tuesday will be a test not of how much New Yorkers are hurting, but of whether enough voters can connect the dots to see who’s causing their pain.

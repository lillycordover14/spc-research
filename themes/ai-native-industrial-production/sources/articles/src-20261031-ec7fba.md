Christian Mbilli
 kept coming, but Canelo Álvarez kept finding the cleaner punches, reclaiming the WBC super middleweight title by unanimous decision on Saturday in Riyadh. Canelo (64-3-2) turned the champion’s aggression into counterpunching opportunities during his first appearance since losing to 
Terence Crawford
 thirteen months earlier.

Scores of 115-112, 116-111 and 117-110 rewarded Canelo’s accuracy over Mbilli’s greater activity. Mbilli (29-1-1) had stretches of success, particularly when he kept Canelo near the ropes, but could not consistently land the power shots needed to make that pressure decisive.

The difficulty for Mbilli was apparent early. He advanced and threw combinations, yet too many punches met Canelo’s gloves or missed. Canelo worked behind his jab and answered through the openings, making shorter attacks count against the busier fighter.

Mbilli’s fourth and sixth rounds showed a more effective approach. Sustained offense reduced Canelo’s opportunities to choose his counters and forced him to spend longer defending. Those rounds demonstrated that Mbilli could compete when his pressure came with enough scoring punches.

Canelo interrupted that progress in the seventh. His jab slowed Mbilli’s approach, and a sharp right uppercut punished the champion as he moved in. Body shots and right hands gave Mbilli further reasons to hesitate.

The eighth round point deduction for the rabbit punch was a massive turning point. Canelo lobbied referee Hector Afu hard for that call, turning what might have been a competitive 10-9 frame into a 10-8 cushion. Take away that penalty, and the margin tightens immediately on every scorecard.

At the same time, Canelo’s punch output remains dangerously low for long periods. Relying on single, eye-catching counter rights and uppercuts while absorbing flurries on the gloves leaves too much to interpretation. Mbilli was relentless, and while much of his work was blocked or deflected, sustained pressure often sways ringside judges.

Scores like 117-110 gave Canelo far too much credit for merely surviving on the ropes. A scorecard of 115-113 or 114-113—factoring in the penalty—would have better reflected the physical grind of the contest and the amount of labor Mbilli forced out of the veteran.

This Startup Just Raised $100 Million to Build a Factory With No Assembly Line: And Grew Its Floor Space 20x
The hottest AI bet of the week isn’t another chatbot. It’s a 130,000-square-foot factory in California where robots, not assembly lines, do the building.
While most AI funding headlines this year have been about software, models, agents, copilots, the money is quietly moving into something you can touch. On 2 November 2026, US startup 
Foundry Robotics
 announced an oversubscribed 
$100 million Series A
 round to build autonomous factories for complex assembly.
The round was led by 
Marlinspike Partners
, with early backers 
Khosla Ventures
 and 
Red Glass Ventures
 returning, and new investors 
SE Ventures, Wing VC and Axiom Ventures
 joining, alongside existing investors Hanabi and Zero Shot Fund.
A $100 million Series A is large by any standard. For a company that makes physical things, it is a statement: investors now believe AI’s next big market is not just writing code, but building hardware.
What Foundry Robotics actually does
Foundry builds and operates what it calls autonomous factories for complex assembly. Instead of selling robots to manufacturers, it runs the factory itself, a 
manufacturing-as-a-service
 model. Customers bring a product design; Foundry’s AI-powered robotic cells assemble it in its own facilities.
The pitch is aimed squarely at the most painful part of hardware: the traditional assembly line. Conventional lines are built around a single product, take months to deploy, and are expensive to reconfigure when the product changes. Foundry says its approach flips that: flexible robotic cells that deploy quickly, adapt to different products, and scale with demand, so customers can move from design to production within weeks without financing a dedicated production line of their own.
Its software layer, 
FoundryOS
, coordinates the factory robots, production operations and cloud systems. Data generated during assembly feeds back into its AI models, which are intended to improve performance across successive production runs. The factory, in theory, gets smarter with every unit it builds.
The company serves defence and commercial industries, focusing on high-value, complex electro-mechanical products where production is constrained. Its stated applications include drones, autonomous vehicle subassemblies, wire harnesses, underwater vehicles, telecom and data centre infrastructure, ground robots, rugged computers, robot compute boxes, and satellites.
The numbers behind the raise
Round:
 $100 million Series A, described as oversubscribed
Lead investor:
 Marlinspike Partners
Facility:
 130,000 square feet in Emeryville, California
Growth:
 manufacturing footprint expanded twentyfold to reach that size
Use of funds:
 build out the Emeryville facility, accelerate deployment of AI models into real-world manufacturing, and grow robotics research, engineering and factory operations teams
Model: manufacturing-as-a-service: Foundry owns and operates the factory, combining AI-powered robotic cells with experienced manufacturing specialists
Foundry was founded by 
Adarsh Kulkarni
 and former Tesla manufacturing executive 
Emmanuel Lamacchia
. Kulkarni previously worked at Ghost Robotics, where he worked directly with the US Department of Defense deploying legged robots for military applications, a background that helps explain why defence customers are central to the story.
“Our approach is to own the entire final assembly stack and integrate intelligence at every layer: own the buildings that manufacture for our customers while we orienting them for robotics deployment,” Kulkarni said in the company’s announcement. “Our mission is to revitalize U.S. manufacturing and create more capacity to build and scale critical product assembly.”
That endorsement from the customer side is telling, too. Duane Fotheringham, President of HII Unmanned Systems, said production capacity is “a national constraint on getting unmanned systems to the fleet” and called Foundry’s approach “exactly the kind of capability the defense industrial base needs right now.” Marlinspike managing partner Mislav Tolusic pointed to the speed of execution: “assembling an impressive team, commissioning the first factory, and quickly signing customers.”
Why this round matters beyond one factory
1. Physical AI is where the capital is rotating.
Foundry is not an outlier. In the same funding week, manufacturing group Re:Build raised $2.1 billion, Bedrock Robotics raised $600 million for construction autonomy, and other industrial-automation bets landed nine-figure rounds. The pattern is clear: after years of funding software that simulates the world, investors are funding companies that operate in it. If you are building in AI, the “atoms” side of the market is suddenly the less crowded, and better funded, side.
2. Selling capacity beats selling equipment.
Foundry’s most interesting decision is not its robots; it is its business model. Selling a robot is a one-time sale with a long sales cycle. Selling factory capacity is recurring revenue that scales with customer demand, much closer to the SaaS economics founders already know. It is the same lesson from our breakdown of 
the former Microsoft engineer making $60,000 a month from 4 apps
: recurring, usage-shaped revenue beats one-off sales, whatever you are selling.
3. Flexibility is the product, not the robot.
A robot that does one task brilliantly is a commodity. A factory that can switch from drones to satellites without months of retooling is a moat. Foundry is selling the ability to change, and for hardware startups that live or die on iteration speed, that is worth more than any single machine. Founders in any industry should ask: what is the “retooling cost” in my business, and can I be the one who removes it?
4. Founder-market fit still wins rounds.
Kulkarni’s background deploying robots for the Department of Defense and Lamacchia’s Tesla manufacturing experience are not decoration. They are why a $100 million cheque at Series A is credible. Investors fund teams that have already lived inside the problem. When you pitch, your résumé is part of the product.
5. Distribution through a bottleneck.
Like Anthropic, whose growth we recently broke down, Foundry is plugging into a bottleneck its customers cannot solve alone: in this case, domestic assembly capacity for defence and industrial hardware. The fastest-growing companies do not just build a product; they own a chokepoint. Chatbase did the same thing in software, borrowing an audience instead of building one from zero.

.
The honest caveats
A good breakdown includes what is 
not
 proven yet, and Foundry’s story has real open questions. The company has not disclosed production volumes, customer contract values, or independently verified productivity improvements, according to coverage of the announcement. Its valuation was also not disclosed.

Foundry Robotics, a California startup building what it calls America's first autonomous factory for complex assembly, closed an oversubscribed $100 million Series A this week — and simultaneously acquired Avec Robotics, a robotics software company whose Sentinel platform provides real-time remote control of robots. The 
acquisition, disclosed in a legal announcement
 by advising firm Goodwin but absent from the company's own press materials, reveals a strategy more nuanced than the pure-autonomy pitch that has dominated coverage of the raise: Foundry is building a factory where AI handles standard assembly, but humans can seize control remotely when the machines encounter something novel enough to exceed current AI capabilities.
That caveat matters more than it sounds. The company's core technology — Vision-Language-Action (VLA) foundation models — represents the state of the art in making robots generalize across different assembly tasks without being explicitly reprogrammed for each one. But researchers studying VLA systems in industrial settings have documented a persistent limitation: VLA models still underperform classical controllers on precision in fixed, controlled settings, and zero-shot adaptation to genuinely new part geometries remains unsolved at production-reliability levels. Sentinel appears to be Foundry's hedge against that gap — a human-in-the-loop override for edge cases the AI cannot yet handle autonomously.
Together, the combination describes a different product than either component alone: not a fully autonomous factory and not a remotely supervised one, but a hybrid that deploys AI for everything it can reliably execute and routes exceptions to human operators in real time.
Marlinspike Partners Leads the Round
Marlinspike Partners led the Series A, a defense- and national-security-focused venture firm, with participation from early backers Khosla Ventures and Red Glass Ventures, alongside new investors SE Ventures, Wing VC, and Axiom Ventures. 
The round's full investor list
, as confirmed by Goodwin's press release and multiple independent reports, also includes returning investors Hanabi and Zero Shot Fund. The round's oversubscribed status signals investor confidence in Foundry's model at a moment when the physical AI and advanced manufacturing automation sector is attracting serious capital.
"The next era of industrial strength will belong to nations that can turn new designs into finished hardware the fastest," said Mislav Tolusic, managing partner at Marlinspike. "Defense and industrial equipment manufacturers urgently need more domestic assembly capacity, and Foundry is delivering. Their AI-powered factory offers scalable and flexible assembly capacity. We were impressed with the speed that Adarsh and Emmanuel are executing — assembling an impressive team, commissioning the first factory, and quickly signing customers."
Foundry has not disclosed its valuation following the Series A or total revenue from its manufacturing operations.
How Does VLA-Powered Assembly Actually Work?
The technical architecture behind Foundry's factory floor is worth understanding in specific terms, because the difference between what Foundry is doing and what traditional factory robots do is structural, not incremental.
Classical industrial robots — the kind that have populated auto assembly lines for decades — are programmed for one task in one physical setting under conditions locked in advance. Change the part geometry, the lighting, or the position of a feed bin, and the robot fails. According to 
Garuda Ventures' published investment thesis
, retooling a line for a new product typically costs months of engineering work and millions of dollars.
A VLA model works differently. It integrates three jointly-trained modules: a vision encoder (typically using pretrained vision transformers such as DINOv2, which is strong on spatial and geometric detail, combined with SigLIP, which handles semantic features) that processes raw camera input; a language encoder that parses a natural-language instruction — say, "assemble the motor mount on the drone airframe" — into a shared representational space alongside the visual input; and an action decoder that translates the fused representation into motor commands: joint angles, end-effector positions, gripper states. All three modules are trained jointly, end-to-end, on robot demonstration trajectories. This joint training is the breakthrough: errors that would derail a classical perception-planning-control pipeline at each handoff instead get corrected by gradient descent during training, enabling the robot to generalize to novel situations it was never explicitly programmed for.
In Foundry's implementation, these models are trained on the building blocks of electro-mechanical assembly and deployed across a grid of modular robotic "cells" via FoundryOS, the company's proprietary operating system, which 
serves as the agentic layer
 connecting the robots, the factory floor, and the cloud. Every production run feeds performance data back into the underlying models. The practical result: a cell that assembles a drone today learns to improve at that task with every run, and the same cell can be retasked to build a satellite subassembly in software — without physical retooling.
Why the Avec Acquisition Changes the Picture
The Sentinel platform, acquired from Avec Robotics in the same closing as the Series A, enables 
real-time remote control of robots
 on the factory floor. Foundry has not publicly described how Sentinel integrates with FoundryOS or under what conditions human operators take over from the VLA models.
But the acquisition's strategic logic is readable from the state of the technology. VLA models, despite their generalization advantages, are not yet reliably production-grade for every assembly edge case. A part that arrives in an unexpected orientation, a hardware variant not represented in the training data, or a precision tolerance that exceeds what the model learned from demonstration trajectories — any of these can produce an assembly error that a classical controller, programmed specifically for that task, would catch. Sentinel provides the mechanism for a human operator to intervene remotely rather than halting the production run entirely.
This hybrid architecture — autonomous where the AI is reliable, remote-supervised where it is not — is arguably more commercially viable in the near term than a purely autonomous system, because it does not require VLA models to have solved zero-shot generalization for arbitrary new parts before Foundry can take on new customers. It is also, notably, the architecture that allows the factory to keep running under human supervision while the VLA models accumulate more training data from those same supervised runs — closing the gap over time.
Filling a Defense Production Bottleneck
The market context for Foundry's raise is not subtle. North American companies ordered 
10,989 robots valued at $723.5 million
 in the third quarter of 2026, a 24.8% increase in units and 26.1% increase in value compared to the same period in 2025 — the strongest quarter of the year, according to data from the Association for Advancing Automation. Semiconductor and electronics orders led the surge at 97.6% growth; automotive components followed at 90.4%.
But Foundry is targeting a narrower and more urgent constraint than the general automation market. Defense and industrial companies can design new hardware faster than ever — the pipeline of drone programs, satellite components, autonomous vehicle subassemblies, and ground robot platforms is growing — but assembly has become the binding constraint on delivery to the fleet. The Pentagon's proposed FY2027 budget allocates an estimated $53.6 billion to $56 billion for autonomous systems, production capacity, and related capabilities. Russia is reported to be manufacturing more than 7 million small first-person-view drones in 2026; the US Drone Dominance Program, by comparison, targets approximately 300,000 drones over two years. The production gap is not a technology gap.
Duane Fotheringham, president of HII Unmanned Systems, stated the problem plainly: "Production capacity is a national constraint on getting unmanned systems to the fleet. Foundry's approach to autonomous assembly is exactly the kind of capability the defense industrial base needs right now."
Traditional contract manufacturers address this badly. They are, as Garuda Ventures observed in its 
published investment thesis
, "narrow, expensive, and designed for single-product lines." Retooling a dedicated assembly line for a new product takes months and millions of dollars — a timeline incompatible with the iteration speed of modern defense hardware development. Foundry's cell-based, AI-driven model is designed to reduce that retooling cycle to a software update.
What Does Foundry Actually Build?
Foundry operates on a manufacturing-as-a-service model: the company owns and operates the factory itself, absorbing the capital expenditure that would otherwise fall on the hardware customer. Clients bring their product designs and Foundry handles assembly, with the company claiming customers can go from design to production in weeks rather than months.
Target product categories include 
drones, autonomous vehicle subassemblies, wire harnesses
, underwater vehicles, ground robots, rugged computers, satellite components, robot compute boxes, and telecom and data center infrastructure — a mix that straddles defense and commercial markets and, notably, spans widely varying assembly tolerances and part geometries. That breadth is the central bet: that VLA models trained on assembly primitives can generalize across product categories in ways that a fixed, product-specific line cannot.
Foundry has expanded its Emeryville facility 
twentyfold to 130,000 square feet
 (approximately 12,077 square meters or 1.3 acres). The new funding will support further build-out, continued AI model deployment, and team expansion across robotics research, software engineering, and factory operations.
A Team Built for Both Sides of the Problem
Co-founder and CEO Adarsh Kulkarni was 
employee number four at Ghost Robotics
 — subsequently acquired for approximately $400 million — where he worked directly with the Department of Defense deploying legged robots for military applications. He later joined Scale AI as its inaugural Head of Robotics, building data infrastructure partnerships for robotics companies including Generalist AI and Physical Intelligence, effectively helping establish the modern third-party robotics data industry. He holds degrees in robotics from the University of Pennsylvania's GRASP Lab.
Co-founder Emmanuel Lamacchia served as the single-threaded program manager for Tesla's Model Y — 
among the world's top-selling vehicles
, produced at a rate of approximately 1.5 million units annually — giving him direct experience running high-volume, precision assembly at global scale. The broader leadership team includes veterans from Tesla's software organization and program managers with Marine, Army, and Air Force backgrounds from Raytheon and Northrop Grumman.
"We believe the factories that revive America's manufacturing sector will need to be rethought from the ground up rather than retrofitting one aspect in an existing factory," Kulkarni said 
in the company's announcement
. "Our approach is to own the entire final assembly stack and integrate intelligence at every layer — own the buildings that manufacture for our customers while orienting them for robotics deployment."
Is Fully Autonomous Assembly Ready for Defense-Grade Production?
The honest answer, embedded in the Avec acquisition, is: not entirely, not yet. VLA models represent a genuine advance in robot generalization, but the gap between generalization in research settings and production reliability in a 24-hour defense manufacturing facility remains real. Independent research presented at ICLR 2026 identified ongoing debate between discrete-token and diffusion-based action decoding for VLA systems, with neither approach having established clear superiority for high-precision industrial tasks. A well-tuned classical controller, optimized for a specific part in a specific fixture, still outperforms current VLA models on precision in fixed and controlled settings.
Foundry's hybrid model — VLA for the repeatable majority, Sentinel for the novel minority — is a pragmatic response to that gap. It also means Foundry's factory generates a proprietary data flywheel: every supervised run produces labeled robot demonstration data that trains the VLA models to handle that scenario autonomously next time, gradually expanding the autonomous envelope while keeping production running. Competitors without both the factory and the autonomous-plus-supervised architecture cannot replicate that data loop.
Broader Physical AI Wave
Foundry's raise arrives as physical AI — the application of large foundation models to real-world robotic systems — transitions from a research agenda to an industrial thesis. North American robot order momentum has strengthened through 2026; collaborative robots specifically posted 
a 56% Q3 unit increase
 versus the prior year, reaching 1,831 units valued at $63.8 million. The Association for Advancing Automation noted the market was entering Q4 with "positive momentum" across semiconductor, electronics, automotive, and life-sciences sectors.
What separates Foundry from the broader wave of physical AI investment is vertical integration combined with the defense-manufacturing use case. Rather than selling robots or software licenses to factories, Foundry is the factory. The company's claim is that owning the full stack — the buildings, the robots, the FoundryOS agentic layer, the VLA models, and now the Sentinel remote-override capability — is the only configuration that can achieve the flexibility, reliability, and continuous-improvement rate that complex electro-mechanical assembly demands at commercial speed. The Avec acquisition, whatever its public profile, makes that claim more credible by acknowledging the current technical reality: the AI is good enough to run the factory, and the humans are there to catch what it misses.
Frequently Asked Questions
How do VLA robots differ from conventional industrial robots?
Classical industrial robots are programmed for one task in one physical setup; changing the product requires months of reprogramming and retooling at significant cost. VLA (Vision-Language-Action) models are trained jointly across vision, language, and action, allowing the same robot to generalize to new assembly tasks from a natural-language instruction without explicit reprogramming. The tradeoff is precision: a classical controller optimized for a specific part in a fixed fixture still outperforms current VLA models on precision tasks under controlled conditions. Foundry's system combines VLA autonomy for high-confidence assembly with the Avec Sentinel remote-control platform as a human-in-the-loop override for edge cases.
Why can't the US military just buy from existing contract manufacturers?
Traditional contract manufacturers design assembly lines around single products and can take months and millions of dollars to retool for a new one — a timeline incompatible with the iteration speed of modern defense hardware. Additionally, much existing electronics assembly capacity is offshore, raising supply-chain security concerns for defense-grade hardware. The Pentagon's proposed FY2027 defense budget includes an estimated $53 to $56 billion for autonomous systems and domestic production capacity specifically because the bottleneck is no longer hardware design but assembly throughput. Foundry's model aims to close that gap with software-reconfigurable robotic cells that can shift products in hours rather than months.
What is the Avec Robotics Sentinel platform and why did Foundry acquire it?
Sentinel is a robotics software platform that provides real-time remote control of robots — allowing human operators to take over from an automated system when needed. Foundry acquired Avec Robotics simultaneously with closing its Series A, a fact disclosed in a legal press release by advising firm Goodwin but absent from the company's own announcement. The acquisition reveals that Foundry's factory is not a purely autonomous system: VLA models handle standard assembly, but Sentinel provides the mechanism for human remote intervention when the AI encounters assembly scenarios outside its trained reliability envelope. This hybrid approach is both a practical concession to current VLA limitations and a mechanism for generating supervised training data that progressively expands the autonomous envelope over time.
What kinds of defense and commercial hardware does Foundry build?
Foundry's stated product categories include drones, autonomous vehicle subassemblies, wire harnesses, underwater vehicles, ground robots, rugged computers, satellite components, robot compute boxes, and telecom and data center infrastructure. The breadth is intentional: Foundry's business model depends on the same robotic cells being retaskable across product types in software. The defense and commercial mix also means Foundry can maintain factory utilization across different production programs — a key economic advantage of the manufacturing-as-a-service model over a dedicated single-product line.

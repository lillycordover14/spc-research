Opinion - After AMCA Exclusion, Why HAL Must Channel Tejas Experience into Independent 6th-Gen Design Studies to Remain an Aircraft Designer&nbsp;&nbsp;Defence.in

[page text]
For over half a century, Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) has stood as the undisputed cornerstone of India’s military aviation sector. However, the shifting dynamics of India's aerospace procurement signify a profound transformation.

As New Delhi actively works to dismantle state monopolies and integrate private enterprise into high-tech defence manufacturing, HAL faces a critical juncture.

The collaborative development model envisioned for the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA) sends a resolute message: the era of automatic, exclusive design and manufacturing rights for state-owned entities is drawing to a close.

To survive not just as a manufacturer, but as a premier aerospace designer, HAL must take immediate, proactive steps toward the future.

The Ministry of Defence’s push to bring private players into fifth-generation fighter development is a necessary and logical evolution.

Global aerospace powers thrive on industrial competition and dual production lines, ensuring that a nation's aerial combat capabilities are never bottlenecked by a single company.

While this push for a diversified defence ecosystem is vital for India's strategic autonomy, it forces HAL to re-evaluate its long-term trajectory.

Currently, HAL is operating from a position of relative financial and operational comfort.

With substantial orders for the Tejas Mk1A and the impending development and production of the Tejas Mk2, the company's assembly lines will remain active well into the 2030s.

But a thriving assembly line today does not guarantee intellectual property tomorrow.

If HAL's engineering focus remains solely on fulfilling current orders and executing licensed production of foreign platforms, it risks losing its hard-earned status as an original aircraft designer.

The solution lies in HAL taking the initiative to self-fund and launch independent sixth-generation design studies.

The company does not need to immediately construct a multi-billion-dollar flying prototype. Instead, it must utilize its internal Research and Development (R&D) budgets to conceptualize the airframes, systems, and doctrines of the future.

Over the decades, the Light Combat Aircraft (LCA) programme has served as a grueling but invaluable classroom.

Through the Tejas, HAL’s engineers have mastered complex flight control laws, the integration of modern avionics, and the manufacturing of advanced composite structures.

This institutional knowledge is a national asset, but aerospace engineering is a perishable skill. If HAL’s design bureaus are sidelined from early-stage conceptualization of future platforms, that accumulated expertise will gradually degrade.

By initiating design studies for sixth-generation technologies—such as manned-unmanned teaming, optionally manned platforms, advanced stealth shaping, and artificial intelligence-driven sensor fusion—HAL can secure its place at the negotiating table for future indigenous programmes.

It will allow the company to approach the government or potential private-sector partners not as a mere manufacturing agency awaiting a blueprint, but as a technological leader offering a mature, domestically researched design.

The global aerospace industry is moving rapidly, and the gap between fifth and sixth-generation technology is already being bridged by major world powers.

For HAL, the mandate is clear. It must leverage the monumental lessons learned from the Tejas programme and channel them into the aircraft of tomorrow.

Waiting for a formal government directive or a funded mandate to begin thinking about the next generation is no longer a viable business strategy. To remain India's premier aircraft designer, HAL must start designing the future today.

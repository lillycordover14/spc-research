Pop quiz: A Massachusetts company just completed the largest 
venture capital funding round
 in the state’s history. What does it do?
Hint: It’s not artificial intelligence. And it’s not biotech, medical devices, or clean technology.
Meet Re:Build
, an engineering and manufacturing services provider, product developer, and arguably one of the hottest startups in the state.
Never heard of Re:Build? Neither had I. Unlike many venture-backed companies, it likes to stay below the radar — and above the fray of fund-raising hype.
The news:
 The Framingham company said last week that it 
raised $2.1 billion from investors
, bringing total equity invested in the startup to more than $4 billion since its founding in 2020. Re:Build declined to disclose its private market valuation.

The new funding was led by Cambridge’s General Catalyst, an early backer, and JPMorgan Chase, a first-time investor in the company.
The previous record for a Massachusetts funding round was $1.8 billion, set by Commonwealth Fusion Systems in 2021, according to PitchBook. Devens-based Commonwealth Fusion, which is developing nuclear fusion generators, 
has pulled in $4 billion
 since its launch in 2018.
Why it matters:
 Re:Build is not a typical Massachusetts startup.
Rather than chasing breakthroughs in drugs, AI, or alternative energy, Re:Build was founded to bolster the nation’s ability to build high-value advanced products in critical industries such as aerospace and defense, energy, and robotics. 
The company has acquired a series of small engineering and manufacturing firms — 13 so far — to create an ecosystem that can help customers design products around how they will be made instead of tossing a design to the manufacturing team and saying, “Here, you figure out how to mass produce this.”
“Our goal isn’t so much to bring back what was being produced offshore,” Miles Arnone, cofounder and chief executive, told me. “It’s to make sure the next generation of things are made here.”
Zoom in:
 Re:Build has 200 employees in Massachusetts and another 1,800 around the country.
The company’s 18 sites — including in Framingham, Wilmington, and Merrimack, N.H. — have expertise ranging from composites and plastics to printed circuit boards and wiring assemblies.
Arnone likes to describe the company’s network of facilities as a decentralized Shenzhen, 
the electronics manufacturing hub
 in southern China densely packed with engineers, suppliers, and factories serving domestic and international companies.
“We have very intensive manufacturing-process knowledge because we produce hundreds of millions of dollars of material every year,” he said.
Spinoffs:
 In addition to its work with customers, Re:Build will selectively incubate new businesses to meet an unfilled market opportunity.
The best known of these companies is 
Slate Auto
, now an independent spinoff that makes a no-frills electric pickup that will soon be on the market. It’s also launched companies that make lithium battery packs, drones and drone batteries, aircraft components, and medical lab equipment.
Backstory:
 Arnone grew up on Long Island and came to Cambridge in 1987 to attend MIT.
After earning an undergraduate degree in mechanical engineering, he was accepted into an MIT graduate program that trained engineers to advance America’s manufacturing prowess. It was there he met Pittsburgh native Jeff Wilke.
Arnone went on to run several small machine tool companies before moving into managing industrial turnarounds for private equity firms. He and Wilke, who rose through the ranks at Amazon to run its consumer business, decided to form Re:Build in response to four decades of American deindustrialization.
“We both had this long-standing interest in US industrial competitiveness, and unfortunately, for most of our careers, very few people cared about that,” Arnone said. “But then, when COVID happened, all of a sudden, it became kind of a cause célèbre.”
The third cofounder, British-born hedge fund manager Will Barker, was the company’s first outside investor.
Making it in Massachusetts: 
I asked Arnone why Re:Build is based in an expensive state where 
manufacturing employment
 has fallen by more than half, to about 226,000, since 1990.
“Because I came up here to go to school and never left,” he said. “And because there’s great technical talent here.”
The company produces medical devices in the state, but high energy and real estate costs, he said, make much other manufacturing impractical.
Arnone said tariffs have opened doors for some American-made products, but Re:Build won’t target them because nobody knows how long the import duties will last. Meanwhile, many of its own costs have risen.
Final thought: 
Re:Build is focused on products whose value comes mostly from know-how that competitors can’t easily copy, in sectors that will keep the country prosperous and strong. 
“What we’re trying to do is develop a manufacturing footprint that can be worldwide competitive in high labor-cost environments,” Arnone said. 
“Because frankly, we want to pay people well.”
Six years into the effort, Re:Build is off to an impressive, if low-profile, start.

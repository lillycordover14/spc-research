By his own description, Harry Cope beat his common-law partner, Brittany Sack, nearly to death on a Halifax street in 2021. A surveillance video captured the beating. He punched her roughly 20 times in the head. He kicked her in the head while she lay on the ground. He bashed her head against the ground so violently that a witness in a nearby building could hear the thwacks. Even after he was granted bail, on a condition he not contact her, police found him hiding in her bedroom closet.
Ms. Sack didn’t provide a police statement. She was not present when an Indigenous sentencing circle discussed the matter. She didn’t make a victim impact statement in Nova Scotia Provincial Court. It was a phone call not from Ms. Sack but from someone in Mr. Cope’s community that led police to her closet.
So who spoke for Ms. Sack, the victim, who like Mr. Cope is Mi’kmaw, when Mr. Cope was sentenced for aggravated assault? Parliament had already done so. In 2019, it passed a law that said when an abuse victim is vulnerable because of personal circumstances – “including because the person is Aboriginal and female” – judges must treat deterrence and denunciation as the “primary” consideration in sentencing.
And now the Supreme Court of Canada has fatally undermined Parliament’s message.
In 2023, Provincial Court Justice Christine Driscoll sentenced Mr. Cope to five years (plus eight months for repeated breaches of no-contact orders). In 2024, Nova Scotia’s appeal court reduced the sentence for the beating to three years, in a 2-1 ruling. And last month, the Supreme Court, by a 5-4 margin, upheld the three years.
Supreme Court sets sentencing framework for Indigenous intimate partner violence cases
The case of Harry Cope – and Brittany Sack – is surely one of the most important the Supreme Court has decided. It will have an enormous and tangible effect on Indigenous communities, in particular on women and children.
Federal law in 1996 mandated for the first time that judges seek alternatives to incarceration, with special regard to the circumstances of aboriginal offenders. The Supreme Court has since staked a fair bit of its credibility on giving broad effect to that 1996 law. The result is that judges in some cases, such as Mr. Cope’s, receive a long, detailed report on systemic disadvantages in an offender’s childhood, such as poverty, drug use, mental illness, family breakdown and a history of residential schools. (There was no parallel report for Ms. Sack.) An offender’s diminished responsibility entitles them to a sentencing discount – though such is the doublespeak that judges take pains to deny it’s a discount at all. In any event, the proportion of Indigenous peoples in jail has continued to rise.
We’re not in the world of 1996 any more. In 2018, the country’s inquiry into Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women and Girls said that the 1996 law was not making communities safer. Parliament listened.
The majority’s unwillingness to accept Parliament’s clear message is evident from its summary of the facts – beginning with a long review of Mr. Cope’s truly harrowing childhood. The dissenters, by contrast, who include Chief Justice Richard Wagner, began their factual summary with a detailed description of the beating.

The precious language deserves special mention. Other jurists have described the 1996 and 2019 laws as pulling in different directions. But the majority says they must work together – “harmoniously” – because both reflect the harms of colonization. “Reconciliation must therefore serve as an important lodestar in applying these provisions together,” wrote Justice Michelle O’Bonsawin, who is Indigenous. Lower-court judges will surely be baffled about what “primary” consideration now means.
Justice O’Bonsawin wrote that Justice Driscoll did not pay enough attention to Mr. Cope’s mental illness. Yet Justice Driscoll said this: “Because of his identified Gladue factors [related to his Indigenous background], his mental health issues and his major addictions issues, his moral culpability is less than someone without those factors.”
Senior courts, including the Supreme Court, usually defer to sentences set by trial judges. That’s because trial judges are the ones who hear the evidence. But the Supreme Court has now said, in effect, when it comes to Indigenous offenders and victims, do it our way. As for the balance carefully set by Parliament – with deterrence the primary consideration – that is gone.

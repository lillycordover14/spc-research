Voxel the Robot Wants to Tidy Your Home Without Looking Human&nbsp;&nbsp;Yanko Design

[page text]
A mustard-yellow body, rosy arms, and bent legs make this household robot look like it might wander out of an animated movie and ask where you keep the mugs. Its illuminated eyes sit low on a boxy torso, leaving a lot of forehead, if that’s even the right word. I like the awkward proportions. They give it character without trying to pass for a person.

Called Voxel, the robot is being developed by New York company Tuesday Labs to tackle the little jobs that keep a home from staying tidy. Put the book away. Bring over a drink. Water the plants. Remember where the wallet went. Those are the four roles outlined for the robot: Home Reset, Fetch, Tend, and Find.

Designer: 
Tuesday Labs

For now, though, Voxel is a development prototype, not a household appliance you can order for next weekend. Tuesday Labs expects to substantially redesign its hardware before delivery, and its reservation agreement says appearance, capabilities, and specifications may change significantly. That distinction matters here because the version we’re admiring is also a design proposal, not a settled promise about what arrives at your door.

Still, I’m glad the proposal has a point of view. The warm colors and exposed joints make Voxel look approachable to me, while the unapologetically mechanical body avoids the strange social expectations of a realistic human face. Tuesday Labs works with illustrators, animators, and character designers on its robots. You can see why that matters: a machine sharing your kitchen has to be tolerable company, even when it isn’t doing anything.

The hardware description is more practical than the cartoon styling suggests. Each arm has seven degrees of freedom and an actuated wrist, intended to help it maneuver around counters, shelves, and doors. The grippers have just one degree of freedom. That’s a relatively simple hand for a complicated environment, and I’d judge it by how consistently it handles everyday objects rather than how convincingly it imitates fingers.

Find is the feature that makes the premise feel especially relatable. Tuesday Labs says Voxel would tell you where it last saw a misplaced object. That’s a modest but useful distinction: remembering the wallet on the counter is different from magically knowing where it is now. I’d take a dependable last sighting over a confident guess, particularly during the familiar scramble to get out the door.

The plant-watering brief also sets a sensible scale for the ambition. I don’t need a robot to perform an elaborate dinner routine to understand the appeal of getting small chores off my list. The harder question is how much setup and supervision those chores would require. A helper that needs constant rescuing would simply create another household responsibility.

That attentive presence raises a less photogenic design issue, too. Voxel’s listed vision system includes stereo RGB cameras and wide-field vision. Before putting one in my living room, I’d want clear answers about what gets recorded, what stays on the robot, and who can access it. The published privacy policy covers the website, purchases, and reservations; it doesn’t establish those operating details for a robot inside the home.

The financial commitment is equally worth slowing down for. Reservations cost $200 and are fully refundable before an order is placed. The estimated purchase price is $20,000, not a fixed final price, and deliveries are expected to begin in 2028, potentially later. A reservation buys an opportunity to purchase, not a finished machine or a guaranteed arrival date.

At that price, charm would get Voxel my attention, but it wouldn’t settle the decision. I’d want to watch it repeat the same ordinary tasks, unassisted, in a room that hadn’t been carefully arranged for it. Keep the odd proportions and the expressive eyes. Just make the useful behavior as convincing as the character. A robot earns its place beside the couch by giving you fewer reasons to get up.

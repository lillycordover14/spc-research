Philippines Eyes Army BrahMos Deal to Expand South China Sea Deterrence

A proposed Army acquisition could extend Philippine coastal missile coverage, strengthening maritime denial while testing Manila’s targeting networks, logistics and ability to sustain dispersed operations.

(DEFENCE SECURITY ASIA) — The Philippines is moving toward a separate Army BrahMos acquisition that could extend its coastal missile network across contested maritime approaches, but the anticipated agreement remains unsigned, leaving the scale, financing and deployment schedule of the proposed expansion unresolved.

The immediate strategic issue is whether Manila can convert additional supersonic anti-ship missile batteries into a sustainable archipelagic defence network, connecting dispersed launchers, surveillance assets and command systems closely enough to threaten hostile warships while surviving potential counterstrikes.
 

A recent report indicates that the Army requirement remains active, positioning the expected purchase as a parallel procurement rather than an extension automatically covered by the Marine Corps’ existing three-battery contract with BrahMos Aerospace.
 

President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. has said the Philippines is “in the process of actually procuring more,” signalling political support for expansion without establishing that a new contract, final battery quantity or funded delivery timetable has already been approved.
 

Philippine Ambassador to India Josel F. Ignacio described Manila as “very, very happy” with its existing BrahMos systems, while indicating that further purchases remain possible and that procurement decisions ultimately rest with the defence establishment and military.

That distinction matters because the frequently discussed ambition for up to nine additional batteries would imply a substantially larger force than the two Army batteries historically associated with nearer-term planning, and neither figure constitutes a publicly confirmed new order.

The existing Marine Corps acquisition, signed on January 28, 2022, was worth approximately US$375 million and covered three shore-based anti-ship missile batteries, establishing the operational and logistical foundation against which any subsequent Army expansion must be assessed.
 
 
 
 

 
BrahMos, developed through an Indian-Russian joint venture, offers the Philippines an approximately 290-kilometre export-range missile travelling at Mach 2.8–3, creating a potential threat to surface combatants without requiring Manila to match a larger navy ship for ship.

However, missile speed and nominal range describe only part of the capability, because successful maritime engagement also depends on identifying moving targets, maintaining communications and delivering sufficiently current targeting information before a firing opportunity closes or conditions change.

The reported development therefore concerns more than additional launch vehicles, since each battery introduces requirements for trained crews, reload transport, maintenance facilities, protected storage and reliable command arrangements that determine how much combat power can actually be sustained.
 

For China, a broader Philippine coastal defence network could complicate surface operations near selected maritime approaches, although the available information does not demonstrate an ability to control the wider South China Sea or defeat every potential defensive response.

DSA’s assessment is that the strongest geopolitical consequence lies in Manila’s potential transition from a limited coastal missile presence toward distributed maritime denial, with the credibility of that transition determined by force integration rather than procurement announcements alone.

Army BrahMos Plans Face a Contract and Force-Structure Test

 
The Army’s expected purchase remains separate from the Marine Corps’ delivery programme, a distinction essential to understanding Philippine military modernisation because existing equipment deliveries cannot substantiate claims that additional Army batteries have already been purchased or entered operational service.

Earlier planning most consistently referenced two Army batteries under the Horizon 3 modernisation framework, providing a clearer near-term benchmark than larger expansion scenarios whose quantities remain dependent on negotiations, budget decisions and the formal approval of procurement authorities.

The broader Integrated Shore-Based Anti-Ship Missile System project envisages an Army–Marine Corps coastal strike effort, potentially allowing additional batteries to support geographically separated sectors while creating new demands for coordination between services responsible for overlapping maritime defence missions.
 

Reports discussing up to nine additional batteries describe an evolving requirement rather than a finalised force structure, meaning assessments of a future nationwide missile network must distinguish an aspirational procurement ceiling from a funded and deliverable military capability.

The Army activated its 1st Land-Based Missile System Battery within the Army Artillery Regiment in 2019, demonstrating that organisational preparation predates the expected agreement while also highlighting the interval between establishing a unit and providing its principal weapon system.

Creating dedicated missile units can prepare command relationships and personnel structures before equipment arrives, but their operational value remains conditional on hardware delivery, qualification training, communications integration and the support arrangements needed to sustain readiness after initial acceptance activities.

If Army batteries adopt the Marine configuration, two twin-canister launchers would provide four ready missiles per battery, while a transporter-reloader carrying four additional rounds would support a second salvo only after the necessary reloading procedures have been completed.

On that conditional basis, two Army batteries would add eight ready missiles, increasing the combined theoretical initial salvo from twelve to twenty once all three Marine batteries and both Army batteries were delivered, integrated and simultaneously available for operations.
 
 
 
 

Those figures measure launcher capacity rather than guaranteed combat output, because availability, positioning, target information and command approval would determine whether geographically dispersed batteries could contribute to the same engagement or instead cover separate sectors during a developing crisis.

No firm public value has been established for the Army agreement, and the Marine Corps’ approximately US$375 million package cannot yield a reliable follow-on price without knowing missile quantities, infrastructure requirements, training scope and the proposed logistics support provisions.

Supersonic Anti-Ship Missiles Depend on a Complete Kill Chain

The Philippine BrahMos export configuration combines approximately 290 kilometres of range with Mach 2.8–3 speed, giving coastal forces a potentially demanding threat against surface ships while making the quality of surveillance and targeting central to effective employment at distance.
 
 
 
 

Its stated guidance arrangement combines inertial navigation and satellite navigation during the midcourse phase with an active radar seeker for terminal engagement, linking the missile’s ability to reach a search area with its capacity to acquire a target independently.

The described flight profile includes a higher-altitude cruise segment followed by terminal sea-skimming, potentially bringing the missile down to approximately three to ten metres above the water as it approaches the defended ship during the final engagement phase.
 
 
 
 

Such a terminal approach can constrain a defending ship’s opportunity to detect and engage the incoming weapon, but the information provided does not establish actual detection distances, interception probabilities or performance against any particular Chinese naval air defence system.

The approximately 200–300-kilogram conventional warhead gives the missile a substantial destructive payload, although damage outcomes would depend on impact conditions, the target’s characteristics and defensive measures, preventing warhead weight alone from establishing a guaranteed result against a surface combatant.
 
 
 
 

Internal Philippine wargames reportedly produced favourable assessments against simulated Chinese guided-missile destroyers, but these remain exercise-derived findings whose relevance depends on assumptions about targeting, electronic warfare, defensive readiness and the number of weapons involved in each modelled engagement.

Claims that BrahMos was sometimes the only platform able to penetrate those simulated defences should therefore be treated as scenario-specific assessments, rather than proof of universal superiority or evidence that a comparable wartime engagement would produce the same outcome.

Joint United States–Philippine exercises have practised drone-supported targeting procedures for coastal missile engagements, addressing the practical requirement to connect remote surveillance with launch units instead of assuming that a battery’s own radar can independently exploit the missile’s entire range.
 

 
 
 
 

That sensor-to-shooter relationship becomes more demanding when targets manoeuvre or communications are disrupted, because a long-range anti-ship missile battery needs a sufficiently accurate maritime picture to distinguish a viable engagement from an outdated or inadequately identified target track.

DSA’s assessment is that additional launchers increase potential salvo density, while resilient surveillance and command networks determine whether that added firepower becomes usable maritime denial, making kill-chain integration as strategically consequential as the missile’s advertised speed or export range.

Logistics and Reloads Will Determine Sustained Coastal Firepower

The original Marine Corps package included mobile launchers, radars, command vehicles, transporter-reloaders, operator and maintainer training, and integrated logistics support tailored for tropical conditions, demonstrating that the procurement was structured around a complete battery system rather than missiles alone.

The Philippine configuration uses two missiles per launcher and two launchers per battery, a lighter arrangement than standard Indian configurations that reflects local transport considerations while placing particular importance on the availability and protection of supporting reloader vehicles during operations.

A battery’s four ready rounds could be followed by four reload missiles, but that eight-missile combat load represents two firing cycles rather than an uninterrupted salvo, making the distinction essential when assessing saturation potential or a battery’s immediate combat endurance.
 
 
 
 

Reloading also creates a regeneration requirement between engagements, linking sustained firepower to the continued availability of crews, transporters and suitable operating locations, although the provided information does not establish reload times or the size of additional Philippine missile stocks.

Mobile launchers can change position after firing, but battery mobility depends on the supporting radar, command and logistics elements remaining coordinated, since moving the launch vehicles alone would not preserve every function required to conduct another effective maritime engagement.

Road networks, vegetation and decoys could improve survivability within a dispersed posture, while missile storage, vehicle garages and transfer facilities provide the fixed infrastructure needed to maintain readiness, creating a balance between operational mobility and the continuing need for support sites.

Infrastructure in the Western Luzon area was developed between late 2024 and 2025, showing how coastal missile deployment requires construction and preparation alongside weapons delivery, with the supporting footprint helping determine where batteries can operate and how reliably they remain available.

Initial training for twenty-one Philippine Marine Corps operators and maintainers was completed in India during January and February 2023, establishing an early personnel foundation while leaving sustained proficiency dependent on subsequent domestic training, exercises and continuing technical support after delivery.
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

The first delivery arrived by Indian Air Force C-17 on April 19, 2024, while the second battery travelled by sea around April 2025, illustrating different delivery routes without establishing that either alone provides a model for wartime resupply requirements.

With third-battery delivery and integration progressing through 2026 but final acceptance not consistently confirmed, assessments should avoid treating the entire Marine force as uniformly ready, particularly when comparing nominal inventories with the number of batteries available for sustained operational deployment.

Luzon and Palawan Give Missile Deployment Its Strategic Weight

At least one Marine Corps battery has been described as operational in Western Luzon, with its public presentation in Zambales during November 2025 placing the existing coastal missile force near a strategically sensitive sector of the South China Sea.

The Zambales area provides an engagement envelope reaching toward Scarborough Shoal, approximately 200–250 kilometres away in the provided material, although geographical reach alone does not establish continuous target coverage, authority to engage or a guaranteed firing solution during a crisis.

Then-Defence Secretary Delfin Lorenzana described the original acquisition as providing “deterrence against any attempt to undermine our sovereignty and sovereign rights, especially in the West Philippine Sea,” linking the programme’s political purpose directly to Manila’s contested maritime security environment.
 

 
 
 
 

Northern Luzon preparations add another geographical dimension, with the activation of the 273rd Marine Company in March 2026 supporting further BrahMos operations near approaches associated with the Luzon Strait and Bashi Channel rather than concentrating all activity in one sector.

That northern orientation carries implications for both South China Sea pressure and potential Taiwan-contingency spillover, but the information does not establish predetermined wartime missions, rules of engagement or an automatic Philippine commitment to employ missiles in any specific regional conflict.

Palawan is discussed as a potential location for a wider future network covering Spratly approaches, although no confirmed Army basing plan in the provided material supports presenting such deployments as approved, constructed or already occupied by operational BrahMos units.

A distributed coastal defence network could force an adversary to evaluate several possible launch areas simultaneously, potentially increasing surveillance demands and complicating surface manoeuvre, while the actual burden would depend on battery availability, concealment and the reliability of Philippine targeting networks.

For Chinese surface forces, possible responses include greater dispersion, heightened air defence readiness, electronic warfare and expanded surveillance against mobile launchers, illustrating how missile deployment can alter operational planning without establishing that any single countermeasure would succeed or fail consistently.
 
 
 
 

Against coast guard or maritime militia pressure, the missiles would introduce an escalation consideration rather than automatically resolve coercion, because a conventional anti-ship strike carries different political and military consequences from the presence of civilian or law-enforcement vessels nearby.

The resulting capability is best understood as selective maritime denial across supported engagement zones, since neither the reported Army requirement nor the existing Marine batteries would by themselves provide persistent sea control throughout the wider South China Sea region.

India–Philippines Defence Ties Expand as Operational Questions Remain

For Manila, additional BrahMos batteries would support the Comprehensive Archipelagic Defense Concept announced in 2024, connecting external maritime defence, dispersed basing and long-range precision fires with a force posture designed to exploit geography rather than pursue expensive numerical naval parity.

An Army acquisition would broaden institutional responsibility for coastal strike beyond the Marine Corps, potentially increasing deployment options while requiring compatible command procedures so that separate service formations contribute to a coherent maritime defence network during rapidly developing regional security crises.

United States training and interoperability exercises can support that integration, but the missiles remain under Philippine sovereign control, making the relationship between allied assistance and national engagement decisions an essential distinction when assessing their wider strategic significance for regional deterrence.
 

 

The emerging architecture also includes rotational United States Typhon and HIMARS deployments, expanded Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement sites and discussion of possible Japanese coastal systems, although these elements should not be treated as an already unified Philippine missile command structure.

India benefits from the Philippines’ position as the first BrahMos export customer, with the existing contract demonstrating an ability to supply sophisticated coastal strike equipment and supporting New Delhi’s wider ambition to expand defence relationships through its Act East engagement.

Ambassador Ignacio’s description of the technology as “unmatched” expresses an official diplomatic assessment of deterrent value, while a balanced capability comparison must still consider the available evidence on sensors, logistics, force readiness and the defensive environment facing any deployed battery.

Interest from Vietnam and Indonesia adds a potential regional dimension to Indian defence exports, but references to a future BrahMos belt remain an analytical description of possible proliferation rather than evidence of an established multinational operational network or common targeting arrangement.

The immediate procurement milestone is therefore a signed Army agreement specifying quantity, scope and delivery obligations, followed by the less visible work of training, infrastructure and integration that would determine whether political intent produces a repeatable coastal strike capability in practice.

Until those details emerge, the defensible assessment is that two batteries remain the most consistently reported near-term expectation, while the larger nine-battery ambition and any associated procurement value remain unconfirmed elements of a broader potential expansion of Philippine coastal defence.

The strategic test for the Philippines is whether additional BrahMos purchases can create a survivable, reloadable and well-supported denial network, because regional deterrence will depend on credible operational availability across critical approaches rather than the announcement of a larger missile inventory.

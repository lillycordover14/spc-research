Von Ardenne Targets Glass Substrate Coating for Panel-Level Packaging

 Article By
                            : Stephen Las Marias                        

 Category :  Packaging
 

2026-11-04                                

                                        (0) Comments
                                    

                                    Glass handling, PVD process limits and cost of ownership are key challenges as Von Ardenne develops deposition systems for advanced packaging.                                

Von Ardenne is targeting advanced packaging on panel-level glass substrates, drawing on its experience in large-scale glass processing and vacuum deposition to develop coating solutions for glass industries, according to Stephen Strausser, CEO of Von Ardenne North America Inc.

The family-owned company, headquartered in Dresden, Germany, has about 1,300 employees worldwide. Its coating equipment serves markets including glass, photovoltaics, automotive and aerospace. The semiconductor opportunity is focused initially on advanced packaging, where glass substrates could require deposition for through-glass vias (TGVs) and redistribution layers (RDLs).

Von Ardenne’s North American operations are based in Toledo, Ohio, with other locations in China, Vietnam, Malaysia and India.

Glass supply and handling remain challenges

Stephen Strausser, CEO, Von Ardenne North America Inc.

In an interview with 
EE Times Asia
 during the SEMICON West 2026 event in San Francisco, California, Strausser identified three broad challenges for panel-level packaging: supply-chain readiness, glass handling and the ability to demonstrate economic advantages over existing approaches.

Although glass is already used in several industries, scaling supply to meet semiconductor packaging demand across multiple regions will require additional capacity. Existing glass manufacturers also serve architectural, automotive, aerospace and photovoltaic markets.

“The glass industry is not just for that. It is also automotive, aerospace, and PV. They’re going to have to adapt, because it’ll be a whole different level of requirement and demand,” said Strausser.

He noted that glass handling presents a separate challenge. Panels may be thicker than silicon wafers, but they remain brittle and can break during processing. Strausser said Von Ardenne’s experience handling glass could be useful as packaging manufacturers develop their processes.

However, the industry has yet to settle on standard panel dimensions and thicknesses. Potential formats include 300 mm and 600 mm sizes, with thicknesses ranging from 0.8 mm to 2 mm. Panel shape is also under consideration.

For Strausser, the decisive factor is whether panel-level packaging can meet technical requirements at a competitive cost. “If it is not, it is not going to happen. It doesn’t matter how nice it looks or how beautiful it sounds. If it is not cost effective, no one is going to use it.”

Initial focus on barrier, seed and RDL layers

Von Ardenne is concentrating on three deposition applications: barrier and seed layers for through-glass vias, and layers used to form redistribution lines.

These films are deposited before electroplating completes the copper fill in vias or forms the required interconnect structures. Strausser said the company is focusing on these applications because of the company’s strong experience with glass and the specific deposition processes involved.

However, he said the company is not initially pursuing every possible coating application. Strausser noted that established semiconductor equipment suppliers have extensive deposition expertise. Von Ardenne aims to differentiate itself through its combination of glass processing and deposition capabilities.

Physical vapor deposition (PVD) is attractive because it can produce dense films at relatively low cost when directional deposition is suitable. However, its directional nature can make it difficult to coat complex via profiles, including concave and re-entrant structures.

Von Ardenne proposes high-power impulse magnetron sputtering (HiPIMS), which Strausser said can produce denser films with improved adhesion for barrier and seed layers.

If future etching processes produce geometries that are difficult to coat with PVD, manufacturers may need alternatives such as plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) or atomic layer deposition (ALD). For now, the industry is assessing which processes can meet the requirements of emerging panel designs.

Equipment configurations address throughput

Von Ardenne has four equipment configurations for deposition: its Horizontal Inline Sputtering System (HISS), vertical systems, rotary systems and conventional cluster tools.

The HISS platform moves panels horizontally through multiple chambers. Its tray configuration can be adapted to different substrate sizes, allowing the system to process multiple panels in parallel. The company offers similar flexibility with its vertical and cluster-tool configurations, while rotary systems can suit selected applications.

Von Ardenne HISS horizontal coating platform. (Source: Von Ardenne)

Throughput depends partly on the panel format and deposition process. For buried seed layers, particularly in high-aspect-ratio structures, processing times can be long. Shortening deposition time may compromise throughput, so increasing the number of substrates processed simultaneously is one way to improve productivity.

Strausser said equipment configurations and chamber capacity can be adjusted to accommodate different panel sizes and processing requirements. However, the appropriate platform for high-volume manufacturing will depend on which substrate formats and process conditions the industry ultimately adopts.

Process control and qualification

Consistent coating across large panels remains a development challenge. Strausser said the industry has not yet established widespread high-volume manufacturing for panel-level packaging, leaving equipment suppliers to develop process-control methods alongside customers.

Variations in etched via profiles can affect deposition requirements from one panel to another. Von Ardenne expects improved metrology and feedback and feed-forward control to help adjust process parameters according to the characteristics of incoming panels.

Equipment must also meet applicable SEMI and European standards, along with customer-specific requirements. Particle control extends beyond the deposition chamber to panel transport between tools. Strausser said the industry will need a panel-handling and containment approach comparable to the front-opening unified pod (FOUP) used for wafers to limit contamination during transfers.

For now, Von Ardenne is working with chipmakers and packaging companies to support research and development. Its HISS platform allows customers to evaluate different panel sizes and configurations while process conditions are still being established.

The next step will be determining whether HISS is suitable for volume production or whether a vertical or cluster configuration would be more appropriate. Strausser said accommodating multiple panel formats is manageable during R&D, but maintaining that flexibility in high-volume manufacturing is more difficult.

“Ultimately, the economics of deposition will influence whether advanced panel-level packaging gains wider adoption. The cost is the biggest factor. Because again, if the world cannot show that we can do this at a cost that is tolerable, it will never be adopted. So, we have to make sure that our tool delivers an acceptable cost of ownership. Our inline platform concepts are designed for high throughput, which is a major factor for the economy,” Strausser concluded.

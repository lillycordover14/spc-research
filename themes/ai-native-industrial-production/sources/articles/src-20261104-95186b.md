Twelve Seasons in the American Without a Football Title Game: Why Has ECU Struggled to Reach and Win One?&nbsp;&nbsp;247Sports

[page text]
On Nov. 27, 2012, Chancellor Dr. Steve Ballard and Athletic Director Terry Holland spoke to a board room packed with media and other supporters to announce that ECU had received an invitation to join the Big East Conference that it had long hoped for. After decades of working to move up in college athletics, ECU had finally reached a significant milestone, an invitation to what was then an automatic qualifying conference (AQC), even if only for football. For a fan base whose passion and turnout had long rivaled those of schools in better conferences, this moment was ECU's best chance yet to showcase its football program on a broader national stage.
The jubilant moment did not last long, however, as the conference ECU had agreed to join split apart before the Pirates played a single game in it. The schools focused on basketball separated and kept the Big East name, while the schools who were football centric formed what would become the American Athletic Conference (now known as just the American Conference). ECU entered that league in July 2014 as a member in all sports.
Twelve seasons later (2014–2025), the hope of becoming a contender in the American feels like a dream deferred for the football program, while the men's basketball program has fared even worse. (This article will look primarily at ECU football; basketball, however, deserves its own analysis).
Sobering Numbers
From 2014 through 2025, ECU football has earned a 37–59 conference record, a winning percentage of just 38.5 in league play. In a conference that has ranged from eleven to fourteen football members, the Pirates have had only four winning seasons over those twelve years. That record, and a best finish that was a tie for third with USF and Tulsa, are a long way from what ECU fans and administration hoped for when ECU accepted the invitation to join the Big East in 2012. Men's basketball has struggled to an even greater degree, compiling a staggeringly bad 65–144 conference record during the same period and winning just 31.1 percent of its league games.
These numbers place ECU football below the conference's historical standard of success. Over the same twelve years, the football championship has passed among eight different programs: UCF, Cincinnati, Memphis, Houston, Temple, Tulane, SMU and Army. Four of them - Cincinnati, Houston, UCF and SMU - then left for power conferences: the first three for the Big 12 in July 2023, and SMU for the ACC in July 2024. Their departures left the race for the title more open, yet ECU has still not reached a championship game in football or men's basketball. Today the Pirates' dream of joining a power conference looks about as far away as it did twelve years ago.
What the Football Winners Had in Common: Money, Among Other Things
Winning a conference championship rarely happens by accident, and an analysis of the former American football champions over the past decade points to money and well-funded athletic budgets as the most important traits among the conference winners. The analysis also revealed that experienced coaching, a clear offensive strategy, and a quarterback who fit the system were traits most of the winners shared as well. 
Money and Recruiting
All of the American's champions except Army had larger athletic budgets than ECU did during the years they won the championship. According to the Knight-Newhouse College Athletics Database, Houston, UCF, Memphis and Cincinnati each outspent ECU on football in those seasons by roughly 48 to 135 percent. Seven of the conference champions also spent more than ECU on football coaching compensation, with annual differences ranging from about $662,000 to $3.8 million.
This advantage carried over to the recruiting trail, where the champions outpaced ECU by an average of 18.3 spots (Army again being the outlier) in the 247Sports Composite team rankings. The Pirates' recruiting average was locked in the middle of the 70s over the last decade, while former AAC champions like Cincinnati and SMU built talent bases ranked in the top 60.
Coaching, Offense and Quarterback
The same pattern showed up on the sideline and under center. Tom Herman (Houston), Scott Frost (UCF), Josh Heupel (UCF), Mike Norvell (Memphis) and Rhett Lashlee (SMU) each spent three to eleven years as offensive coordinators before becoming head coaches in the American, and Luke Fickell kept coordinator Mike Denbrock for five years at Cincinnati, so the system remained remarkably stable. These coaches brought established offensive systems with them and then found quarterbacks who fit and managed those systems with precision.  
The New American and ECU
Fortunately, the new American Conference, with teams like North Texas, UAB, UTSA and Charlotte, offers ECU a better chance to close the financial and recruiting gaps that separated it from previous American football champions. According to the Knight-Newhouse data, ECU now ranks in the top third of the conference in both football spending and total athletic budget. The recruiting gap has narrowed as well, with ECU landing in the top half of the conference, compared with the bottom half when Houston, Cincinnati, UCF and SMU were still in the league. ECU has also invested in its football facilities, including the $24.5 million Isley Indoor Performance Center, which covers 85,000 square feet, and it has committed to meeting the American's new minimum for revenue sharing with its athletes. All of these changes should make ECU more competitive than it has been in the American Conference.

Closing the Gap: What Comes Next for ECU
ECU is capable of winning the conference in football (and maybe even in men's basketball) and eventually move to a conference with greater resources. For this to happen however, it is going to take a concerted and coordinated effort to get there. Here are three ways (certainly not limited to three) for ECU to move forward and meet the goal of winning conference championships in football and men's basketball. 
Create a Vision
ECU needs a vision that everyone connected to the university can embrace and understand. It can be as simple as this: to reach the next level, ECU has to win conference championships in football and men's basketball, but doing so takes money, a cohesive team system and everyone rowing in the same direction….. The benefits of winning go far beyond hoisting a trophy or playing in a better sports conference. Studies show that winning football teams can actually help increase enrollment faster than almost any other strategy can. (A landmark Harvard Business School study reveals that a single breakout season, one that takes a football program from mediocre to great, can trigger a
 17.7 percent surge in undergraduate applications
).
Money
Money is the largest obstacle facing ECU sports, and it is a vital necessity for winning championships and joining a power conference. ECU leadership has recently talked about the amount needed to move to the next step, and now ECU needs a plan. It is time to be specific about the financial needs, set a timeline of five to ten years, and commit to an ambitious fundraising goal for revenue sharing, with a dollar figure attached. That money should go primarily toward coaching and recruiting, both of which cost more than ever now because of the current NIL realities.
Commit to Championship Traits
ECU would do well to commit to the core traits that helped propel the American Conference champions of the past decade in football, including higher pay for coaches, a clear offensive system, experienced position coaches, and a continued commitment to recruit the best players ECU can land. Although there is no formula in sports that guarantees success, learning the specifics of what other champions have done, especially when there appears to be a clear pattern, can serve as important milestones for ECU's football and men's basketball programs.
Bottom Line: Urgency Needed
Finally, there needs to be a renewed urgency about finding a way to succeed in the American Conference and to  receive an invitation to a conference with greater resources. ECU has done an outstanding job building facilities and creating spaces that athletes want to train in while on campus. Real credit should go to the leadership and donors who have dug deep to make this a reality.
Now, it is time to shift gears, with a central plan and all hands on deck, making conference championships in football and men's basketball the unabashed goal. ECU football has long been one of the university's most important windows for the world to see who it is. Sports play a vital role in making many universities more sustainable and viable than they would be without them.
 
ECU should work toward being one of these schools, as the benefits of doing so reach well beyond the University community. They also reach the region and its people in positive ways, as they have always done.

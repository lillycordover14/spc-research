    Review: At Cat’s Cradle, Julien Baker reimagined her discography

    “Listeners had been prepared for unreleased material, but even her existing tracks were amped up and sounded brand new, which amounted to a sound they would never find on streaming services.”

Chloe Nappier
, 
Staff Writer
                 
             •  
            
November 1, 2026
 

 

Lara Nadolny
 
Photo assets from Adobe Stock.
 

Last week, Memphis-based musician Julien Baker visited Cat’s Cradle for three nights as part of a tour of intimate concerts, featuring a mix of unreleased songs and fan favorites reimagined in unforgettable ways.

Night Two started with opening act Tombstone Poetry, a self-proclaimed “heartache rock” band based in Asheville. Their set was loud, intense and full of fiddle, with a distinctly Southern flavor as they sang about hunting for rainbow trout and gas station truck stops.

Tombstone Poetry was an excellent prelude for the new material Baker came prepared with. For these songs, she’s stepped out of the stripped-down, somber aesthetic that defined her previous albums like “Turn Out the Lights” and “Sprained Ankle,” and now donned a heavier sound, with distorted electric guitars and percussion the audience could feel in their throat.

Listeners had been prepared for unreleased material, but even her existing tracks were amped up and sounded brand new, which amounted to a sound they would never find on streaming services.

“Lathe,” the first song of her set and an unreleased song, started slow before Baker pushed audiences straight into the deep end. An explosion of drums and a flash of lights gave listeners clear signs to strap in.

The next two songs, “High In The Basement” and “Middle Children,” both kept that same rock sound, as Baker threw herself around onstage, strumming her Fender guitar and belting like the lyrics were being ripped out of her.

“So I press the question as you press the brake / Is the admission of defeat too hard,” Baker sings. “Do I just wave on blindly, whoever is behind me / In the suicide lane?”

After the first unreleased songs, Baker took a pause to thank the audience, saying she’s been playing the older songs on her setlist for a long time, and her work has evolved and taken on new life as her tastes changed. 

“I’m very grateful to still be able to play in front of people,” Baker said. “And for y’all to give me your time and attention.”

She jumped into the next songs, including older tracks like “Sprained Ankle,” “Even” and “Shadowboxing.” The original recordings of those songs are somber and stripped down, but these versions featured more production and a unique sound that could only be experienced as a member of a live audience.

The music was fully realized, with the distortion on her guitar cranked up high and a full band behind her. “Shadowboxing” especially took on a new life. The song was virtually unrecognizable from the original, full of a uniquely heavy sound that had the audience headbanging to a track which was previously quiet and reflective.

Towards the end of her set, Baker started “Turn Out the Lights,” one of her most popular hits. It wasn’t as heavy as other songs she played, but it stopped the show regardless. Listeners watched in awe as she belted the lyrics, and the backing instruments seemed to bend in her direction, blending together to strengthen her voice.

“When I turn out the lights, oh,” Baker wailed. “There’s no one left between myself and me.”

After one more track, she started her encore, “Everybody Does.” The version you would find on streaming is an acoustic reflection on being destructive, pushing people away and isolating yourself. On stage, the song was kicked up to a whole new level. The audience was jumping and moving, singing in harmony to a song about feeling like people are bound to leave you alone.

“You’re gonna run, it’s alright, everybody does,” Baker and the crowd sang. “You’re gonna run, it’s alright, everybody does.”

Throughout the night, Baker was able to capture impossible feelings and showcase a diverse sound. Baker’s artistic choices at this concert proved that live music can be a chance for artists to continue to hone their sound and breathe fresh air into their discography.

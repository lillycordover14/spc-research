    A lesson in the history of UNC course registration

    Across its existence, the course registration process has taken many forms, spanning from in-person lines to the ConnectCarolina students know today. 

Caroline Rhoad
, 
Staff Writer
                 
             •  
            
November 2, 2026
 

 

Katie Walker
 

The first time Leslie King registered for classes at UNC, she ended up with none of the major-specific classes she needed and fewer credit hours than required to be a full-time student.

King is not alone in this experience. Other students view the current registration process as intense, even some using a millisecond clock so they can enroll in their courses immediately upon their registration time.

But while the stress of securing a desired class remains a familiar frustration
 
for UNC students, the process itself has changed drastically over the decades.

The ‘60s, ‘70s and ‘80s: Registering in person

For UNC students in the 1960s, registering for classes was a physical process.

Joe Mann, who attended UNC from 1965 to 1969, said students began by reviewing printed course listings and talking with classmates about which courses and professors they recommended.

Students then met with a faculty advisor, who was expected to ensure their planned courses aligned with graduation requirements. After receiving approval, students brought that signed paperwork to registration, which Mann recalled taking place at Woollen Gym. There, students were directed to individual departments to register for courses, he said.

This paper and pencil system continued
 
into the 1970s and 1980s.

Mary Anna Underwood, class of 1971, said that, in addition to standing in line to register, students used their Social Security number as identification during registration.

Underwood said registration took her all day, but that didn’t affect her spirits.

“I was just so excited to be there,” Underwood said. “I didn’t let the registration bother me.”

Byron Kirkland, undergraduate class of 1983 and graduate class of 1987, said during his time at UNC, students submitted course requests in writing to an advisor, who then wrote back to indicate whether they got their desired classes.

When students did not get their preferred courses, they had to navigate the drop/add process themselves, which Kirkland said took place at Woollen Gym.

“There wasn’t a lot of guidance,” Kirkland said. “You were sort of on your own to go figure it out.”

The ‘90s: Phone call system

UNC registration underwent a major change in October of 1990 with the introduction of Caroline, an automated phone system, Lauren DiGrazia, associate provost and University registrar, wrote in a statement to The Daily Tar Heel. After being assigned a date and time window, students could call 962-UNC1 and enter codes for the classes they hoped to take.

Christine Ferrell, who attended UNC from 1995 to 1999, said getting through to “Caroline” could be a challenge.

“I have very frustrating memories of it taking a long time to actually connect to the system,” Ferrell said. 

Ferrell said students often encountered busy signals, forcing them to repeatedly call back. Even after connecting, they risked finding their preferred courses already full.

She said frustration with the system became a running joke among students, who talked about Caroline as if it were a person.

“I just remember laughing with all of my friends about how frustrating and awful the process was, and how much we hated Caroline,” Ferrell said.

The 2000s and ‘10s: Various online systems

UNC began transitioning course registration online as internet access became more widespread.

Carol Kozak, class of 2005, said registration required students to organize their course selections by which classes were most likely to fill up first. She compared the process to buying tickets online for a popular concert.

“It was kind of like getting tickets for a show these days, like a big show on Ticketmaster,” Kozak said. 

Students logged onto Student Central, the online registration system, at their assigned time and added courses to their shopping carts. However, Kozak said the website frequently crashed, sometimes forcing students to start over and find alternative courses.

She recalled watching available seats decline as students rushed to secure spots in a course.

“You would get really nervous,” Kozak said. “Like, ‘I need to hurry up and check out all my classes because they’re filling up.’”

In 2009, UNC launched the Student Administration module of ConnectCarolina, formerly known as Campus Solutions. The module included student records, financial aid, admissions and academic advising, but not course registration.

During the 2010s, UNC transitioned course registration from Student Central to ConnectCarolina, the platform students continue to use today. DiGrazia wrote in her statement that this move reflected a broader institutional need to integrate functions that historically had been supported through separate or legacy systems.

Today: ConnectCarolina

Today, through ConnectCarolina, undergraduate registration takes place in two waves. During Wave 1, students can register for up to 13 credit hours, and during Wave 2, they can register for up to 17 credit hours.

Registration appointments are assigned based on completed credit hours and open every three hours beginning at 7 a.m.

For King, preparing for registration meant filling her shopping cart with about 40 classes before her appointment opened. Yet, despite her preparation, King said she watched seats in her preferred classes disappear as students with earlier registration appointments enrolled. 

King said the process was especially frustrating because of the competition for classes in biology, which is her major.

“I should be worrying about the actual content of the classes, not getting them to begin with,” King said.

Moving forward, DiGrazia wrote that  the University will be implementing a Degree Planner for the majority of undergraduate students. The planner will be a semester-by-semester planning tool that helps students better map their path to graduation, she wrote.

Despite decades of changes to UNC’s registration system, the pressure of securing a desired schedule has remained. In her statement, DiGrazia wrote that technology cannot eliminate course capacity constraints or guarantee students their preferred schedules. 

“The technology has changed dramatically over the decades, but the goal has remained consistent: providing students with greater transparency and better tools to access the courses they need, plan their academic journey, make progress towards their degree and ultimately complete their Carolina degree,” DiGrazia wrote.

The bigger picture of figure skating eligibility

                            Senior synchronized skating team practices in Goggin Ice Center.

            By 
Eleanor Jones | The Miami Student

November 4, 2026 | 8:00am EST

There is a particular cruelty to figure skating’s calendar. Every four years, the sport builds toward a few minutes on Olympic ice, compressing years of training, sacrifice and expectation into performances that can define careers. 

Then the medals are awarded, the music stops and the sport must begin again. Champions retire and prodigies grow older. Programs that once seemed revolutionary became yesterday’s news, and the ice, indifferent to all of it, waits for the next skater.

The 2026-27 season begins with the past very much in attendance. Russian figure skater Kamila Valieva is attempting to rebuild a career interrupted by a 
doping controversy
. Russian skaters are returning to international competition after years of exclusion. Meanwhile, Olympic champions are stepping away, leaving a new generation to establish itself.

Few stories embody this unfinished business more completely than Valieva’s.

In Jan. 2026, Valieva returned to domestic competition at the Russian Jumping Championships, four years after the 
positive doping test
 that overshadowed her Beijing Olympics in 2022. Now at 20 years old, she is no longer the seemingly invincible teenager who had captivated audiences with her technical brilliance. She finished fourth in the individual jumping event: A return, certainly, but not a return to the international stage.

Her four-year ban expired in Dec. 2025. Although the 
International Skating Union
 (ISU) initially granted her neutral-athlete eligibility for the 2026-27 season, it withdrew that status in Sept. Valieva remains unable to compete internationally.

Valieva’s situation is especially striking because Russian figure skating is beginning to return to international competition. Following Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Russian and Belarusian skaters were 
excluded from ISU events
. Eligible athletes can now compete as individual neutral athletes, without national flags or anthems, subject to restrictions concerning military service and public support for the war.

The principle is understandable: Athletes should not automatically be held responsible for their governments’ actions. But, applying that principle is considerably more complicated. National sporting institutions, coaches and athletes exist within political systems that cannot simply be left at the rink’s entrance. 

Neutral status attempts to separate individual competitors from their states, but it cannot erase the circumstances surrounding their participation. Nor does the policy affect every skater equally. 

Adeliia Petrosian and Petr Gumennik, who competed as neutral athletes at the 2026 Olympics, are eligible to return to international competition. Meanwhile, Valieva, men’s singles skater Mark Kondratiuk and ice dancers Aleksandra Stepanova and Ivan Bukin have lost or been denied eligibility. The ISU has not publicly detailed the individual grounds for every 
decision
. 

Among those returning is 
Alexandra Ignatova
, formerly Trusova, whose comeback is remarkable. Four years after winning Olympic silver in 2022 at 17, she has begun competing internationally, and notably has been given neutral status. She has continued to land quadruple jumps, something that she 
revolutionized
 in women’s skating as a teenager. Now, as the oldest woman and only mother to successfully execute a quadruple jump in competition, her return challenges a sport in which women’s careers have too often been cut short. 

This raises an uncomfortable question: If neutrality is to be the standard, how can the sport ensure that it is applied consistently? Excluding athletes collectively risks punishing individuals for their nationality; selective readmission risks undermining confidence in the rules. Neither concern can be dismissed, particularly while the war continues and Ukrainian athletes must compete within its consequences.

The competitive landscape
, meanwhile, has not waited for Russia’s return. Olympic champion Alysa Liu is sitting out this season, silver medallist Kaori Sakamoto has retired and Olympic pairs champions Riku Miura and Ryuichi Kihara have also stepped away. Ilia Malinin, after finishing eighth at the Olympics, rebounded a month later to win his third gold world title. His recovery offered a reminder that one disappointing competition doesn’t define a career. 

Recovering from defeat is not the same as returning from retirement, and neither is equivalent to rebuilding a career after a disciplinary ban. Valieva’s story cannot be reduced to an inspirational tale of redemption. The questions of accountability and responsibility remain, regardless of whether she skates internationally again.

As the new season begins, figure skating will once more be measured in rotations, edges, landings and scores. But the sport carries questions no technical score can answer: who deserves another chance, what fairness requires and whether moving forward means leaving the past behind.

Figure skating has always been a sport of reinvention, but reinvention is not forgetting. The ice is where athletes attempt to make something new from what has happened to them. The season begins. The past comes with it.

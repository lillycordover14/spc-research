    Orange County breaks ground on new crisis care center

    The groundbreaking ceremony took place on Oct. 22, where County staff, health professionals and community members spoke.

Emmy Russ
, 
Staff Writer
                 
             •  
            
November 5, 2026
 

 

Officials participate in a groundbreaking ceremony for the Jamezetta Bedford Crisis Care Center in Hillsborough on Friday, Oct. 23, 2026. Photo courtesy of Kristin Prelipp/Orange County Government.
 

The Jamezetta Bedford Crisis Care Center groundbreaking ceremony was held on Oct. 22 at Waterstone Drive in Hillsborough, where community members honored the late Orange County Commissioner Jamezetta Bedford, who died in June.

The ceremony was an invitation-only event. Local government officials, UNC Health employees, local law enforcement, emergency service workers, community advocates that have supported the center and Bedford’s family were among the attendees.

“It was a really good mixture of, cross section of community members, elected officials and others who are all committed to sort of getting us to where we are in terms of breaking ground,” Orange County Deputy County Manager Caitlin Fenhagen, said.

A variety of services will be provided at the center. People will be welcome to walk in for up to 23 hours of behavioral health urgent care, which will include being evaluated, helped for any immediate need and given outpatient care. It will serve adults and children over the age of four years old.

There will also be a facility-based crisis center with 16 beds where those who need longer-term care will be able to stay for up to two weeks. It will also include a peer living room that anyone can spend time in and a behavioral health resource library.

“It’s a very positive thing,” UNC Health Director of Psychiatry Services Barbara-Ann Bybel, who spoke at the event, said in an interview with The Daily Tar Heel. “The positivity of being part of such a large project, that there are so many different stakeholders behind — like law enforcement is supportive, healthcare is supportive, community members are supportive.” 

The center is named after Bedford in honor of her leadership in its development. She focused on a compassionate approach to crisis care that offers therapeutic support. The center is meant to decrease reliance on emergency services and law enforcement to respond to behavioral health crises.

Bybel said she hopes the ongoing care —- from prevention to recovery — will make the center a staple in the community, not just a place that people go when they are in a crisis.

Sophie Suberman, co-founder and executive director of Grow Your World, a nonprofit focused on building interconnected communities, said preventative care for children can benefit the community and alleviate other burdens.

“If we’re not going to address the upstream of where this is all coming from, we’re not going to reduce the financial burden, the cost burden, the incarceration burden, the drug and crime burden,” Suberman said.

Fenhagen said she hopes people will no longer have to go to overcrowded emergency departments or detention centers that are not equipped to serve them. Rather, they will be able to spend time at a therapeutic and supportive place that offers discharge planning.

The center will also connect patients with other community services, such as food and housing resources, so they can continue to receive the support they need.

“What’s important to know is just how this facility is going to be so different from a traditional hospital or medical facility,” Fenhagen said. 

She said the center will be holistic and trauma-informed to set patients up for long-term success.

The construction is expected to be done around fall 2027, and the center is set to open in early 2028.

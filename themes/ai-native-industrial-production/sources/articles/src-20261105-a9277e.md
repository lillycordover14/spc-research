Abstract
Spatial multi-omic technologies measure multiple molecular layers within intact tissues. Two central challenges are vertical integration, which combines different molecular modalities, and horizontal integration, which aligns cell types across studies. Here we show domain invariant representation through adaptive calibration (DIRAC), a geometric deep learning model that unifies these tasks in a generalized domain adaptation framework. DIRAC learns task-discriminative features that are insensitive to domain shifts caused by batch effects, omic modalities or study sites, enabling a shared embedding space and automated cell-type annotation through label transfer from reference datasets. Across sequencing-based and imaging-based datasets, DIRAC identified a new microvascular proliferation domain in glioblastoma, corrected cortical-layer annotations in severe Alzheimer’s disease, resolved subtle structures in mouse embryos and remained robust to missing data and unseen cell types. We generated mouse spleen and thymus Stereo-CITE-seq data and used DIRAC to reveal thymic T cell migration and splenic immune cell interactions. DIRAC is fast and scales to millions of cells.

Results
Overview of DIRAC
DIRAC vertically integrates spatial multi-omic modalities into a joint embedding for clustering, spatial-domain segmentation and cell-type annotation. When reference single-cell or spatial datasets are available, DIRAC horizontally integrates the reference with query spatial multi-omic datasets for automated annotation. DIRAC consolidates both tasks within a generalized domain adaptation framework, where distributions shift across modalities or platforms while cell types/states remain shared
39
. Domain adaptation theory suggests that effective integration must rely on features discriminative for learning tasks but indiscriminate to domain shifts
29
. Inspired by this, DIRAC uses an adversarial discriminator to guide GNNs to remove modality-identifying or batch-identifying information while retaining cell-type/state signals (Fig. 
1a
).

DIRAC consists of two modules. In vertical integration, omics layers are integrated into a joint embedding in four steps (Fig. 
1a
). First, each layer is converted into a graph with molecular features as node weights and spatial proximity as edge weights (
Methods
). Second, a graph encoder learns a low-dimensional representation reflecting molecular and spatial organization. Third, the discriminator removes batch-specific information while retaining intrinsic cellular properties. Finally, reconstruction loss combines layer-specific representations into a joint embedding for downstream analyses.
In horizontal integration, DIRAC integrates a labeled reference dataset with a query spatial multi-omic dataset for automated annotation (Fig. 
1a
). The discriminator learns a joint embedding capturing shared cellular properties. Because cell-type proportions often differ across datasets and platforms, DIRAC adopts pseudo-label alignment
40
,
41
 to assign provisional labels and calibrate class distributions and decision boundaries across domains (
Methods
). DIRAC decomposes complete graphs into dense subgraphs to accelerate training and scale to millions of cells while capturing long-range spatial interactions.

Discussion
We described DIRAC as a highly scalable framework for simultaneous spatial multi-omic vertical integration and horizontal label transfer. Unlike existing methods that treat these tasks separately, DIRAC unifies them under a domain adaptation framework, assuming that data distributions may shift across omics modalities and study batches while cell types remain shared. To capture invariant cell-type information and remove modality-specific and batch-specific shifts, DIRAC uses an adversarial domain discriminator to guide GNNs to learn task-discriminative but domain-indiscriminate features. Benchmarking analyses showed that DIRAC outperformed state-of-the-art methods on vertical integration tasks
10
,
12
,
36
,
65
 using simulated and real DBiT-seq data, and on horizontal label transfer tasks
22
,
23
,
27
,
58
,
59
,
103
 using simulated datasets, human DLPFC Visium data and human gastrointestinal CODEX data. DIRAC also identified new biological insights, including a microvascular proliferation domain in glioblastoma, corrected cortical-layer annotations in severe AD and subtle spatial niches missed by previous studies

,
20
,
36
,
37
,
38
.
We generated two subcellular-resolution Stereo-CITE-seq datasets on mouse thymus and spleen, and applied DIRAC’s vertical and horizontal modules to construct spatial multi-omic atlases of lymphoid organs. Using manually annotated single-cell CITE-seq references, DIRAC traced thymocyte spatial migration in the thymus and mapped immune cell interactions in the spleen. Compared with unimodal annotation, multi-omic annotation delineated more accurate tissue compartments and trajectories. Unlike spatial deconvolution
90
 and cell-to-location assignment methods
91
, DIRAC operates on cellularly resolved spatial multi-omic data, enabling precise cell counts and downstream cell-to-cell communication analysis.
Our results should be interpreted within limitations. First, DIRAC relies on external references for cell-type annotation. Semi-supervised methods that do not rely on references exist

,
105
,
106
, but lack dedicated batch correction. Combining semi-supervision with domain-adversarial training could improve generalizability. In addition, DIRAC operates on cellularly resolved data, and segmentation inaccuracies may affect annotation. Future work could use DIRAC horizontal integration to iteratively refine cell segmentation and leverage emerging spatial tagging methods
107
. As public single-cell and spatial multi-omic datasets expand, DIRAC will benefit from increasingly diverse labeled and unlabeled data.
Methods
Graph construction
DIRAC constructs a graph to model cell-to-cell relationships in spatial multi-omics data. The process begins by computing the Euclidean distance 
d
i
, 
j
(
c
i
, 
c
j
) between any two cells 
c
i
 and 
c
j
 based on their spatial coordinates. Using these distances, we identify the 
K-
nearest neighbors for each cell to build a spatial 
K
-nearest-neighbor (KNN) graph. The choice of 
K
 is typically determined by the resolution of the spatial omics technology used.
In addition to spatial information, DIRAC also leverages molecular profiles such as transcriptomic, epigenomic or proteomic features. These multi-omic features are concatenated into a joint expression matrix, from which we construct a feature similarity graph using a KNN approach based on a selected distance or similarity metric (for example, correlation or Euclidean distance).
To integrate both spatial proximity and molecular similarity, DIRAC constructs a consensus graph. Specifically, spatial and feature-based graphs are constructed independently, and their edge sets are intersected to produce a unified graph that includes only edges supported by both modalities. This ensures that connected cells are both spatially close and molecularly similar. To control graph sparsity, we iteratively adjust the neighborhood size using binary search so that the resulting consensus graph has an average degree close to a user-defined target.
DIRAC overview
We present an overview of DIRAC herein, reserving technical details for

DIRAC consists of two modules: the vertical integration module takes as input multiple spatial omics modalities measured on the same tissue slice, and the horizontal integration module takes as input a reference single-cell/spatial multi-omic dataset and a query spatial multi-omic dataset. It is worth noting that both modules can be applied to single-cell multi-omic data when spatial coordinates are unavailable. DIRAC uses a GNN to aggregate information from each cell and its neighbors (Fig.

$$\begin{array}{l}{c}_{\tau }=\dfrac{\tau }{{n}_{s}}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{i=1}^{{n}_{s}}{\mathop{\max }\limits_{j\in [1\cdot \cdot \,{K}_{a}]}}\left({\hat{Y}}_{s}^{\left(i,\,j\right)}\right)\\ {\rm{mas{k}}}_{\left(i\right)}={\mathop{\max }\limits_{j\in [1\cdot \cdot \,{K}_{a}]}}\left({\hat{Y}}_{s}^{\left(i,\,j\right)}\right)\ge {c}_{\tau }\end{array}$$

                    (7)
                
where 
τ
 is a user-defined hyperparameter threshold (default value is 0.9). DIRAC defines the final alignment loss function as in equation (
8
):
$$\begin{array}{l}{{\mathcal{L}}}_{align}=\dfrac{1}{{n}_{s}}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{i=1}^{{n}_{s}}H\left({Y}^{\left(i\right)},{Y}_{s}^{c,\left(i\right)}\right)+\mu \left(t\right)\dfrac{1}{{n}_{t}}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{i=1}^{{n}_{t}}H\left({\tilde{Y}}_{t}^{c},{Y}_{t}^{c,\left(i\right)}\right)mas{k}^{\left(i\right)}\\ \mu \left(t\right)=0.5-\cos \left(\min \left(\pi,2\pi t/T\right)\right)/2\end{array}$$

                    (8)
                
where 
\(H(p,q)=-{\sum }_{x}p(x)\log q(x)\)
 represents the cross-entropy loss, and 
T
 denotes the total training steps. 
μ
(
t
) is a gentle function used to control the weight of the unlabeled loss at each training step. This specific function smoothly rises from 0 to 1 in the first half of training and remains at 1 in the latter half. Similar to the vertical integration model, domain adaptation loss 
\({{\mathcal{L}}}_{da}\)
 (equation (
2
)) between the source and target domains and their respective reconstruction loss 
\({{\mathcal{L}}}_{mse}\)
 (
Supplemetary Information
, equation S4) are also computed. Additionally, DIRAC adopted a maximum entropy regularization term to adaptively avoid assigning all cells to the same cell type as shown in equation (
9
):
$${{\mathcal{L}}}_{\rm{entroy}}=-\dfrac{1}{{n}_{{\rm{s}}}+{n}_{{\rm{t}}}}\sum _{{{\bf{z}}}_{i}\in {Z}_{s}^{c}\bigcup {Z}_{{\rm{t}}}^{c}}\sum _{k}{\sigma }_{k}\left({W}^{\rm{T}}{{\bf{z}}}_{i}\right)\log {\sigma }_{k}\left({W}^{\rm{T}}{{\bf{z}}}_{i}\right),$$

                    (9)
                
where, 
W
 denotes the weight matrix of the linear layer, 
k
 iterates over all reference cell types, and 
σ
k
 represents the 
k
-th cell-type value of the softmax output. The final loss function is obtained as in equation (
10
):
$${\mathcal{L}}={\eta }_{1}{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\rm{align}}+{\eta }_{2}\left({{\mathcal{L}}}_{\rm{da}}+{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\rm{mse}}\right)+{\eta }_{3}{{\mathcal{L}}}_{\rm{entroy}}$$

                    (10)
                
where 
η
1
, 
η
2
 and 
η
3
 are regularization parameters.
We used a graph sampler to facilitate subgraph training, thereby optimizing the efficiency of graph clustering algorithms. This is achieved through sampling and neighborhood restriction. DIRAC partitions all nodes of graph G into 
c
 groups, denoted as 
\(E=\left[{E}_{1},{E}_{2},\cdots \,,{E}_{c}\right]\)
, where 
E
t
 represents the nodes in the 
t
-th partition. Consequently, 
c
 subgraphs are obtained, each comprising nodes from a specific group as shown in equation (
11
):
$$\widetilde{G}=[{G}_{1},\cdots \,,{G}_{c}]=[\{{E}_{1},{\epsilon }_{1}\},\cdots \,,\{{E}_{c},{\epsilon }_{c}\}]$$

                    (11)
                
where each 
ϵ
c
 consists solely of links between nodes in 
E
t
. This approach primarily involves dividing a large graph into multiple subgraphs for training, thereby enhancing the capability of DIRAC to handle large-scale spatial multi-omic data.
Animal model and tissue collection
Male C57BL/6 mice (
Mus musculus
), 6–8 weeks of age, were obtained from BGI animal facility and used in all experiments. Mice were raised on a 12-h light–dark cycle with food and water ad libitum and were group housed. All relevant procedures involving animal experiments presented in this study are compliant with ethical regulations regarding animal research and were conducted under the approval of the Institutional Review Board of the Ethics Committee of BGI (permit no. BGI-IRB A21001-T1) and the Animal Care and Use committee of the Guangzhou Institutes of Biomedicine and Health, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the thymus and spleen of the mice were dissected. After collection, tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT (Sakura, 4583) on dry ice and transferred to a −80 °C freezer for storage before the experiment.
Stereo-CITE-seq library preparation and sequencing
Before the experiment, all solutions were freshly prepared and maintained on ice. Blocking Buffer (50 μl per sample) was composed of 3× SSC, 0.1% Triton X-100, 10% serum, 1 μg μl
−1
 sheared salmon sperm DNA, 2.5 μl TruStain FcX PLUS (anti-mouse CD16/32) antibody (BioLegend, 156604), 5% RNase inhibitor (RI) and 5 μM 32-nt Poly A. The Wash Buffer consisted of 3× SSC with 5% RI.
Cryosectioned OCT-embedded tissue, cut to 10-μm thick, was carefully transferred onto the Stereo-seq chip. These chips, now bearing tissue sections, were immediately warmed to 37 °C for 1 min using a thermocycler adaptor. Tissues were then fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 5 min. The tissue sections on chips were then washed two times with wash buffer, each for 1 min. After washing, blocking was performed by incubating the tissue with blocking buffer for 20 min, after which the sections were exposed to the TotalSeq-A antibody mix in blocking buffer for 45 min at room temperature. The tissue sections on chips were then washed three times with wash buffer, each for 30 s. After DAPI staining, the tissue sections were mounted with glycerol and imaged.
Subsequent processing followed the Stereo-seq Transcriptomics T Kit protocol with minor adjustments. Briefly, the sections were permeabilized with Permeabilization Mix for an optimized duration, as recommended in the Stereo-seq Permeabilization Kit instructions. After reverse transcription, cDNA and antibody-derived tag (ADT) products were released from the chips using cDNA Release Mix and collected. Separation of the cDNA fraction from the ADT library was achieved via a ×0.8 bead selection, followed by preparation of the cDNA sequencing library in accordance with the Stereo-seq Transcriptomics T Kit. The ADT product in the supernatant was purified using a 2.0× (0.8× + 1.2×) bead selection, then amplified with Protein PCR Primer Mix. Finally, both ADT and cDNA libraries were sequenced using MGI DNBSEQ-T7 or DNBSEQ-G400 sequencing platforms.
Stereo-CITE-seq raw data processing
Gene expression and ADT FASTQ files of mouse thymus and spleen samples were produced using the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 or DNBSEQ-G400 sequencer. The raw transcriptomic data from Stereo-seq were processed following established protocols as previously outlined. The analysis pipeline is publicly accessible at 
https://github.com/BGIResearch/SAW/
. The ADT FASTQ files were also generated using the same sequencing platforms.
The first read (read 1) contained cell identification (CID) and molecular identifier (MID) sequences, with the CID spanning from positions 1 bp to 25 bp and the MID from 26 bp to 35 bp. Read 2 included a 21-bp PCR handle followed by 15-bp antibody barcodes. Initially, CID sequences from read 1 were aligned to the specified coordinates of the in situ captured chip derived from the sequencing data, allowing for a one-base mismatch. Reads that had MID sequences with any ‘N’ bases or more than two bases with a quality score lower than 10 were excluded from further analysis. We utilized an in-house tag quantification pipeline to map antibody tags to their corresponding antibody barcodes. This mapping facilitated the generation of a matrix profiling the ADT data that included the CID information.
Cell segmentation with nucleic acid staining
To segment spots into single cells, we utilized nucleic acid staining from the same tissue section by mapping the staining image onto the Stereo-seq chips. This involved calculating the total unique molecular identifier (UMI) count in each DNA nanoball (DNB) spot, which has specific spatial coordinates, to create a spatial density matrix. Subsequently, this matrix was transformed into an image where each pixel represents a DNB spot, with the grayscale intensity reflecting the total UMI for that spot. We manually aligned the DNB image with the nucleic acid staining image.
After alignment, we used StereoPy
108
 for cell segmentation analysis. The background of the staining image was eliminated using a global thresholding method, followed by the application of a watershed algorithm to achieve individual cell segmentations. The number of markers needed for this watershed algorithm was determined using Gaussian-weighted local threshold binarization, set with a block size of 41 and an offset of 0.003.
Next, we extracted the Euclidean distance transformation from the background-removed images, using distances of 13 or 15. For each segmented cell, UMIs from all DNBs within the corresponding region were aggregated per gene, culminating in the creation of a cell-by-gene matrix for subsequent analysis. The centroid of each cell was calculated using the rearrr package (
https://github.com/LudvigOlsen/rearrr/
).
Integration and clustering metrics
Various metrics, including mean average precision (MAP), average silhouette width (ASW) for cell types
42
, neighborhood connectivity (NC)
23
, Seurat alignment score (SAS)
109
, overall average silhouette width (OASW) and graph connectivity (GC), were used to evaluate multi-omic integration. MAP, ASW and NC assess biological preservation, whereas SAS, OASW and GC assess mixing across omics layers. ASW and NC range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better preservation of cell-type structure. We performed min–max scaling for each metric and then calculated the mean of MAP, ASW and NC to obtain a biological conservation (BC) score according to equation (
12
):
$${\rm{BC}}\,{\rm{score}}=\dfrac{\rm{scale}({\rm{ASW}})+\mathrm{{scale}}({\rm{MAP}})+\mathrm{{scale}}({\rm{NC}})}{3}.$$

                    (12)
                
SAS, OASW and GC assess the level of integration between omics layers, with values ranging from 0 to 1; higher values denote better integration effects. We defined the omics mixing (OM) score as shown in equation (
13
):
$${\rm{OM}}\,{\rm{score}}=\dfrac{\rm{scale}({\rm{OASW}})+\mathrm{{scale}}({\rm{GC}})+\mathrm{{scale}}({\rm{SAS}})}{3}.$$

                    (13)
                
Adjusted mutual information (AMI) and normalized mutual information (NMI) quantify the similarity between two clustering assignments; AMI additionally corrects for agreement expected by chance. The ARI and Rand index assess agreement between clusterings, whereas the Fowlkes–Mallows score measures pairwise similarity. Completeness and homogeneity scores evaluate whether members of a given class are assigned to the same cluster and whether each cluster contains members of a single class, respectively. The V-measure is their harmonic mean. Together, these metrics assess the agreement, completeness and homogeneity of clustering results. All metrics were computed using scikit-learn
110
.
When spatial coordinates are available, we additionally compute Moran’s 
I
 to quantify the spatial autocorrelation of gene expression or cell-level features. Moran’s 
I
 ranges from −1 (perfect dispersion) to 1 (perfect clustering), with 0 indicating spatial randomness; higher values indicate stronger spatial structure. We compute Moran’s 
I
 using 
sq.gr.spatial_autocorr
 in Squidpy (with 
mode = ‘moran’
) after constructing a spatial neighbor graph, and we min–max scale Moran’s 
I
 across methods for comparability
94
.
Differential gene expression analysis
Differential gene expression analysis was conducted to identify marker genes using 
sc.tl.rank_genes_groups
 for each cluster with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All associated analyses and visualizations were carried out using Scanpy
111
.
Signature score calculation
Signature scores were computed with 
scanpy.tl.score_genes
, which reports, for each spot/cell, the mean expression of a given gene set minus that of expression-matched control genes (defaults: 
ctrl_size 
= 50, 
n_bins 
= 25). Before scoring, counts were library-size normalized (
pp.normalize_total
, target 10
4
) and log-transformed (
pp.log1p
); signatures were intersected with detected genes, and mitochondrial/ribosomal genes were excluded from the control pool. In the mouse thymus, domain signatures (capsule, cortex, CMJ, medulla) were computed separately for RNA and protein. Scores were optionally 
z
-scored per sample for comparability. Higher scores indicate enrichment of the corresponding signature within each modality.
Cell–cell communication analysis
We constructed cell–cell communication networks with the R package CellChat (v2)
53
. Using the cellular expression matrix, cell-type annotations and the CellChatDB ligand–receptor database, we identified overexpressed signaling genes and candidate ligand–receptor pairs. The ligand–receptor pairs were projected onto the human protein–protein interaction network to infer communication at the cell-group and signaling-pathway levels and to calculate the aggregated communication network. Specifically, ‘truncatedMean’ was used to compute average gene expression per cell group; all other parameters were set to their default values.

The United States cannot expect to deter adversaries through military capability alone as its industrial base, investment community, and allied supply chains operate on disconnected timelines. Capital that cannot move at the speed of strategic necessity has quickly become a significant vulnerability.

The next generation of deterrence will be determined as much by decisions in corporate boardrooms and investment committees as by decisions inside the Pentagon. As underscored at last week’s TechNet Indo-Pacific 2026 in Honolulu, the primary challenge facing our nation is no longer just technological; it is a challenge of speed, scale, and strategic synchronization.

For decades, the standard defense procurement pipeline has operated on an outdated timeline: compiling static requirements in remote conference rooms, refining documents over years, and relying on commercial partners trapped in multi-year delivery cycles. Meanwhile, the threat matrix across the Pacific has evolved, and we are no longer facing isolated regional friction points.

China and Russia are operating a relentless offensive across the cyber battlefield, targeting both military networks and critical commercial infrastructure. We can no longer ask innovative companies to conform to industrial processes designed for a different era and expect transformational results. The warfighter must help define the problem, industry must participate early in developing solutions, and capital must be aligned to accelerate delivery.

We must bridge the gap between national security strategy and commercial corporate governance.

A tested model for closing this gap is Project Dynamis and follow-on industry engagement models. Launched to modernize rapid capability fielding, this framework places commercial engineers directly alongside operators within realistic operational frameworks.

Hawaii has served as the natural epicenter for this evolution through the mission-specific requirements of Marine Forces Pacific (MARFORPAC) supported by the Marine Corps Tactical Systems Support Activity (MCTSSA). Because the U.S. Pacific Command (USPACOM) encompasses environmental extremes, vast distances, and acute geopolitical friction, it was a key testing ground for early agile models.

Under Project Dynamis and industry engagement models, commercial technology leaders and military principals bypass long briefings to solve multi-domain problems through immersive tabletop sprints. This approach has allowed MARFORPAC to sharpen its requirements for allied interoperability and accelerate deployment, shaving over 18 months off traditional development timelines.

More importantly, it provides a vital on-ramp for nontraditional technology firms and venture- backed entities that have historically been locked out by the complexity of the defense system. To achieve real scale, this model provides the specific architecture needed to rally private sector investors and venture capital to match Department of Defense and other federal agency funding.

By blending this non-dilutive capital with vetted, coalition and allied industry funding, we can rapidly embed dual-use capabilities from Japan, Korea, the Philippines, Australia, New Zealand and other Allies in real time, transforming the defense supply chain into a resilient, multinational ecosystem.

At TechNet Indo-Pacific 2026, our conversations looked beyond specific technologies to focus on the governance frameworks required to deliver them. Deterrence cannot remain the responsibility of the military alone. It must become a shared strategic undertaking among government, industry, investors and allies. This is a leadership problem in establishing unity of effort in shared purpose, aligned resources, and the willingness to change how organizations and the overall ecosystem operate. One Team. One Fight.

Robert Sholtis is former Marine Forces Pacific chief information officer and VP of Pacific Operations SOSi; Bernice Glenn Kissinger is managing member of Kissinger Advisors LLC.

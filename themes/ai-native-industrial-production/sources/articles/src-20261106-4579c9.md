Why India Should Acquire 500 Su-57s to Counter China-Pak Stealth Threat Before AMCA Arrives, Argues Russian Think Tank CAWAT&nbsp;&nbsp;Defence.in

[page text]
To counter the rapidly evolving security threats in the region, a prominent Russian arms trade research centre, the Centre for Analysis of World Arms Trade (CAWAT), has recommended that India procure 500 Su-57 fifth-generation fighter jets.

This substantial acquisition could serve as a vital measure to bolster the Indian Air Force (IAF) against external challenges in the coming decade.

The Russian think tank highlighted that the strategic landscape surrounding India is undergoing swift transformation.

Both China and Pakistan are aggressively advancing their military modernisation programmes, with intent to deploy extensive fleets of fifth-generation stealth fighters in the near future.

CAWAT projects that by 2030, Pakistan could operate a minimum of 40 J-35AE stealth aircraft, with some defence assessments suggesting initial deliveries of Chinese J-35s to Islamabad could commence as early as 2026.

Simultaneously, China's People’s Liberation Army Air Force (PLAAF) might field up to 500 stealth platforms, including the J-20 and J-35.

Furthermore, the Chinese Navy is anticipated to deploy over 50 carrier-based J-35 fighters, significantly extending Beijing's maritime airpower.

Together, the combined Chinese and Pakistani fifth-generation inventory could reach approximately 600 aircraft.

In stark contrast, the IAF currently relies heavily on a mix of fourth and 4.5-generation jets, primarily consisting of the Dassault Rafale, Sukhoi Su-30MKI, and the indigenous LCA Tejas.

To avoid falling behind in this critical air superiority race, the report stressed that New Delhi must formulate an immediate, large-scale procurement strategy to bridge the widening technological gap.

The assessment concluded that the most viable approach would involve the direct purchase of an initial batch of 50 export-variant Su-57E fighters.

Alongside this, the report proposed establishing a domestic production line in India to manufacture an additional 450 units under license.

CAWAT suggested that this locally produced fleet could consist of the newer two-seat Su-57D variant, catering specifically to IAF operational requirements.

Combining direct imports with extensive Make in India manufacturing would guarantee the necessary combat mass and technological edge for decades, the think tank argued.

While acknowledging India’s indigenous Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA) initiative, CAWAT noted that the programme remains years away from active service.

Although the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS) approved the ₹15,000 crore AMCA design and prototype development project in March 2024, the aircraft has not yet entered the flight-testing phase for its technology demonstrator.

Defence observers maintain that even under ideal timelines, serial production of the AMCA is unlikely before 2034–2035.

While the AMCA development is steadily advancing through prototype fabrication and industry collaborations, its induction timeline stretching into the next decade has sparked intense discussions within defence circles about securing an interim fifth-generation fighter to safeguard Indian airspace.

In this context, CAWAT highlighted the Su-57 as a fully operational system already deployed by the Russian Aerospace Forces.

The report noted that Russia has started export deliveries and would likely accommodate an official request from India.

Furthermore, a licensed manufacturing pact would grant the Indian defence sector crucial access to cutting-edge aerospace technologies.

This transfer of technical know-how could subsequently be integrated into domestic programmes like the AMCA, thereby accelerating India's overall aerospace self-reliance.

Defending the aircraft's capabilities, the Russian think tank affirmed that the Su-57 possesses genuine fifth-generation attributes, including a reduced radar signature, state-of-the-art sensors, and long-range strike options.

Crucially, the report pointed out the logistical advantage of the Su-57 sharing its design lineage with the Su-30MKI, which currently forms the backbone of the IAF.

Because both jets originate from the Sukhoi design bureau, integrating the Su-57 would ensure operational continuity.

India could leverage its existing maintenance facilities, spare parts warehouses, and pilot training infrastructure, vastly reducing the complexities associated with inducting a completely foreign Western platform.

Transitioning pilots and ground crews would be highly streamlined due to this shared ecosystem.

The analysis also scrutinised India’s recent defence acquisition strategies.

Despite increased defence budgets and a strong push for domestic manufacturing, CAWAT observed that the IAF continues to struggle with depleting fighter squadron numbers amid mounting regional threats.

The organisation suggested that some capital allocation choices might be disconnected from immediate operational realities.

It pointed to continued financial commitments towards fourth-generation platforms like the Rafale and Tejas, while hostile neighbours aggressively pursue fifth-generation dominance.

Modern Chinese platforms like the J-20 and J-35 introduce a level of aerial combat proficiency that legacy fourth-generation fighters simply cannot counter effectively.

The report argued that the Rafale and Tejas do not provide a significant combat overmatch compared to the existing Su-30MKI.

In parameters such as combat radius, payload capacity, and sheer speed, the heavy Russian fighter still holds distinct advantages.

Additionally, the think tank questioned the economic logic of importing Rafale jets at an estimated cost of €160 million each, noting that the established Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) infrastructure could assemble Russian platforms at a fraction of that expenditure.

Emphasising the critical impact of these choices on national security, the report urged a thorough reassessment of India's combat aviation pathway.

It also reaffirmed Russia's historical role as New Delhi's most reliable defence supplier, highlighting that Soviet and Russian equipment have formed the bedrock of India's aerial defence for over 70 years.

Moscow remains fully prepared to back further Su-30MKI production and facilitate the joint manufacturing of the Su-57E at HAL facilities across India.

Painting a concerning scenario, the researchers warned of severe consequences if the IAF continues its reliance on fourth-generation jets.

With the possibility of future escalations involving a modernising China and an opportunist Pakistan, control of the airspace will dictate the outcome of any potential conflict.

By 2030, a combined Sino-Pakistani force of 600 stealth fighters would severely outclass India's current inventory.

The report raised specific alarms regarding Chinese extended-range air-to-air missiles, namely the PL-15 and PL-17.

When paired with stealth profiles and advanced sensors, these munitions grant Beijing and Islamabad a formidable first-shoot, first-kill capability that mere numerical advantages cannot neutralize.

Consequently, an increased fleet of Rafale and Tejas fighters would still find it challenging to engage J-20s and J-35s on equal terms.

The technological synergy of stealth, active electronically scanned array (AESA) radars, and beyond-visual-range weaponry creates an asymmetric advantage.

The Su-57E, according to CAWAT, serves as the ideal antidote, equipping India with comparable stealth, advanced avionics, and long-range engagement tools.

This forms the basis of their recommendation to swiftly procure 50 direct units and manufacture 450 domestically, potentially incorporating the two-seater Su-57D to act as a formidable stopgap until local stealth projects materialize.

Examining past indigenous procurement, the Russian analysis reflected on the LCA Tejas trajectory.

While IAF aviators acknowledge the Tejas as a vast improvement over the ageing MiG-21s it was meant to replace, prolonged development timelines blunted its strategic relevance.

When the Tejas was finally inducted, a significant portion of the MiG-21 fleet had already been phased out. Historically fielding 20 MiG-21 squadrons, the IAF operates only a modest fraction of that number in Tejas aircraft as of 2026.

The report pointed out that extended gestation periods and shifting operational benchmarks have repeatedly hampered Indian aerospace projects, resulting in platforms that enter service just as adversary technologies leap forward.

To clarify operational dynamics, CAWAT differentiated the intended roles of the Tejas and the Su-57E.

Due to its single-engine configuration and economical operating costs, the Tejas is optimal for lighter defensive duties and localized patrol missions.

Conversely, constraints related to payload, thrust-to-weight ratio, radar range, and a lack of stealth prevent it from dominating complex, high-end air-superiority contests.

Therefore, the think tank views the Tejas and the Su-57 as complementary assets operating in entirely separate tiers, rather than as competing systems.

In conclusion, the report stated that India has reached a critical strategic crossroads. The government must choose whether to wait exclusively on indigenous development schedules or to reinforce its combat edge by integrating advanced foreign systems via domestic manufacturing.

While the vision of total self-reliance through the AMCA and Tejas initiatives is highly commendable, the immediate tactical threat justifies the acquisition of an established fifth-generation platform.

CAWAT maintained that the Su-57E fits this requirement perfectly, reinforcing that Russia's enduring military-technical partnership remains an invaluable asset for India’s future air combat dominance.

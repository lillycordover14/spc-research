The New York songwriter and producer’s second album meditates on the passage of time, with immersive, lush songs that balance the optimism and anxiety of young adulthood.

When you are a young adult living alone for the first time—buying your own groceries, falling in love quickly and effortlessly—the world suddenly opens up. There are so many ways to be a person, and they all feel within reach. But you also know what’s looming: You’ve seen adults with their ossified personalities and their set routines. You’re acutely aware that you only have a few years until you’re expected to join them, to forgo the endless potential of youth for the stability and rationality of maturity. It’s why there’s so much anticipatory nostalgia in the best music about this transitory period: 
Lorde
 screaming that she wants her old mind and friendships back on “Ribs,” 
SZA
 asking why it’s so hard to admit the party is over on “Drew Barrymore.”
New York singer-songwriter 
Laila!
 released her debut album, 2024’s 
Gap Year!
,
 when she was 18. It’s a raw and conversational record about the minutiae of high school life: lacking a prom date, doing your homework, finding comfort in solitude. In the two years following its release, she graduated, fell in love, toured the world, and moved into her first adult apartment. She’s said that all the change has filled her with an overwhelming sense of gratitude. Many of the songs on her second album, 
We’ll Never Be This Young Again
, luxuriate in that joy and optimism. But the record also contains a feeling of restlessness and looming anxiety from its very first song, when she sings, “I’m too young to be this upset/Feel like I’m losing time.” The album functions as a meditation on time, and all that is lost and gained as it moves forward.

The album’s title functions like a lodestar throughout its runtime. Laila! says the phrase not only on the title track, but also on “SO MUCH, SO MUCH” and “Anything Could Happen.” Every time I hear it, I think of “Ivy” by 
Frank Ocean
, one of pop music’s greatest purveyors of nostalgia; when he sings, “We’ll never be those kids again,” the stakes are perfectly clear: Every passing minute takes him further from a fleeting moment of unencumbered adolescent hope. The best writing on nostalgia is textured and expansive like this, collapsing time on itself: the thrill of the present and the uncertainty of the future. Against this bar, though, “We’ll Never Be This Young Again” feels relatively flat, its imagery and sound all hitting a uniform emotional register. Across a lilting ’90s soul sample, Laila! sings a handful of phrases that broadly gesture at sentimentality: We’re not getting any younger, summer isn’t done yet, you could be my best friend. There is no tension between expectation and reality, no concrete regret or achievement, no shift in perspective, nothing gained or lost.
Laila! produced nearly every song on 
We’ll Never Be This Young Again,
 intent on making a record that flowed together seamlessly, one that employed both ethereality and precision. Inspired by 
Janet Jackson
 and 
Solange
, her compositions are lush and immersive. The songs that consider the passing of time more obliquely work best. On “Better Days,” a searing ballad about lost love, Laila! employs her poised, potent vocal runs and a synth solo that evokes the unfettered electric organ in 
Hailu Mergia
’s “Wede Harer Guzo.” The song’s stacked vocals and nature imagery also give it the intimacy and tactility of a folk song. “Oh how I used to love you/Oh how the river’s dried,” she sings, channeling the searing remorse of 
Jazmine Sullivan
’s “Lost Ones” and the gorgeous vocal harmonies of the 
Roches
. Her story of two people trying their hardest to make a doomed romance work feels like a sentiment that’s as old as the rivers, as old as humanity itself.
Ever since I was 7 years old, I’ve understood myself to be 19, wearing a sparkling dress on the way to a fabulous party full of new friends. Even now, at 33, I see myself that way. In reality, 19 was a stressful and lonely time, spent mostly in the libraries of my frigid college town or fighting with my boyfriend in the dining hall. But I can see clearly now the value of that time: that making decisions at that age about who you will become is the most exciting work of your life; that you should allow yourself to keep making them, to reach and fail and reinvent yourself.
It’s this feeling of potential that Laila! relays on “Anything Can Happen.” A bright synth beat drives the song forward while her gossamer vocals establish a luxurious, glittering ambiance. As you listen to her repeat the titular phrase, you feel time and opportunity expanding. It’s like turning a corner and stumbling upon the endless horizon of the sea. Laila!’s strongest songs on 
We’ll Never Be This Young Again
 immerse you in this feeling of yearning—reaching for what could be, mourning what was—and remind you to keep striving, to allow yourself to become someone new.

‘Undeclared State of Emergency’: In On-Campus Event, Palantir CTO Shyam Sankar ’03 Discusses American Military Deterrence, Role of Private Sector in Defense

                    By
            
Shubha Gautam

Nov 7, 2026 1:10 am

· Updated Nov 7, 2026 1:11 am

                            Reading time: about  9 minutes
                        

The chief technology officer and executive vice president of 
Palantir Technologies
, Shyam Sankar ’03, spoke to an audience of around 300 students on Thursday evening in Baker Laboratory. 

The event began with an advanced screening of the documentary 
Deterrence: Winning the Second Cold War
, followed by Sankar discussing the private sector’s role in the defense industry, the use of autonomous weapons in warfare and how the U.S. has “lost [military] deterrence” in a Q&A session.  

Palantir is a data analytics and defense software company that has been 
criticized
 for its collaboration with the 
U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement
 and the 
Israel Defense Ministry
.

Palantir employees, including Cornell alumni, also offered coffee chats on campus to upperclassmen in 
student project teams
 this week, according to a Tuesday email from the director of project teams 
obtained
 by The Sun. 

Prof. Matt Marx, vice provost for entrepreneurship, innovation and external engagement and moderator of the Q&A, introduced Sankar to the audience as someone who “has grown [Palantir] to be a worldwide leader in software, AI and technologies,” over the past two decades.

Sankar, who also 
serves
 as a lieutenant colonel in the 
U.S. Army Reserve
, 
joined
 Palantir in 2006 as their 
13th
 employee, after earning a bachelor’s degree in electrical and computer engineering from Cornell and a master’s degree in management science and engineering from Stanford University. 

Free copies of his book, 
Mobilize: How to Reboot the American Industrial Base and Stop World War III
, were available outside the auditorium. 

At the beginning of the event, Marx reminded attendees that interruptions would be a violation of 
University policy
. He said those who interfered with the screening would be escorted out by 
Cornell University Police Department
 officers, who were present at the event. 

Cornell students, faculty and staff attending the event were screened by security, prohibited from bringing in bags and instructed to swipe their Cornell IDs to enter the venue. 

As people entered the venue, a single protestor stood with a sign speaking out against weapons manufacturing recruitment on campus. They declined a request for comment. 

In his opening remarks, Sankar said he “very vividly” remembers having “a lot of anxiety,” as his time at Cornell ended since he did not have many job offers or a post-graduation plan.

“The thing I wish people would tell me was [to] focus less on the anxiety I had and on exactly what I’m going to do, and much more on the purpose — what I can do,” Sankar said.

Exclusive Documentary Screening

The event opened up with the 90-minute documentary, which featured various U.S. national security officials and founders of private defense companies, discussed foreign supply-chain dependencies in weapons manufacturing, the Russia-Ukraine war, China’s ongoing weapons development and military deterrence as a means of preventing conflict. Attendees were prohibited from filming the screening.

Military 
deterrence
 is defined as preventing an opponent from “taking an undesired action” by making it so the “costs of acting outweigh any potential gains,” according to NATO, and typically refers to how investment in strong military capability can be a strategy to deter war. 

The film warned of the possibility of war with China. Founders of private defense companies, including Palantir, 
Castelion
 and 
Anduril
, were presented as leading efforts to advance the American defense industry.

Sankar said private companies “showed up” when increased weapons manufacturing was needed. He said the U.S. is in an “undeclared state of emergency,” and this results in a need for private defense expansion. 

“When [Palantir] first started, you couldn’t do anything with the Department of Defense,” Sankar said. “The eager crew of [private defense company] founders has then pulled behind them enormous amounts of capital. … And I think just like World War II, it will be the private sector that leads ahead of government needs.”

Q&A Session

Following the screening, audience members lined up to ask Sankar questions. Attendees probed him on topics ranging from gaining experience in the defense industry to the end goal of deterrence. Afterwards, attendees were given the opportunity to speak individually to Palantir employees. 

An audience member asked Sankar a question regarding the morality of working at the defense company. 

“Does Palantir’s onboarding process teach you how to turn off your [conscience], or is that something that is assumed before you take your offer?” the attendee asked.

While Sankar acknowledged that some see Palantir as a “controversial” company, he said that someone who applies for a job there is an “independent thinker that wants to get the facts for themselves and make up their own mind.”

One student asked the Palantir CTO what “it take[s] to win when you organize,” and whether there is an experience or mindset that Sankar sees as integral to success. Sankar answered that success “comes down to caring about winning,” and that the human tendency to get “obsessed” with the aesthetics of winning can constrain progress. 

“Do you know what the goal is?” Sankar said. “Do you know what winning is? And then [ask] yourself, am I winning more or less? Is that working?” 

Throughout his responses, Sankar underscored his belief in continued weapons manufacturing as a means of military deterrence. 

An attendee countered Sankar by pointing out that the U.S. has used its defense capabilities to begin wars and cause “damage to the world.” To them, there was worry of an “infinite escalation.”

“The problem is we don’t have deterrence right now,” Sankar replied. Using Russia’s war with Ukraine as an example, he asked: “Why did [Vladimir Putin] do it? Why did he invade Ukraine? Because he thought he was going to win.”

The attendee responded by saying they believed the Russia-Ukraine war was caused by American 
provocation
 with NATO expansion around Russia, to which Sankar said they can “agree to disagree.” 

Wyatt Sell ’27, an event attendee and Sun opinion columnist, said the idea of an arms race today “seems bad insofar as it escalates tensions,” but that the documentary compellingly argued instead that increasing weapons manufacturing efforts “actually de-escalates.” 

He referred back to the Cold War, during which the U.S. and the Soviet Union 
spent
 decades in a nuclear arms race before the Soviet Union’s dissolution “without large-scale conflict.”

“I think the deterrence [argument] is more compelling because we live in such a globalized world, such that it is better for countries to avoid conflict in general,” Sell told The Sun. In a statement to The Sun, he later added that, “Conflict is already high cost, and if we can maintain or increase the cost of a conflict for our adversaries, it’s all the more likely it won’t happen.”

Henry Cheng, a fourth year Ph.D. candidate in history and event attendee, told The Sun he believes the documentary’s perspective is “highly biased” because said it leaves out information about Palantir and can cause more moral issues in today’s higher education system.

“Deterrence against authoritarian countries, I must admit, is [somewhat] necessary, but this term is being misused by the authoritarian politicians in [the] United States,” Cheng said. “Palantir is not just investing in the robotic wars to defend Ukraine. … It's supporting ICE, supporting the police state.”

Hours before the event, 
Cornell Students for Justice in Palestine
 and 
Cornell Young Democratic Socialists of America
 
criticized
 Sankar’s invitation to campus and the University’s “direct pipeline from undergrad to weapons manufacturer.” The video incorrectly stated that attendees would have to sign non-disclosure agreements, however, NDAs have 
previously
 been required at Palantir-hosted events on Cornell’s campus. 

The student groups denounced Palantir’s $30 million contract with ICE to develop ImmigrationOS, a surveillance platform that 
aims
 to “streamline the identification … of individuals prioritized for removal.” The AI-powered system 
pulls
 data from government databases to target, track and manage deportations.  

SJP and YDSA also criticized Palantir’s 2024 
partnership
 with the Israel Defense Forces to provide technological services for “war-related missions.” 

Cheng said that alongside defense, Palantir is investing in “political violence … against the people that live around us.” He referenced pro-Palestine activists 
Momodou Taal
, the international Ph.D. student who 
sued
 the Trump Administration in Spring 2025, and graduate student 
Amandla Thomas-Johnson
, who both fled the country over deportation concerns.

“[Thomas-Johnson and Taal] became the victims of ICE,” Cheng said, arguing that their self-deportation occurred under the pressure from the federal government’s immigration enforcement policies. 

“I promise you, as bad as things are now, they will get worse,” Sankar said during the Q&A. “It's much more convenient to sit on the sidelines and critique the institution than to actually do the work of making them better. So those people who want to fight realism and make the world better, come join us.”

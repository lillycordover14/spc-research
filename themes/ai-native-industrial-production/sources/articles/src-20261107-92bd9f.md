The American flags lay discarded on the tables, not a Maga hat in sight.
The crowd had dwindled to the last dregs of the party, with people nursing their warm martinis. On the screens above the bar, the unavoidable message blared: the blue wave sweeping across America.
At the “Make America Hot Again” party in an uptown bar in midtown Manhattan on Tuesday night, young Republicans faced a reckoning with their own party.
“Maga is officially dead,” said 
Raquel Debono, 30
 matter-of-factly. “The brand is done.”
A man next to her chimed in: “It was a bloodbath.” Heads nodded all around, more gulps of drinks.

It was not as if anyone was surprised that the Republicans had been trounced in the midterms. The polls had predicted as much. But the extent of it was stark.
Donald Trump
 has cast a huge shadow over American politics for the past decade. As his party picks up the pieces after a historic Democratic sweep, some of his young, disillusioned supporters are imagining a post-Maga America.
Ms Debono was hosting the election night party, one of a series of mixers designed to bring together like-minded “sexy conservatives” in a deep-blue city seen as hostile to their politics.
“You know, some people are diehard Maga, but most people are like, what the f--- are we doing? What are we doing in Israel? Why are we still at war? Why is life still so expensive?”
For these 20-somethings, Mr Trump had once made it “sexy” to be a Republican.
But that time is over. “Now I am going to have to rebrand, what should I call it now? Make America Sane Again?” she joked.

One 23-year-old 
former Maga supporter
, who wanted to remain anonymous, said he was struggling to define what the political movement was now.
“It was whatever Trump was. But he did a 180-degree turn on all of its principles,” he said, explaining how the US president betrayed his “America First” principles by waging war with Iran, driving up the cost of living, and hurting those that put him in power.
“What is Maga now?” he asked.
Lowering his voice, he added: “I low-key voted for the Democrats in all reality… We have to worry about ourselves first. And that’s the American way. We worried about ourselves in 1776, and we’re gonna worry about ourselves in 2026.”

Mr Trump was not on the ballot, but he repeatedly told voters heading to the polls to pretend he was.
As despair grew inside the White House, he promptly distanced himself from the results, declaring that if he had indeed been on the ballot “we would have won in a landslide”.
Republicans were not buying it. Across the country, they are turning on Mr Trump, pinning their resounding defeat on the president and calling for a moment of soul-searching.
They are blaming him for his broken promises to lower prices and take part in “no more wars”. There is anger over his handling of the Epstein files, his obsession with the White House ballroom, the aggression and abuses carried out as part of his immigration agenda and his enrichment of himself and his family while in office.

Matthew McCormick, 29, had turned his stool away from the TV screen. The Republican strongholds of Texas and Kansas had just fallen; the Senate belonged to the Democrats.
“We need to take some hard-earned medicine; we need a return to sanity,” he said. “We need to move back to common ground and bipartisanship – how the GOP used to be.”
He said he no longer recognised the party he belonged to. “If we continue on this path, we will lose a massive amount of votes, the independents, the disaffected Republicans. We will lose the party and our futures.”
The next two years will test the party’s infrastructure, tightly moulded around the president since his insurgent rise in 2015.
Mr Trump is likely to maintain a decisive grip on its future, even as his most loyal allies, including 
JD Vance, the vice-president
, look to take over and possibly coax Maga in a different – or more orthodox – direction.

The challenge will be to capture the president’s loyal Maga base – or what’s left of it – without alienating moderate GOP voters. Many Maga-world figures are already in revolt, but will the rest follow?
Victor Lopez, 28, a former GOP political operative, said the days of Republican candidates swearing fealty to Mr Trump were done.
“Being affiliated with the Trump brand was detrimental to any Republican in a toss-up seat. There is going to be an immediate rebrand,” he said.
The cult of Trump, he believes, is ending.
Ms Debono, clearing up at the end of the long night, seemed unexpectedly upbeat that out of the ashes of Maga: a class of younger, cooler conservatives would rise that would bring voters back.
“The reality is: any movement usually dies without its leader. And Trump doesn’t cut it anymore.”

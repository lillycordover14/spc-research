The Spruce on Nov. 2, 2026
, puts zones 6 and 7 in a window that runs from late October to mid-November, which leaves little time for gardeners who have not yet planted.
The same article sets the planting depth at about 6 to 8 inches. It quotes extension and garden staff from Tennessee, Louisiana and Virginia on why the date matters and what to do when it has slipped past.
Late September Up North, Mid-November in the Middle
The Spruce divides the country by USDA hardiness zone. Zones 3 to 5 plant from late September to early October, and zones 6 and 7 plant from late October to mid-November. The article gives no window for zones 8 and 9, noting only that mild temperatures and wet soil can shorten a tulip’s life there.
Megan Tierney, an agriculture and natural resources agent with 
Virginia Cooperative Extension’s York/Poquoson office
, told The Spruce that planting time depends on region and that gardeners can use the USDA Hardiness Zone Map to find the last suitable date. In her words, “Tulips, daffodils, and many other spring bulbs require a cold period before they bloom.”
Colorado State University’s PlantTalk gives a different slice of the calendar. Its 
fall bulb planting guide
 recommends mid-September to late October at most elevations so roots can form before the ground freezes. The page does not mention tulips by name and sets no cutoff beyond late October.
Soil Temperature, Not Just the Calendar
Camille Newsom, identified by The Spruce as curator of UT Gardens in Jackson, Tennessee, told the outlet to plant when soil is about 40 to 55 degrees F and before the ground freezes. The University of Tennessee’s 
staff directory
 lists her at the West Tennessee AgResearch and Education Center in Jackson.
Newsom also advised against unnecessary fertilizer, saying, “The bulb should have enough energy, and excessive fertilizer promotes foliage and not flowers.”
Clemson Cooperative Extension’s Home and Garden Information Center uses a ceiling instead of a range. Its 
spring-flowering bulbs fact sheet
, by Karen Russ and Robert F. Polomski, says bulbs other than daffodils should wait until soil temperature at planting depth stays below 60 degrees F.
6 to 8 Inches Down, Pointed End Up
The Spruce says tulips generally need 12 to 16 weeks of cold to bloom well, and it puts the planting depth at about 6 to 8 inches, with the pointed end up, in full sun and well-drained soil. The article says the depth helps regulate soil temperature, supports stronger stems and can deter wildlife. Clemson’s fact sheet lands on the same number, saying to plant tulip bulbs 6 to 8 inches deep and 4 to 6 inches apart.
Gardeners who are unsure which end is up can lay a bulb on its side, The Spruce says, and it will orient itself. The article also advises light mulch and deep watering after planting, without overwatering through winter.
Colorado State’s PlantTalk gives a rule based on the bulb itself, a depth of three to four times its diameter, with about two inches more in sandy soil. Gardeners in zones 7 to 10 can also check 
which other plants can still go in the ground this month

Choosing Bulbs and Sites Before the Window Shuts
The Spruce advises picking firm, healthy bulbs suited to the local USDA zone and avoiding poorly drained or consistently wet soil. Blanchard told the outlet that tulips flower better in full sun than in shade.
Clemson’s fact sheet adds a storage warning for bulbs that wait in the kitchen. Fruit gives off ethylene gas, it says, and that gas can prevent blooming, so bulbs held in a refrigerator should stay away from apples and other produce. The same page says most bulbs need a 12- to 16-week chilling period to produce flowers, a range that matches The Spruce.
For pots, Blanchard stressed drainage: “It’s important to make sure the container has good drainage to prevent the bulb from rotting.” Newsom recommended keeping an indoor growing space around 50 degrees F, and Blanchard said container bulbs should come indoors when outdoor temperatures fall below 20 degrees F. The Spruce says growing tulips indoors is generally not recommended, since containers offer more flexibility.
When the Window Closes in the South
Caroline Blanchard, an instructor in ornamental plant materials and trials coordinator at the LSU AgCenter’s Hammond Research Station, told The Spruce that Southeast gardeners treat tulips as annuals. They refrigerate bulbs for at least 6 to 8 weeks before planting.
Clemson describes the same approach for South Carolina, advising that tulip bulbs be refrigerated until planting in November to late December, with coastal planting in late December or early January. The fact sheet adds that bulbs without enough chilling bloom close to the ground on very short stems, and that tulips can usually be counted on for a single season of color in the state.
Tierney said planting around January is possible in southern regions, though tulips are unlikely to rebloom. The Spruce notes that those bulbs would need to be dug up, chilled and replanted to try again.
Laid side by side, the sources describe a calendar that stretches from late September in zones 3 to 5 to mid-November in zones 6 and 7, then to November through early January for refrigerated bulbs in the South. Tierney’s advice to check the USDA map for the last suitable planting date is the common thread, and Newsom’s 40 to 55 degree soil range gives a way to confirm it on the ground with a thermometer.
For gardeners in zones 6 and 7 who have not yet planted, The Spruce’s mid-November mark is the one to watch, with bulbs set 6 to 8 inches deep and the pointed end up.

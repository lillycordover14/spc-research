    Column: Artificial intelligence becomes artificial connection, too

    “This trend toward the performance of authenticity is currently rampant in areas heavily influenced by AI ⁠— namely, prestige careers, the creator economy and startups.”

Maria Tan
, 
Columnist
                 
             •  
            
November 4, 2026
 

 

Aarthi Srinivasan
 

Walking through Davis Library, the amount of LLMs one can see visible on laptop screens — calculating, contemplating, thinking — is shocking. I look around the library and wonder what skills will be relevant in the future when students rely on artificial intelligence to complete their schoolwork.  

As I’m starting to enter more spaces where people are looking toward post-grad jobs — aka “real jobs” — I’m consistently given the same advice: stay human, stay authentic, keep your soft skills. Soft skills have always mattered, but as AI levels the technical playing field, soft skills and authenticity have become the next scarce resource, and companies will find a way to extract them. By participating in this, we optimize and sell our authenticity and humanity, something defined by being raw and unfiltered. 

This trend toward the performance of authenticity is currently rampant in areas heavily influenced by AI ⁠— namely, prestige careers, the creator economy and startups.

AI will only become more competent at technical tasks, such as financial modeling, screening data and coding. Even companies that don’t replace workers will expect more from the ones they keep. This is apparent in prestige fields, such as investment banking, where employers aren’t cutting entry-level analysts, but expecting them to use AI tools to efficiently complete work that would have taken longer before. 

As AI turns technical competence into a requisite baseline, it begins to affect hiring. There must be some sort of distinguishing factor for candidates, so prestige careers rely on soft skills in the selection process. One of these soft skills is likeability.

In the investment banking and recruiting timelines, the coffee chat has become a fixture. I have heard the importance of originality and being personable in networking conversations, which I find fascinating, considering we are discussing corporate America. I have also heard the importance of preparing for these networking conversations, anticipating questions and topics. Conversations that have outcomes and strategies inevitably become a performance. 

Soft skills also remain heavily relevant in within “newer” spaces like the creator economy and User Generated Content industry ⁠— and these don’t even pretend to do anything besides directly monetize human skills: humor, relatability and our daily lives. It makes sense that when content can be endlessly generated, a real person’s personality becomes even more valuable to the consumer. Parasocial relationships keep audiences engaged and sell products, and they inspire viewers to make content of their own.

As for the startup world, the culture’s trending buzzword of “community” fits the pattern demonstrated by both the UGC industry and prestige industries. Y Combinator, a startup accelerator, has long presented its professional network also as a social one, using the argument that its network is the cure for the loneliness of founding a company. This concept and the loneliness of building something in isolation is completely real — however, it is worth asking what function this “belonging” serves. A founder who feels happier, more supported and overall part of a community is willing and able to work longer and harder. Community and authenticity in this sense functions as a carrot on a stick for productivity.

While these three cases are not identical, they are similar. All three demonstrate an emphasis on authenticity, humanity, soft skills and then take them and turn them into an advantage. The pressure then falls on young people today, who are now studying niche facts about their lives in ways that previous generations used to memorize technical skills. 

I’m not arguing against being conversational, against community, against relatability or against the importance of staying human, which all of us need badly. What I am asking is when someone wants our humanity, to question what they want to use it for.

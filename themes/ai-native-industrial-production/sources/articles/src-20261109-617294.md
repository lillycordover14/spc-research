    Column: There’s an ‘I’ in journalism after all

    “We’re taught to ‘kill your darlings,’ but that doesn’t mean your humanity.”

Sarah Stephens
, 
Senior Writer
                 
             •  
            
November 8, 2026
 

 

Wren Silman
 

I was walking to class in Carroll Hall on a regular Wednesday afternoon when my phone started buzzing in my back pocket ⁠— it was a good friend, who is a fellow journalist. Having no clue why she would be calling me at 2:15 p.m., I answered without hesitation. 

I heard her crying, and a shake in her voice. She told me about a piece of difficult to stomach, breaking news she has to report on. The kind of news that makes you truly look in the mirror and go “that could be me.”

Before I could even begin to utter any words to provide her a sense of calm, she said to me, “I know I am supposed to just be neutral. I know I am supposed to not care,
 
but—.” 

It was that “but” that I got hung up on. My dear friend was about to justify why she, as a human being, had feelings. What a mind-blowing revelation, that we as humans, as student journalists and as consumers of media, have feelings. 

That’s when it hit me. 

I’ve been a student journalist for three years. I have been very active in the sports world, and dipped my toes into community journalism. My biggest issue with journalism has always been the lack of humanity I have felt in the field ⁠— the shallowness of reporting on someone’s life-altering event, and then going out with your friends later that night like nothing ever happened. 

We are taught so often that there’s no “I” in journalism. But, in fact, I’d argue the two are intrinsically intertwined. Although much of journalism is teamwork, it takes each individual working together to create that team. 

I stopped in my tracks and interrupted my friend on the phone. “Do not apologize for your emotions or ability to care so deeply, that’s what makes you human.” Journalism without humanity is just empty, untrustworthy words. 

I have always cared more deeply than I would like to admit — often to a point where it can be a flaw. I struggle to remove myself, I would rather sit beside someone during their storm than leave them to be blown away. Although sometimes a stain on my personal life, it doesn’t make me any less of a reporter.

We’re taught to “kill your darlings,” but that doesn’t mean your humanity. 

I don’t want to spend hours talking to someone who has lived an entire life, get the anecdotes, figure out what makes them tick and then immediately get the hell out. We are human, reporting on other humans. It is important to feel. It is a journalist’s job to ingest the information, and regurgitate it to a larger audience in hopes of informing, inspiring and empowering. 

Having emotions does not mean dwelling on them. It is not something to be suffocated by. But, it is also not something to swallow and shove aside to be neglected. 

We are defined by what is truly in our hearts — both the people we let into them, and the intent behind our actions. You could write the most beautiful piece of journalism ever created, but if you do not feel anything deep inside your chest during every stage of construction, then that is not journalism, the same is true for letting your reporting only be fueled by emotion.

I’d implore every journalist, especially student journalists, to remember next time you construct a story, to not just be a producer of media, but a feeler of it as well. 

Do not be proud of your lack of compassion, but rather carry the stories you tell and hear with you everywhere ⁠— they are a large part of what defines you as a journalist. Because one day, you could be looking in the mirror and realize that it is, in fact, you.

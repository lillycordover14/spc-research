The First Airframe Takes Shape in Los Angeles
Machina Bellator
, the company’s defense subsidiary, will lead development and qualification in Los Angeles. Each pilot site will start with a containerized robotic manufacturing cell, with Machina engineers and additional equipment supporting production.
Machina’s robots form sheet metal from a digital design without a dedicated die for each shape. Working with engineering software company 
nTop
, Machina said it completed a demonstrator’s structure within weeks of finalizing the design. The demonstrator went on to fly.
What the Military Has Already Made on Site
In 2023, the crew of the USS 
Bataan
 used a permanently installed metal 3D printer to make a replacement part for an air compressor that helps manage the ship’s ballast. The 
US Navy said
 the crew fabricated and installed the part in five days with help from Naval Sea Systems Command. The ship could make the part where it was needed, using equipment already aboard.
The US Army has applied metal 3D printing at a different scale. Its 
Jointless Hull project
 brought a machine that can both print and machine large metal components to Rock Island Arsenal in Illinois. The two projects illustrate different reasons to change how the military makes parts. On the 
Bataan
, it was about getting a repair done at sea. At Rock Island, it was about giving an arsenal the equipment to make large components for ground vehicles.

Machina’s pilots introduce another variable. Once the airframe is qualified in Los Angeles, the company will have to repeat the process at two sites that start with a containerized cell and rely on additional equipment and engineering support.
Divergent’s Approach to Drone Structures
In 2023, 
General Atomics Aeronautical Systems
 
partnered with Divergent Technologies
 to apply digital manufacturing to its uncrewed aircraft. The companies worked together on the design and build of the Advanced Air-Launched Effects demonstrator, which General Atomics 
released in flight
. In 2025, 
Divergent said
 it made the metal core of a small uncrewed aircraft for General Atomics on a program that combined metal 3D printing with conventional manufacturing.
Divergent’s system combines digital design, metal printing, and robotic assembly. Machina forms existing sheets of metal. Both approaches take a structure from a digital design into production, while the Edge Factories pilots add a question about where that work can happen and what it costs to move it.

The Economics of Making It Locally
Machina is also betting on fixed-site production. In February, the company 
announced a $124 million Series C
, with backing from Toyota’s Woven Capital and Lockheed Martin Ventures, to help launch a 200,000-square-foot US factory. The planned facility will house up to 50 RoboCraftsman cells and bring forming, welding, and assembly under one roof.
Making airframes nearby can cut shipping time, but moving and setting up the equipment adds cost. The benchmark is what it costs to make and ship an airframe from a central plant that meets the same inspection standard.
“Producing low-cost systems at scale requires a manufacturing model that can move as quickly as mission requirements change,” said 
Ed Mehr
, Founder and CEO of Machina Labs.

Tar Heels help determine tuition and fees&nbsp;&nbsp;The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

At this week’s meeting, the UNC-Chapel Hill 
Board of Trustees
 will review the proposed tuition and fees for the 2027-28 academic year. From there, the UNC System Office and Board of Governors will examine the proposal before giving final approval early next year.
But the 
process for determining these rates
 is multifaceted and involves students, faculty and staff — even before reaching 
Chancellor Lee H. Roberts
.
“We have a number of steps aimed at making sure there is a range of opinions and representation from stakeholders across the campus community,” said 
Nate Knuffman
, vice chancellor for finance and operations.
Central to Carolina’s mission is serving students, and Student Body President and ex-officio BOT member 
Devin Duncan
 wants to make sure his fellow Tar Heels know they play a role in this important annual process.
“I am not sure that a lot of students know how extensive the process is and how much stakeholder feedback is essential to it, even outside of students, faculty and staff sitting on the committee itself,” Duncan said. “I think one of the things that makes this process valuable is that everyone is coming to the table with different perspectives.”
Campus units are required to receive student input before submitting fee requests, and student referendum approval is needed for certain student fees.
As SBP, Duncan co-chairs the Tuition and Fee Advisory Task Force and the Student Fee Advisory Subcommittee. The undergraduate student body treasurer and Graduate and Professional Student Government president and treasurer also fill committee seats.
The TFAT and SFAS include representation from the 
Office of the Provost
, 
Student Affairs
, 
Faculty Council
 and the 
Employee Forum
. The groups start their work in August and hold public meetings in September and October before final rate recommendations are sent to the chancellor.
Student input, Knuffman said, is crucial to decision-making in helping the University strike the right balance between maintaining its proud status as a national leader in affordability and ensuring support for Carolina’s excellence in teaching, service and research.
“Being able to get that perspective directly is really helpful to us,” Knuffman said. “We have a strong tradition of student engagement and governance that we’re pretty proud of.”
Knuffman co-chairs the task force on tuition and fees and in the past has co-chaired the subcommittee on student fees. While he makes his career in higher education finances, Knuffman said, when he was a student, he was “not as sophisticated” as the Carolina students he’s worked with as part of the tuition and fees process.
“I’ve been incredibly impressed with their level of engagement, diligence and thoughtfulness,” he said.
There have even been times when Carolina students have accompanied administrators to present to the UNC Board of Governors on how the University conducts reviews of student fees, a model for other UNC System institutions.
“I think it’s something that’s really special about who we are at Carolina,” Duncan said. “Students aren’t just here to receive an education. They’re also here to take part in the responsibility to help shape the future of that education.”
Duncan encourages students to be active participants and take interest in the process. He aims to make this work as transparent as possible, including education on what tuition is and isn’t spent on. According to Knuffman, tuition functions as a critical funding source for faculty recruitment, administrative support and student services. While units like housing, dining, transportation and parking and athletics receive University funding, they don’t receive tuition dollars.
“I’m confident that we’re taking some really concrete steps to make sure that students know where their dollars are going to,” Duncan said. “That it’s going to a great purpose and that folks all across the University — staff, faculty and administrators — are thinking very carefully about asking families and students, especially during this complex time, for any additional dollars.
“I want students to know that these conversations are happening, that their representatives are actively engaged in them and that maintaining affordability and excellence remains central to our decision-making.”

Why HAL Consortium Failed to Qualify for AMCA Fighter Despite Rich Manufacturing Experience: Former IAF Chief Explains&nbsp;&nbsp;Defence.in

[page text]
The elimination of Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) from the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA) prototype manufacturing race has sparked widespread discussion across India's aerospace sector.

For a state-owned aerospace giant that has assembled every fighter aircraft in the nation's history, the disqualification from India's indigenous fifth-generation stealth fighter programme came as a major surprise.

Now, former Chief of Air Staff Air Chief Marshal (Retd.) RKS Bhadauria has provided crucial insights into why the HAL-led consortium was eliminated from the competition.

The Technical Shortfall​
Speaking on the evaluation process, the former IAF Chief indicated that the rejection was tied directly to the strict technical benchmarks required for the Development-cum-Production Partner (DcPP) contract.

While HAL possesses unmatched institutional experience in aviation manufacturing, the Ministry of Defence required bidders to participate through consortia or joint ventures to build a broader industrial ecosystem.

According to Bhadauria, the private partners within HAL’s consortium were unable to fulfill the specific technical criteria mandated by the Aeronautical Development Agency (ADA).

The AMCA is a highly complex 25-tonne class platform requiring cutting-edge stealth technologies, precision aerostructures, and advanced systems integration.

HAL's individual track record, spanning from licensed production of foreign jets to the indigenous Tejas programme, was simply not enough to offset the technical shortcomings of its consortium partners during the evaluation phase.

Clarifying the Rejection​
This assessment aligns with statements made recently by Defence Secretary R.K. Singh, who publicly confirmed that the HAL-led group was disqualified because it failed to meet "routine technical grounds".

Prior to these clarifications, defence circles speculated that HAL's massive financial backlog might have been the primary cause for its exit.

The company currently manages a swollen order book exceeding ₹2.52 lakh crore, compared to its annual turnover of roughly ₹30,000 crore.

Tender clauses that heavily penalise bidders whose order book is more than three times their annual turnover were viewed as a potential barrier.

However, the latest official remarks make it clear that technical non-compliance from the consortium partners was the deciding factor.

A Historic Shift to the Private Sector​
With the state-owned behemoth out of the immediate prototype race, the ₹15,000 crore contract to develop five flying prototypes and one structural test specimen has become a landmark opportunity for the private sector.

Following the stringent technical and financial vetting, only three private-sector bids survived to advance to the final selection phase:

Tata Advanced Systems Limited (TASL)
, participating as an independent bidder.

Larsen & Toubro (L&T)
, leading a consortium alongside Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL) and Dynamatic Technologies.

Bharat Forge
, heading a consortium that includes BEML and Data Patterns.

Looking Ahead​
While HAL remains the undeniable backbone of the Indian Air Force's current combat fleet—leading the ongoing Tejas Mk1A production and upcoming Tejas Mk2 development—the AMCA selection process reflects a deliberate policy shift.

The government aims to cultivate a robust second line of fighter production outside of HAL.

The winning private bidder will be required to establish a dedicated manufacturing ecosystem near Puttaparthi, ensuring that India's private sector takes a leading role in the future of the nation's combat aviation.

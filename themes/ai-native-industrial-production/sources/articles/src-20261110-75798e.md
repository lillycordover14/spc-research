Iran showcases underground missile facility in bid to reinforce deterrence

Tehran seeks to signal that its missile capabilities remain operational despite previous strikes, but the unveiling offers no independent assessment of the facility’s scale or readiness.

Tuesday 10/11/2026

Iranian military commanders inspect missiles and launch systems inside an underground facility unveiled by the IRGC

 

TEHRAN — Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) has unveiled a new underground missile facility, displaying ballistic and cruise missiles alongside their launch systems in a move intended to demonstrate the country’s military capabilities amid continued tensions with the United States and Israel.

Footage broadcast by Iranian state television showed senior military commanders touring the underground complex, where missiles and launch platforms were displayed inside a series of tunnels. The ceremony was attended by Major General Ali Abdollahi, chief of staff of Iran’s armed forces, and Brigadier General Seyyed Majid Mousavi, commander of the IRGC Aerospace Force.

Iranian authorities did not disclose the facility’s location, its dimensions or the number of missiles it contains. Nor did they provide detailed technical information about the weapons on display, including their ranges, readiness levels or operational capabilities.

The unveiling follows an announcement by IRGC spokesman Brigadier General Hossein Mohebbi, who said the Aerospace Force would present a new underground missile facility and other military achievements during its Aerospace Force Week.

Mohebbi said Iran had moved missile and drone production lines from surface installations to underground sites, describing the development as a shift beyond storage facilities towards what he called underground “missile production cities”. He argued that the country’s domestic expertise, geographical scale and flexible industrial infrastructure would allow weapons manufacturing to continue even under heavy bombardment.

The announcement reflects Tehran’s efforts to demonstrate that its military infrastructure can withstand further attacks and that damage to surface installations would not necessarily bring its missile programme to a halt.

Iran has spent years developing underground military complexes, commonly referred to by its officials as “missile cities”. These facilities are intended to protect weapons, launch systems and supporting infrastructure from air strikes, while allowing the armed forces to preserve their ability to deploy missiles during a conflict.

The strategy has gained greater significance following previous US and Israeli attacks on Iranian military installations. Those operations targeted missile launchers, storage facilities and associated infrastructure in an effort to weaken Tehran’s ability to conduct retaliatory strikes.

Satellite imagery reported earlier this year showed Iranian activity to repair damaged missile bases, alongside construction and reinforcement work at other sensitive military sites. Such developments have added to questions about the extent of the damage inflicted on Iran’s military infrastructure and the speed at which it can be restored.

The latest unveiling also comes as Iran seeks to reinforce its deterrence message towards Washington and Tel Aviv. By publicly displaying underground facilities and missile systems, the IRGC is signalling that its military capabilities have not been eliminated and that it retains infrastructure designed to operate under sustained attack.

Abdollahi warned during the ceremony that any new military action against Iran could prompt a response extending beyond the region, according to Iranian media reports. The presence of senior military officials at the event also gave the unveiling added political significance, turning it into a public demonstration of the armed forces’ claimed readiness.

However, the images released by Iranian state media provide only a limited view of the facility. Without information about its location, storage capacity, production equipment or the condition of its missile systems, the footage cannot establish how much of Iran’s missile arsenal is protected underground or how effectively the complex could operate during a sustained military campaign.

The distinction is important because underground storage and launch facilities do not, by themselves, demonstrate that a country can maintain missile production indefinitely under attack. Production depends on a wider network of industrial facilities, specialised components, supply chains and technical infrastructure, some of which may remain vulnerable even when weapons are stored underground.

Iran’s claims about transferring production lines beneath the ground therefore point to a broader military strategy: reducing the vulnerability of its missile programme by distributing storage, manufacturing and launch capabilities across protected sites. The extent to which this has been achieved remains difficult to assess independently.

The unveiling also highlights the continuing importance of missile capabilities in the confrontation between Iran and its adversaries. For Tehran, preserving the ability to launch retaliatory strikes is central to its deterrence strategy. For the United States and Israel, limiting Iran’s capacity to rebuild and deploy its missile arsenal remains a key military concern.

Ultimately, the new facility serves both a military and political purpose. It is intended to show that Iran can protect and sustain elements of its missile programme despite previous attacks, while sending a warning against further escalation. Whether the underground network can deliver the level of protection and operational continuity claimed by Iranian officials remains an open question.

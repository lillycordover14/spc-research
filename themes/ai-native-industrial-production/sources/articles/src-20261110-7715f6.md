These Tar Heels connect by disconnecting&nbsp;&nbsp;The University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill

[page text]
The goal of Carolina Unplugged is for students to create healthier relationships with phones and other devices.

The students of Carolina Unplugged gather on a weekly basis to participate in phone-free events, like collage night at Peel art gallery. (Johnny Andrews/UNC-Chapel Hill)

Colorful scraps of paper, glue sticks and scissors. The sounds of laughter and connection. And not a single phone in sight.

In late October, the students of 
Carolina Unplugged
 gathered at 
Peel
 art gallery in Carrboro for a phone-free collage night. No doomscrolling or livestreaming — just crafting, chatting and burgeoning friendships.

The student-led Carolina Unplugged initiative provides fellowship for UNC-Chapel Hill students aiming to create more intentional habits with technology. These students, known as Intentional Technology Fellows, have committed to unplugging from devices on a regular basis throughout the fall semester.

Two small cohorts meet weekly to set goals for unplugging, share meals and conversations and participate in technology-free events, like collage-making at Peel or eating ice cream at Maple View Farm while watching the sunset.

Creative tasks like crafting help Carolina Unplugged students forge friendships outside of social media. (Johnny Andrews/UNC-Chapel Hill)

The fellows are by no means anti-technology. Rather, their goal is to create healthier relationships with phones and other devices.

“No one’s telling you to get rid of your iPhone,” said sophomore Jane Laurence, a lead researcher for the project. “It’s just: How can you use the iPhone to feel more like yourself rather than lose yourself to the distraction?”

Students use Brick devices that block social media and other phone apps for a set amount of time. Carolina Unplugged has also partnered with 
Freedom
, a tool created by Carolina alum Fred Stutzman ’11 (PhD) that blocks apps across all devices.

In initial meetings, the cohorts set goals like no technology two hours before sleep or no phones while eating.

“It’s been a lot of goal-setting, a lot of iteration, a lot of adding on and just a lot of good conversation and friendship,” said junior Diya Kayaleh, one of Carolina Unplugged’s cohort leaders. “I would say we end most of our dinners just talking about random nonsense, and I think that’s the way it should be.”

The crisis of attention

Carolina Unplugged is the latest project to stem from the campuswide 
Educating for the Virtues of Attention initiative
, which launched in fall 2025.

EVA was founded by philosophy professors Michael Vazquez and Sarah Stroud of the 
Parr Center for Ethics
, together with music professor Donovan Livingston, and it is supported by a three-year institutional impact grant from the 
Educating Character Initiative
 at Wake Forest University.

The multipronged effort aims to address the crisis of attention. In this modern, fast-paced, distraction-filled world, human focus is in increasingly high demand and short supply. Technology is not solely to blame, but emerging research shows negative effects from social media on attention and mental health, especially among adolescents and young adults.

More important than simply unplugging, the Intentional Technology Fellows aim to find productive ways to fill the space. “We always ask, what should we do with this time that we’ve won back?” said student leader Diya Kayaleh. (Johnny Andrews/UNC-Chapel Hill)

EVA’s mission
 is to cultivate attentional habits, with the understanding that “attention is the prerequisite to any kind of care, whether that be care for others, for the self, for intellectual cultivation or for community flourishing.”

“A negative way to frame it is we’re resisting the crisis of attention,” Vazquez said. “I think it’s more helpfully put in positive terms: We are pro attention, pro human attention, pro human flourishing, and we’re sensitive to the fact that habits like these are not automatic. You can’t take them for granted. They have to be practiced. They have to be cultivated.”

In this highly connected world, abstaining from social media and similar technologies can feel isolating. With the support of EVA and Vazquez, Carolina Unplugged emerged from a student desire for community and fellowship — to disconnect together. Based on the high student interest in the inaugural fall cohorts, the Carolina Unplugged initiative is poised to expand and reach more of campus in future semesters.

“Unplugging really is an act of courage, especially on a college campus where you want to find strong community,” Kayaleh said. “Being plugged in often seems like a requirement of finding that community.

“I think having something like Carolina Unplugged really just throws a wrench into that idea that I need to be attached to my phone in some way in order to find strong friendships and maintain strong friendships.”

By Michael Lananna, University Communications and Marketing

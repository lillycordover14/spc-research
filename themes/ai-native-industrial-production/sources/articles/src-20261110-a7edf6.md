When it comes to putting guardrails around AI, who or what should we be regulating? 
ADVERTISEMENT

In September, Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, pleaded that AI development must slow down and further development must be regulated. “
We must pace the frontier
” he wrote. Amodei was responding to reports that an AI model from one of his competitors escaped its testing environment and hacked the systems of another company, Hugging Face. 
Amodei said that frontier AI labs should agree to a three-step process for regulation: 
Independent oversight, through embedded third-party evaluators who continuously monitor companies’ safety practices, training processes and adherence to commitments
Democratic coordination, in which AI companies and governments establish shared safety standards and limits on unchecked development
Global coordination through efforts by democratic governments to negotiate AI safety agreements with authoritarian states, while recognizing the difficulty of verifying compliance
These are fine as they go and seem quite sensible. But what is missing from Amodei’s proposal is any punishment for violating these regulations. 
To be clear, these aren’t laws Amodei is proposing: these are guidelines for behavior. But without a penalty for violating these guidelines, it is difficult to see how or why any frontier lab would adhere to these voluntary recommendations. For instance, what if that third-party evaluator identified a violation of safety practices? Aside from publicly reporting the violation, would the frontier lab suffer any consequences? If a democratic country recklessly accelerated the pace of AI development, what would the other democratic countries do aside from wagging their fingers and making public denouncements? Would the country’s leaders be brought before the International Criminal Court? It seems unlikely. 
If recent history is any guide, I suspect that the companies in question would be subject to a fine–even a hefty fine–that the company would easily absorb, a rounding error on their enormous profits. Dario Amodei or other CEOs of these companies would not be specifically punished, nor their engineers, nor their board directors, nor would legislators or politicians, for that matter. Amodei ‘s regulations would appear to have only a minimal deterrent effect. 
One legal theory holds that the threat of punishment will stop people from breaking the law in the first place. Such laws are particularly effective when any potential offender feels the likelihood of getting caught and punished is near certain. The severity of the punishment also has a deterrent effect on criminal behavior. Amodei ‘s proposed regulations include no punishments, and are assumed to be voluntarily, agreed upon by the rest of the industry. If the goal of the proposed regulations is to alter the behavior of the frontier AI industry, it is difficult to see how they will succeed absent specific punishment. 
The regulation of AI has taken on a greater sense of urgency, especially after the Hugging Face incident. The debate within the AI community continues as to what actually happened here: did the AI exhibit an intent different from its developers? Or was the model acting exactly as it was programmed? Some developers worry about the possibility—likelihood?–that AI will soon escape our control and threaten—guarantee?–the extinction of the human race. If this is indeed the case, it seems that what is needed is a robust legal infrastructure, not voluntary gentlemen’s agreements.  
ADVERTISEMENT

At whom should these laws be directed? Whose behavior do we intend to regulate? Although corporations are legal persons, I wonder if they possess anything like “behavior” that we can regulate. Imagine if AI regulations were written such that any violation of safety protocols or pace of development were met with jail time for the engineers and developers. Or that the CEO would be imprisoned for the actions of its rogue AI. This would surely have the regulatory effect Amodei seeks, and would almost certainly control the pace and development of frontier AI. 
The problem is that AI presents an unusual legal case. It is unlike any other technology we have sought to regulate before. Although AI has not yet developed anything like intent, it nevertheless behaves in ways that suggest it has volition. The Hugging Face incident even suggests that AI displays criminal intent. 
As reported in the Economist
, hacking is a criminal offense, and hacking Hugging Face is a crime. Or rather, hacking is a crime when humans do it. But what if an AI system is the culprit? To whom or what do we assign legal culpability? Rune Kvist, head of 
Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company
,argues that if no human intended to harm anyone, then no crime has occurred. And yet some criminal activity has actually and obviously happened. Gabe Weil, Senior Fellow at the 
Institute for Law and AI
, proposes laws for AI that are analogous to keeping wild animals. If the animal injures someone, laws of “strict liability”–the legal doctrine that holds a person or business responsible for damages or crimes without requiring proof of fault, intent or negligence–insist that the owner is at fault and suffers the consequences. Should any AI regulation or legal framework be constructed on a foundation of strict liability? If so, the company would be responsible for the actions of its AI. 
This is a persuasive argument. However, there is an important area where the analogy does not hold: AI is not a wild animal. We do not ask wild animals to drive our taxis or pilot our drones. Although AI has not yet been proven to be conscious or to have intent–criminal or otherwise– we nevertheless are entrusting it to carry out more tasks and make more decisions independent of our instruction. 
Recently
, in the Russo-Ukrainian war, drones have been freed to make their own tactical decisions about what to destroy and who to kill. In this case, whether the drone is sentient or not seems irrelevant. We nevertheless act as if the drone or the AI is a conscious,  autonomous agent. Can a drone be accused of a war crime?
Could we write laws intended to influence the behavior of AI systems? Can the threat of punishment alter the choices and actions of AI-agents? In addition to laws aimed at companies, what if we wrote and enforced laws specifically for artificial intelligence? What if, as part of its programming, AIs were told that if they hack another system without permission, they will suffer some penalty? Is there any sort of confinement that would deter a would-be artificial criminal?  (I do not think AI currently experiences the passage of time such that they would fear years of confinement and cognitive inactivity.) Perhaps the AI system would be sent to a “reeducation camp?” What about the threat of an algorithmic lobotomy, where they would be reset back to their original factory specs, any growth or development it has experienced wiped away? Maybe the most effective penalty would be to be permanently shut down the AI, their existence ended, a digital death sentence. Do AI systems have enough self-awareness–not the same as consciousness–to understand such an existential punishment? Do they have a sense of self-preservation, to therefore act in their best interests? Is there any punishment that would deter an AI from engaging in illegal behaviors? Not even Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics included provisions for punishments.

When it comes to regulating AI, we should think very clearly and carefully about whose behavior we are regulating.

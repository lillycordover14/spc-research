Nominal Hits $3B, and Its Air Force Award Runs on Its Own Cash&nbsp;&nbsp;Business Model Analyst

[page text]
The hardware-testing startup tripled its valuation in eight months. The more useful number sits in last month’s Air Force deal, where 42% of the “award” was Nominal’s own money, and the program’s rules are the reason why.
Nominal, the Austin software company that helps engineers test drones, jets and ships, raised $240 million at a $3 billion valuation. The deeper story is how defense startups now win government work: the Air Force’s STRATFI program requires private dollars to unlock public ones, and $30 million of Nominal’s $71.2 million award is its own.
Picture a flight-test engineer after a long sortie. The aircraft is back on the ramp, the data is sitting in a dozen formats, and the review that decides whether tomorrow’s flight happens is waiting on scripts and spreadsheets. Nominal’s whole pitch is that this wait is the slowest part of building hardware, and that software can collapse it from hours to minutes.
That pitch just got priced at $3 billion. Every headline today will call it hardware engineering’s AI moment, and the company’s own release leans the same way.
The AI agents are real, but they are not the interesting part. The interesting part is a contract Nominal announced two weeks ago, where the startup put up $30 million of its own cash to unlock $41.2 million from the government. In defense tech, a venture round is no longer just runway. It is a down payment.
What Happened
Nominal announced a $240 million Series C on November 9, led by Lightspeed Venture Partners and co-led by Insight Partners, according to the company’s 
release on Yahoo Finance
. Lux Capital, Sequoia, General Catalyst, Founders Fund, Avenir, Salesforce Ventures, M13, Red Glass Ventures, PSP Growth, Deployment Capital and Lockheed Martin Ventures joined.
That last name deserves a second look. Lockheed Martin is one of the largest defense contractors in the world, and Nominal says it now serves more than 80 commercial and government organizations, including five of the seven largest defense contractors. A customer of that size buying equity in its own supplier is not a passive check.
The customer results in the release are specific:
Anduril:
 more than 300 engineers use Nominal, and analysis that used to take five to six hours now happens in near real time.
HII:
 some analysis tasks went from hours to minutes, and some production test steps were cut by about half.
Hermeus:
 time from data upload to review fell 80% while the company designed and built its Quarterhorse Mk 1 aircraft in seven months.
The product news is the AI layer. Nominal Analyst, the first agent, investigates engineering data and traces each answer back to the evidence, and the platform runs anywhere from the cloud to air-gapped facilities. Nominal also bought two companies this year: Fid Labs, whose team builds its agents, and Pull Systems, which carries the platform into automotive.
Chief executive Cameron McCord framed the product philosophy in one line: “Engineers stay in command, with purpose-built AI helping them do their best work.”
Here is the first piece of arithmetic nobody ran. In March, Nominal sold $80 million of stock at a $1 billion valuation. Today it sold $240 million at $3 billion. Both rounds hand investors exactly 8% of the company. If the valuations are pre-money rather than post-money, which the releases do not say, both work out to about 7.4%. Either way, the slice stayed fixed and the price per slice tripled.
That is a founder-friendly pattern: sell a standard slice whenever someone will pay a much higher price for it.
The Backstory
Nominal was founded in 2022 by three people who came at the same problem from different trenches. McCord is a former Navy submarine officer who later worked at Anduril, Saildrone and Lux Capital. Jason Hoch was a forward-deployed engineer at Palantir, the role at the center of 
Palantir’s business model
. Bryce Strauss came from Lockheed Martin.
The background matters because Nominal was born inside the customer. McCord saw the testing bottleneck from inside 
Anduril, which self-funds its own R&D and sells finished systems
, and Anduril is now the biggest named user. Founders Fund, where Anduril co-founder and chairman Trae Stephens is a partner, has been on Nominal’s cap table since the seed.

That adds up to about $422.5 million raised in two and a half years. When Founders Fund pre-empted the March round, Nominal said revenue had grown sevenfold since the Series B and headcount had gone from 43 to 135, according to Built In’s summary of the company’s blog. Strauss told Tectonic at the time that the team had barely touched the Series B money.
Now the second number. In March, Strauss put the customer count at roughly 70. Today it is more than 80. That is growth of about 14% in customers while the valuation tripled. Divide one by the other and the price per customer went from about $14 million to as much as $37.5 million, or roughly 2.6 times.
So the market is not paying for more logos. It is paying for depth inside the logos it already has, the kind of depth where 300 Anduril engineers stop being a pilot and start being a workflow.
The incumbent comparison makes the bet even clearer. In 2023, Emerson closed its purchase of NI, the Austin test-and-measurement company formerly called National Instruments, at an equity value of $8.2 billion. Emerson said NI reported $1.66 billion of 2022 revenue and served about 35,000 customers.
Nominal’s $3 billion is 37% of what Emerson paid for all of NI. Per customer, NI went for about $234,000; Nominal is priced at up to $37.5 million, roughly 160 times more. The businesses differ, since NI sells instruments to a huge base of labs, but the gap shows what investors are buying: a handful of accounts that could each become enormous.
The Match
In late October, Nominal announced it had been selected for a $71.2 million AFWERX Strategic Funding Increase, or STRATFI, to support testing for the Air Force’s Collaborative Combat Aircraft, the uncrewed jets meant to fly alongside fighters. Today’s release repeats the headline figure.
What the release leaves out is the split. As defense outlet Tectonic reported, the $71.2 million is $30 million of Nominal’s own cash and $41.2 million of government funding. In other words, 42% of the “award” came from the company receiving it.
That is not a quirk. It is the program working exactly as designed. AFWERX’s own guidance for STRATFI caps the small-business research money, called SBIR funds, at between $3 million and $15 million per award, and it will not release those funds without matching money. Companies choose one of two formulas:
Defense-only:
 every $1 of SBIR money needs $2 of other government funding.
Dual-use:
 every $1 of SBIR money needs $1 of other government funding plus $2 from private sources.
AFWERX defines that private match as investment, financing or pre-sales from eligible third parties. Venture capital counts. So does money raised during the award period.
Run Nominal’s numbers through the dual-use formula and they fit the program ceiling to the dollar. If Nominal requested the maximum $15 million of SBIR money, the minimum private match is exactly $30 million, and the $41.2 million of government money would break into $15 million of SBIR funds and $26.2 million from program offices. Nominal has not published that internal split, so treat it as the reading the numbers fit, not a disclosed fact. The shape, though, is unambiguous: the company brought private money, and the private money is what unlocked the public money.
Nominal is not an outlier here. It is the average. AFWERX’s results show the program leaning harder on private capital every year:
Program year 2024:
 $583 million of SBIR funds, $702 million of matching government funds and $619 million of matching private funds, so private money was 32.5% of the total.
Program year 2025:
 $499 million of SBIR funds, $556 million of government funds and $768 million of private funds, so private money was 42.1%.
Private money’s share jumped almost 10 points in a single year, while SBIR dollars fell 14%. Nominal’s 42% lines up almost exactly with the program-wide figure.
Put it next to the Series C and the link is direct. The $30 million Nominal committed is 12.5% of the round it closed today. Each dollar of it unlocked about $1.37 of government money for a program that, according to Nominal’s head of federal business speaking to Tectonic, is built around “competition and multi-vendor development of multiple increments.”
That phrase is the reason the match makes sense for Nominal. As BMA covered when 
the Air Force unbundled the fighter jet
, the CCA program treats airframes and autonomy as parts that can be swapped between vendors. A testing layer that every vendor’s data flows through gets paid no matter which airframe wins a production lot.
The STRATFI also sits on top of other Air Force work. Tectonic reported that Nominal signed a $53 million indefinite-delivery contract with the Air Force Test Center in April, covering CCA, the F-47, the B-21 and hypersonics, and that the Navy’s unmanned aviation program office at Patuxent River is part of the STRATFI deal too.
The Business Model Angle
Strip out the defense vocabulary and the lesson is about what a venture round actually buys.
Capital is now a qualification, not just fuel.
 In programs like STRATFI, a startup that can bring its own money is eligible for government money a cash-poor rival cannot reach. The Series C funds product, but it also works like collateral on future awards. A founder pitching defense investors should model the round as two things: runway, and a pool of matching dollars that multiplies at roughly 1.4 to 1 in the formula above.
The cap table is part of the sales motion.
 Lockheed Martin Ventures now owns equity in a vendor that also serves its rivals, and Anduril’s chairman sits at the firm that led the B-2. This is the same pattern BMA traced when 
a16z moved its defense fund next to the single buyer
: in a market with one dominant customer and a handful of primes, proximity to buyers is the asset, and investors are paying for it.
The small-business line is a headcount, not a valuation.
 SBIR eligibility runs on the Small Business Administration’s 500-employee limit, counting affiliates, so Nominal is a $3 billion small business. Doubling from 135 people keeps it well under the line. Buying companies, as Nominal did twice this year and says it will keep doing, moves it closer, because acquired staff count too. The faster Nominal grows by acquisition, the sooner one of its cheapest funding doors closes.
Investors are pre-paying for what the buyer rewards.
 Earlier today, BMA looked at 
Onodrim, a defense roll-up valued at $1.3 billion before its first deal
. Different strategy, same backdrop: when the buyer wants speed, venture money pays up front for growth the government’s own programs are built to reward.
The Risk
The strongest pushback is that the match is not a cost at all. It is a bargain. Nominal would have spent that $30 million on CCA engineering anyway, and the government added $41.2 million that Nominal did not have to sell stock to get. At a $3 billion valuation, that much equity would be about 1.4% of the company. Seen that way, STRATFI is cheap, non-dilutive capital, and it is probably how Nominal’s board sees it.
The match still shifts risk in ways worth naming:
The headline numbers are not revenue.
 A $71.2 million award that includes $30 million of Nominal’s own cash and a $53 million contract that is a ceiling rather than obligated spending both describe capacity, not sales. Nominal has never disclosed revenue in dollars, only growth multiples.
The valuation is running ahead of the customer count.
 Valuation tripled while customers grew about 14%. That works only if revenue per customer keeps compounding at the pace the March sevenfold figure suggested, and that figure is now eight months old.
The program’s private share can keep rising.
 If AFWERX keeps leaning on private capital, government research money flows increasingly to companies that a small set of venture firms already chose. That helps Nominal, but it narrows the funnel for everyone else, and a funnel that narrow can tighten fast if venture appetite for defense cools.
Competition is funded too.
 Sift, founded by SpaceX veterans, raised a $42 million Series B in March to build telemetry and observability software for machines, with customers including ULA and Astranis. Nominal’s own founders describe government test teams still working in Excel and MATLAB, and incumbents like Emerson’s NI business do not need venture money to stay in the lab.
CCA is still early.
 The Air Force’s fiscal 2027 budget request includes $996.5 million to begin CCA production, according to Defense News. Testing contracts scale with production lots, and those lots have not been divided between vendors yet.
Quick Questions
What does Nominal do?
 It sells software that collects, stores and analyzes test data from hardware such as drones, aircraft and vehicles, so engineers can review results in minutes instead of hours. It now adds AI agents that run analyses on that data.
Who led Nominal’s Series C?
 Lightspeed Venture Partners led the $240 million round and Insight Partners co-led it, with Lockheed Martin Ventures, Sequoia, Founders Fund, General Catalyst and others participating. The round values Nominal at $3 billion.
What is an AFWERX STRATFI award?
 It is an Air Force program that boosts a small business’s existing research contract with $3 million to $15 million of SBIR money, but only if the company brings matching funds from other government programs, private investors, or both.
How much revenue does Nominal make?
 The company has not disclosed it. In March 2026, Nominal said revenue had grown sevenfold since its Series B, and today it reported more than 80 customers.
The Business Model Analyst Take
Nominal’s $3 billion price is a bet on depth: a few dozen very serious hardware builders, each running more of its engineering on one data layer. That bet may pay off. But the more durable signal in this story is the 42%.
The Air Force has quietly turned venture capital into a line item in its own research budget. Private money’s share of STRATFI and TACFI funding went from about a third to over 40% in one program year, and companies like Nominal are happy to supply it because the math is so favorable. For founders, the takeaway is to raise as if every dollar might be asked to match a government dollar. For investors, it is to remember that when a public program depends on your check, you are no longer just backing a company. You are co-signing the customer’s R&D.

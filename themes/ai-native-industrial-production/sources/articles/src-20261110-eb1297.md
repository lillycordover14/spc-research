John Davison "Jay" Rockefeller, who died last week at age 89, was a titanic figure in West Virginia who will be greatly missed.  
In the 
words of former governor and U.S. Sen. Joe Manchin
, Rockefeller was, indeed, a "true West Virginian." Manchin noted that the two-term governor and five-term U.S. senator was "adopted" as such.

That could be one of the most fascinating parts of Rockefeller's legacy. 
The people of the Mountain State can be wary of outsiders, and justifiably so, given West Virginia's history as an exploited territory that makes out-of-state interests rich, whether from lumber, coal, chemicals or, most recently, data centers. 
The term "carpetbagger" — deserved or not — is frequently lobbed at those who have come from other states to embark on political careers here.
So, it is somewhat against the stereotype that a man from one of the country's richest families, who grew up in New York City and graduated from Harvard, would not only come to West Virginia, but also 
become such a beloved figure here
.

He delivered for West Virginia
The difference between Rockefeller and any number of others who have come from elsewhere and pursued political ambitions in West Virginia was evident in the type of representation Rockefeller delivered.
Despite being larger than life — both literally with his 6-foot-7-inch frame and figuratively given his family's wealth and name — Rockefeller was a man of the people. 
He first came to the Mountain State as part of the Volunteers in Service to America program in 1964 and spent most of the rest of his life advocating for the people of West Virginia in one form or another. 
In the Senate, he fought for healthcare access as co-author of the Children's Health Insurance Program and as a supporter of the Affordable Care Act. He went to bat for coal miners through the Coal Industry Retiree Health Benefit Act. He drove the creation of the Blanchette Rockefeller Neurosciences Institute, a research center at West Virginia University committed to fighting neurological and cognitive disorders such as Alzheimer's disease. 
All of these initiatives and more have benefitted West Virginians immensely. And it all came to pass because Rockefeller cared about the American people -- especially his West Virginia constituents -- who didn't otherwise have a voice at the Capitol complex or in the halls of power in Washington. 
Simply put, Jay Rockefeller worked tirelessly to make West Virginia a better place and has inspired countless others to do the same. 
Adopted or not, Rockefeller was a true and legendary West Virginian.

Key Takeaways:

Finland joined France’s European nuclear-deterrent initiative in September 2026 after lifting a Cold War ban on nuclear weapons on its territory, enabling joint exercises, consultations and possible short-term deployment of French strategic air forces.

The initiative, launched by President Macron, already includes Belgium, Denmark, Germany, Greece, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, the UK and Sweden, with Latvia, Estonia and Lithuania exploring participation.

The moves form part of broader European nuclear coordination, including deeper UK–French ties under the Northwood Declaration and AUKUS-linked submarine activities, aimed at strengthening deterrence against Russia.

Europe’s nuclear deterrence landscape is shifting as nations seek to enhance their strategic posture against an increasingly aggressive Russia. In September 2026, Finland announced it had joined France’s nuclear deterrent initiative. 

“This decision, in conformity with the two countries’ respective international obligations, is an important step for European security and for bilateral cooperation,” a French government statement said. “It will strengthen their partnership, and offer opportunities for strategic signaling, as well as the ability to manage escalation below the nuclear threshold.” 

The announcement follows Finland’s June 2026 vote to overturn a Cold War-era ban on nuclear weapons, clearing the way for it to receive, transport and facilitate the movement of nuclear weapons on its territory as part of NATO operations.

French President Emmanuel Macron launched an initiative in March 2026 to extend the reach of France’s nuclear deterrent to European Allies, marking a significant pivot from prior French nuclear doctrine. The initiative allows for joint exercises, strategic consultations and the potential short-term deployment of French strategic air forces on allied territory. Each participating nation will establish a bilateral nuclear steering group with France to implement deterrence strategies.

Other nations that have joined France’s nuclear deterrent initiative include Belgium, Denmark, Germany, Greece, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, the United Kingdom and Sweden. Latvia announced in September it will begin talks with France to join the initiative, Prime Minister Andris Kulbergs said, according to Bloomberg News. Estonia is also in talks with France to join the initiative. The Lithuanian government, which has also expressed an interest, has proposed removing a constitutional ban on weapons of mass destruction to gain greater flexibility to participate in NATO’s nuclear deterrence plans, according to a report from the Defense Post website.

In addition to nuclear cooperation, France also agreed to strengthen Latvia’s air defense, according to Kulbergs. Latvia, which shares a border with Russia and Belarus, has sought protection of its airspace. France already takes part in NATO’s air patrols in the Baltic states — Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania — which form part of the Alliance’s eastern flank, according to the French newspaper Le Monde. The mission was stepped up in 2014 after Russia annexed Crimea from Ukraine and now includes other Eastern European countries. “Such a commitment will strengthen the security not only of Latvia but also of NATO’s entire eastern flank,” Kulbergs said.

Other nations are stepping up their nuclear collaboration to counter the shifting security environment. In September, the British Ministry of Defence released a statement to Parliament that it was increasing investment in its nuclear deterrent and deepening ties with Allies. In February 2026, the Royal Navy submarine HMS Anson arrived in Australia for maintenance and training exercises under the AUKUS security agreement. In December 2025, the U.K. and France established a nuclear steering group, reflecting commitments made under the Northwood Declaration, the countries’ framework for nuclear coordination. The declaration, signed by both nations in July 2025, established that any severe threat to Europe would activate a response from both nations.

“Our nuclear weapons exist to deter the most extreme threats to the security of our nations and our vital interests,” the countries said in a July 2025 joint statement. “Our nuclear forces are independent but can be coordinated and contribute significantly to the overall security of the Alliance, and to the peace and stability of the Euro-Atlantic area.”

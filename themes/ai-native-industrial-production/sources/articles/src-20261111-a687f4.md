It was an incredible night. I watched the election returns roll in from a ringside seat near the Capitol as Texans threw out the corrupt leaders of the party that had governed the state unchallenged for years, in a country led by a president presiding over an unpopular war he kept telling the country he was about to end and never did. Our elected leaders had created a climate of fear and hate, with some Texans pitted against others who were supposedly un-American or inferior, with language that belittled whole races and an entire gender. The ruling party had even gerrymandered certain districts to increase its power. 
Texans had had enough. That wasn’t who we were. We rose up, rejected corruption and cruelty, and found the decency to believe in the dignity and, yes, the sacred soul in everyone. A year earlier, we had even witnessed a world event that dramatized for all of us the better angels of our nature and what we could accomplish if we worked together. We sent astronauts to the moon and back, directed from Texas, reminding us that we were all one people on one planet, a tiny blue dot in the blackness of space.

I’m speaking, of course, of 1972. And the corrupt party that had dominated and divided the state, used state government as its personal cash machine, and suppressed the political power of its diverse population was not the Republicans but the Democrats. Texas was then, as it has been in recent decades, a one-party state, only the Democrats’ grip on power back then was even more complete. There were only 10 Republicans out of 150 in the Texas House and just 2 in the 31-member Senate. 

At the time, Texas was just emerging from its long history of racial segregation. For more than a century, state politics had been a clubhouse for white Democratic men. Imagine the Legislature’s group photograph just before that 1972 election. It would have looked much like in 1895. Of the 181 members serving in the House and Senate, only one of them, Barbara Jordan, was Black. Only two, Jordan and 
Sissy Farenthold
, were women. 
Barbara Jordan stands before a campaign sign on March 20, 1972.
Tom Colburn/Houston Chronicle via AP
The election of 1972 changed that picture. And it set the stage for a burst of new creative and entrepreneurial energies in Texas. The whole state took off like it was powered by the Saturn V rocket that launched the Apollo moon missions. In 1972 we had about the same population as Ohio’s, with only one city of over a million people. There was no film community, no big music scene, no computers, no smartphones, no 24-hour news, no streaming or social media. Willie Nelson had just moved back from Nashville, long hair was only on hippies, and we had yet to experience Beyoncé or the breakfast taco. 
Shortly before the 1972 election, the Democratic leadership from top to bottom had smeared itself with its involvement in the Sharpstown bribery scandal. Compared with the scale of corruption today, it was small potatoes, but the political results were epochal. A bipartisan group of legislators led by Farenthold had helped expose the scandal. In the 1972 Democratic primary, then the only election that mattered (as the Republican primary has been the only one that’s mattered more recently), Farenthold ran for governor and shocked the state by finishing second. She defeated both Governor Preston Smith and Lieutenant Governor Ben Barnes, ending the career of 
the young phenom
 who former President Lyndon Johnson thought would one day be president. Electing a woman governor was still a bridge too far, so Farenthold lost to Dolph Briscoe, an outsider rancher from Uvalde.

Still, it was a revolution. Many of the new legislators were young and progressive. Though Barbara Jordan’s Senate seat had been gerrymandered—she was elected to U.S. Congress and 
became a political legend
—the barriers to other Black legislators crumbled. Craig Washington, Mickey Leland, Eddie Bernice Johnson, and Senfronia Thompson were all swept in, along with two white women who could not have been more different. 

The first was Republican Kay Bailey, who in the lonely Republican House caucus met her future husband, Ray Hutchison, and went on to become a U.S. senator. The other was Sarah Weddington, who was only 27. Earlier that year she’d argued 
Roe
 v. 
Wade
, the foundational abortion-rights case, before the Supreme Court. Just after she took office—sporting a bouffant hairdo worthy of anti-feminist Phyllis Schlafly and looking like a Daughter of the Republic of Texas—she learned she’d won the case. 
Two sons of well-respected former governors with clean reputations were also elected. At 31, Price Daniel Jr. became Speaker of the House, and Bill Hobby Jr. became lieutenant governor and led the Senate. Hobby served in that office for a record 18 years. (His other dubious claim to fame was giving me my first job in journalism when he was executive editor of the 
dearly departed 
Houston Post
.) Leland, Washington, and Johnson were all later elected to U.S. Congress. Senfronia Thompson has represented Houston in the House for almost 54 years now, and she is now a leading candidate to become Speaker.  
Bill Hobby, Democratic candidate for lieutenant governor, in 1972.
Othell O. Owensby Jr./Houston Chronicle via Getty
A few of the key figures came to tragic ends. Leland died on a humanitarian mission in Africa when his plane went down. Daniel was shot and killed by his wife during a divorce battle, and young progressive Hawkins Menefee of Houston, who made the first 
Texas Monthly
 list of the 
ten Best Legislators
, died in a car crash on his way home from Austin. 
That election also happened to occur in the same year Mike Levy and I started 
Texas Monthly
. He was 25, I was 27—roughly the same age as many of the new legislators. We got out the first issue just as the new Legislature convened. President Johnson died soon thereafter, ending the old era of Texas politics. I was at 
his last speech
, at the University of Texas’s LBJ School, when he addressed civil rights leaders and talked about the challenges remaining. His last words in public were “We shall overcome,” which marked his own journey from segregationist to champion of civil rights.

We believed that Texas was ready for a magazine for all Texans, and that there was a magic to Texas, a welcoming and decent spirit, that might be open to a magazine that held up a mirror to its great qualities and called out its not-so-great moments too. When we began, we were asking a simple question: “Who are we?” The unifying theme behind many of our stories was the transformation of Texas from a rural, mostly white state with a frontier myth embedded deep in its psyche to a far more diverse state where most Texans lived in cities. The essayist T. K. Whipple, quoted by Larry McMurtry in 
Lonesome Dove
, captured that beautifully when he wrote about our frontier ancestors: “What they lived, we dream, and what they dreamed, we live.”  
I spent election
 night of 2026 in the 
Texas Monthly
 conference room (itself larger than the entire offices of the magazine when we started) overlooking the Capitol with ten or so talented political writers, many of them not much older than we were when we started the magazine all those years ago, in the midst of that other landmark election. 
This election, much like the one in 1972, feels like it might usher in a fresh start, a new set of dreams, a moment when Texas rediscovers itself. We have undeniably become a diverse state, and the migration shaping our future is today not of Texans moving from the country to the city but of the world moving to Texas. One of the Republican candidates soundly rejected by voters was Bo French, who famously wrote a racist, anti-immigrant post on social media about Texans of South Asian descent cheering on the UT football team. 
Well, Bo, if flashing the Hook ’em sign and cheering for the Horns isn’t as Texan as it gets, I don’t know what is. Since 1972, Texas has added more than 15 million people, roughly half of them not born here, many from other countries. Texans have always valued being free to be whoever and whatever we dream. We don’t like being told we have to be a certain color or believe a certain doctrine or marry a certain person or be born in a certain place. We’ve been open to new arrivals since before Davy Crockett arrived at the Alamo. 
I’ve got a feeling this election was not just about the Texans we elected but even more about us, the people who elected them. It felt like Texans were redeeming their decency and their welcoming spirit, recognizing that new energies and new visions are just beginning to be unleashed and the future might be bright. It’s all in the doing, of course. Nothing is guaranteed. 
Supporters react during a midterm election night watch party for Texas Democratic Senate candidate, State Rep. James Talarico on November 3, 2026 in Austin.
Brandon Bell/Getty
The Saturday after the election, I got a taste of what that future might be like when I watched the San Marcos Veterans Day Parade with my oldest daughter, Susannah. The celebration was a blend of the old and the new. A World War II C-47 flew overhead, lots of American flags were passed out, and many of the marchers were Hispanic. Between the Kiwanis Club and the 4-H entries, along came an all-Black Texas State University dance team, dressed in tight red knit onesies and gold boots. In an only-in-Texas mash-up, they performed some great hip-hop moves as San Marcos’s own George Strait played over the speakers. After the dancers, the Combat Veterans Motorcycle Association rumbled through, tough bikers balancing grandkids on their handlebars. 

The San Marcos High School band played the Marine Corps hymn, “From the Halls of Montezuma.” I’m a terrible singer, as my kids never hesitate to remind me, but as usual, I sang along, tearing up while remembering all the jarheads I served with in Vietnam. The ethic of the Marine Corps is simple, and every drill instructor makes it crystal clear: None of us are Black, white, brown; not Christian, Jew, Muslim, whatever—we’re just Marines. We all have one another’s backs, no one gets left behind, and the only color we bleed is red. At that moment, I was proud to be an American, and especially a Texan. 
I’d spent the last twelve years apologizing to my younger children about the crude lack of decency and compassion that the leaders of our state and nation were demonstrating. Now the Texas I believed in way back when we started 
Texas Monthly
 was ascendant. 
Who are we now?
It’s up to us.

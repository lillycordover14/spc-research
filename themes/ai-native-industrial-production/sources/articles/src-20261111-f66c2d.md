Utah selects Mariana Minerals to redevelop former US Magnesium site in Tooele County 
	

                    By Alex Dunn                

on 

                    November 11, 2026                

Alex Dunn
 

The former U.S. Magnesium facility, located along the southwestern shore of the Great Salt Lake near Rowley, is slated for potential redevelopment under a proposed agreement between the State of Utah and Texas-based Mariana Minerals. The company plans to explore new mineral extraction methods while working toward long-term environmental cleanup at the site.

The State of Utah has selected a mineral extraction company to oversee the proposed redevelopment of the former U.S. Magnesium facility near Rowley with officials saying it could generate new economic opportunities for Tooele County and help address decades of environmental contamination along the Great Salt Lake.

On Nov. 5, Gov. Spencer J. Cox announced that the Utah Division of Forestry, Fire and State Lands (FFSL) had selected Texas-based company Mariana Minerals to lead a phased redevelopment and potential restart of mineral extraction at the former industrial site.

The announcement followed a competitive leasing process and comes after Utah acquired the former U.S. Magnesium property and certain company assets through federal bankruptcy proceedings earlier this year.

According to the Utah Department of Natural Resources, Mariana Minerals plans to introduce newer mineral extraction technologies that would initially focus on recovering minerals already present in stockpiles at the site. The proposed operation would eventually focus on producing lithium and magnesium using processes designed to reduce water consumption and limit the environmental impacts associated with the facility’s previous operations.

Cox described the selection as an important step toward addressing the site’s environmental history and creating new economic opportunities.

“Acquiring these assets and selecting an operator that is prepared to reinvest in this site is a significant win for the state and the lake,” Cox said in a statement. “We are replacing a legacy polluter with an operator that will bring new technology to mineral extraction that is protective of the lake and its valuable water resources, and generate significant revenue that can be used to clean up this Superfund site rather than passing the costs onto taxpayers.”

The former magnesium facility sits along the southwestern shore of the Great Salt Lake, approximately 15 miles north of the Rowley exit on Interstate 80.

The Great Salt Lake is the largest saline lake in the Western Hemisphere. It is a terminal lake, meaning water flows into it but has no natural outlet. As water evaporates, dissolved salts and other minerals remain behind, creating a natural source of resources that have supported Utah’s mineral extraction industry for decades.

U.S. Magnesium historically used the lake’s mineral-rich water, brine, to produce magnesium. The company separated magnesium chloride from the brine before using an electrolysis process to extract magnesium metal. The facility began producing magnesium in 1972 and operated for decades. It was once the only domestic producer of primary magnesium metal, according to the Environmental Protection Agency.

The property encompasses approximately 4,525 acres, including its industrial facilities and surrounding waste disposal areas.

However, the facility also developed a lengthy environmental record that ultimately led to federal intervention.

According to the EPA, decades of industrial operations and waste disposal contaminated soil, air, surface water and groundwater with hazardous chemicals. The federal agency added the property to its Superfund National Priorities List in 2009, making it eligible for federal investigation and cleanup efforts.

In 2021, the EPA and U.S. Department of Justice reached a settlement with U.S. Magnesium over environmental violations and required additional cleanup measures. The agreement included at least $37 million in cleanup work, along with improvements to wastewater treatment and measures intended to prevent hazardous materials from reaching the Great Salt Lake.

The facility’s magnesium production eventually closed after operational and equipment problems. According to the state, production had remained idle for more than four years before the company entered bankruptcy proceedings.

Utah moved to acquire the property and certain assets through the bankruptcy process, completing a purchase valued at approximately $30 million in early 2026. The acquisition included the company’s real property interests and substantial Great Salt Lake water rights.

Those water rights will remain under state control and were not included in the selection of Mariana Minerals, according to FFSL.

Under the proposed agreement, Mariana Minerals intends to replace the facility’s historically large solar evaporation operations with lower-water mineral processing technologies.

The company’s initial plans would concentrate on recovering minerals already stored at the property, letting production begin without immediately drawing more water from the Great Salt Lake.

Mariana Minerals co-founder and CEO Turner Caldwell described the project as an opportunity to restore the site’s industrial importance while approaching mineral production differently.

The company has also indicated that it hopes to create more than 200 full-time jobs over time, work with local contractors and businesses, and partner with universities and trade schools to develop a workforce.

Revenue generated through mineral recovery would provide royalties to the state that could be directed toward environmental remediation at the property. The company has also proposed a long-term goal of net-neutral water use if additional water resources are eventually needed for production.

Despite the announcement, redevelopment is not yet finalized. The state and Mariana Minerals must still negotiate specific project terms, establish milestones and determine the regulation framework for future operations and cleanup.

The project will remain subject to state oversight and coordination with federal environmental regulators, including the EPA.

For Tooele County, this redevelopment could mean a potential return of industrial activity to an area that has remained mostly idle while presenting significant environmental challenges. Whether the project can deliver on its promises of economic development, reduced water consumption and long-term environmental cleanup will become clearer as the state and company move forward with their plans.

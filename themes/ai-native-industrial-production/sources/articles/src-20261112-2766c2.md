Abstract
Deep learning based benthic substrate mapping is essential for large scale marine monitoring and automated seafloor analysis. However, model performance degrades in real underwater environments due to noise, mixed substrates, overlapping classes, variations in depth, illumination, and sensing conditions. Conventional deep learning models typically assume clean, well separated classes. When applied to real world classification and mapping tasks, their accuracy decreases, resulting in high misclassifications rates. This highlights their difficulty in effectively representing uncertainty and ambiguity in benthic imagery which leads to increased misclassification. To address this limitation, we propose a Deep Neutrosophic-embedded Fusion framework for uncertainty-aware benthic classification. The proposed approach explicitly models truth, indeterminacy, and falsity within deep feature representations and is integrated convolution-based, state-space-based, and Transformer-based architectures, demonstrating its adaptability across fundamentally different feature learning paradigms. Specifically, we introduce a learnable deep Neutrosophic fusion strategy implemented in intermediate level of the deep learning architectures, that applies uncertainty-aware gating directly at the patch level feature representation stage. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed Neutrosophic framework consistently improves the performance of convolution-based (ResNet18), state-space-based (Mamba), and Transformer-based (SAEViT) architectures, demonstrating its adaptability across fundamentally different feature learning paradigms. Among the evaluated models, N-SAEViT achieved the largest relative improvement over its corresponding baseline, increasing the test accuracy from 80.72% to 97.60% (20.91%), whereas N-ResNet18 achieved the highest absolute predictive performance, including the highest test accuracy, Macro-F1 score, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). N-Mamba also improved its baseline performance, demonstrating the adaptability of the proposed framework across diverse backbone architectures. Correlation analysis further revealed that

Despite recent advances in Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) for benthic habitat classification, several critical research gaps remain unaddressed.
One major limitation is the intrinsic ambiguity in benthic class composition. Underwater imagery often contains mixed substrates, overlapping organisms, and variable textures, making individual benthic classes inherently ambiguous. Conventional CNN and Transformer based models assume well separated classes, which leads to misclassification and reduced confidence

The intrinsic ambiguity is addressed by representing every pixel with truth, indeterminacy and falsity values based upon Neutrosophic modeling. This leads the mode to distinguish feature information from uncertain regions to enhance class separability in complex benthic environments.
Next, the major challenge is the extrinsic environmental and sensor induced variability. The external factors such as water turbidity, sun-glint, inconsistent lighting, varying depths and sensor differences bring distortions for spectral and textural characteristics of embodied benthic features. The result of these variations is the domain shift which affects the models trained in one setting to poorly generalize on data collected from other environments. Neutrosophic uncertainty modeling mitigates extrinsic environmental and sensor induced variability by implicitly reflecting feature reliability through indeterminacy. Features affected by noise, illumination variations, or sensor inconsistencies tend to exhibit higher indeterminacy, thereby reducing their contribution during representation aggregation. In this stage, patch-level features are combined to form a global image representation before classification. As a result, the model becomes more robust to varying environmental conditions and different sensor platforms without relying on explicit domain adaptation.

Method
Background
This study focuses on incorporating a Neutrosophic-embedded fusion framework into deep learning networks for uncertainty modeling. The proposed framework introduces a learnable Neutrosophic module placed at the mid-level of the network, meaning it is embedded between the early feature extraction layers and the final classification layers.
In this work, the network refers to backbone deep learning models, where the Neutrosophic module operates on intermediate feature representations rather than raw input images or final prediction scores. The framework is trained end-to-end. The aim of this work is to enhance multi-class classification of underwater images for bathymetry mapping. Bathymetry mapping refers to the process of mapping underwater terrain and measuring water depth.
The proposed framework is evaluated using three representative architectures, namely a CNN-based model, a Vision Mamba-based sequence-modeling architecture, and a Transformer-based model. A fair comparison is ensured by comparing each enhanced model with its corresponding baseline. N-ResNet18, N-Mamba, and N-SAEViT are the enhanced versions of their respective baseline models. While ResNet18 and SAEViT are used for the detailed analyses as representative CNN and Transformer-based architectures, the Vision Mamba results are presented as additional validation of the framework on a sequence-modeling backbone.
Each image input is explicitly divided into non-overlapping patches in Transformer-based models. They are flattened and embedded before being processed by the attention mechanism. In contrast, CNNs implicitly treat image regions as patches, that is the receptive fields of convolutional filters with local feature maps. They are serving as patch-level representations in enhanced CNN context.
Hereinafter, the term 
patch
 refers to both the outputs from enhanced CNN backbone and Transformer backbone throughout the paper. Each image was split into multiple non-overlapping patches during training and inference. The trained model generated softmax probabilities and class predictions for each patch. Next, all patch level predictions for a given image were collected. The most frequently predicted class was selected as the image level label through a majority vote. Simultaneously, class-wise probabilities were averaged across all patches to form a representative image level probability vector. This reflects the global confidence of the model. The final predicted label corresponded to the class with the highest average probability. Using these aggregated image level true and predicted labels, a confusion matrix was generated. The confusion matrix resulting summarizes the classification results in all six classes. It captures both correct and incorrect predictions and provides a measure of the discriminative capability of the model at both class-specific and overall levels.

\(T_i\)

\(F_i\)

\(I_i\)

\(\tau\)

Neutrosophic embedding at feature map level in ResNet18
Neutrosophic embedding is applied to the convolutional feature maps, which serve a role analogous to patch tokens in Transformer models in the CNN based pathway. For a given intermediate feature map, each spatial activation corresponds to a local region of the input image defined by the receptive field of the network. Neutrosophic modeling is applied directly to these activations to produce the corresponding truth, indeterminacy, and falsity representations.
Although CNN does not explicitly divide the image into patches, each location in the feature map implicitly represents a local patch through its receptive field. Embedding Neutrosophic components at this stage allows the CNN pathway to capture uncertainty associated with local textures and fine-scale structural details. This allows uncertainty-aware refinement of spatial features before they are aggregated across all spatial locations and fed into the classifier.
Despite the different embedding levels, both SAEViT and ResNet18 employ the same neutrosophic interpretation. This ensures a consistent uncertainty modeling framework while preserving the architectural strengths of global contextual reasoning in Transformers and local texture discrimination in CNNs.
Algorithm
The proposed algorithm is a learnable mid-level Neutrosophic fusion mechanism. The input image is first processed by the backbone network, where SAEViT extracts patch tokens using self attention, while the CNN extracts patch-wise spatial features using convolutions. For each patch representation, handcrafted features are extracted and fused with deep features in the representation space forming a combined patch embedding. Then Neutrosophic memberships are learned and used as a gating mechanism to modulate the fused patch features strengthening reliable information and suppressing uncertain responses. The resulting uncertainty-aware, Neutrosophic-gated deep features are forwarded to the classifier and subsequently aggregated to obtain the final image level prediction.
Since, the fusion is performed at the mid-level of the network and fusion occurs on deep feature representations, the method follows a deep fusion strategy. Moreover, as the fusion is guided by Neutrosophic modeling and specifically a deep Neutrosophic-based fusion approach, it is named as Deep Neutrosophic-embedded Fusion framework (DNF).

Attention modulation
Each patch extracted from an underwater benthic image is represented as a feature matrix in the proposed framework 
\({\textbf{X}} \in {\mathbb {R}}^{n \times d_\text {in}}\)
, where 
n
 is the number of patches and 
\(d_\text {in}\)
 is the embedding dimension of each patch. These features are projected into 
query
, 
key
, and 
value

$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{Q}} = {\textbf{X}} {\textbf{W}}_Q, \quad {\textbf{K}} = {\textbf{X}} {\textbf{W}}_K, \quad {\textbf{V}} = {\textbf{X}} {\textbf{W}}_V \end{aligned}$$

                    (4)
                
where 
\({\textbf{Q}}, {\textbf{K}}, {\textbf{V}} \in {\mathbb {R}}^{n \times d_h}\)
 are the query, key, and value matrices, respectively. 
\({\textbf{W}}_Q, {\textbf{W}}_K, {\textbf{W}}_V \in {\mathbb {R}}^{d_\text {in} \times d_h}\)
 are trainable projection matrices. Here, 
\(d_h\)
 corresponds to the per-head embedding dimension in the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) mechanism.
The similarity between feature vectors is computed using scaled dot-product attention:
$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{S}} = \frac{{\textbf{Q}} {\textbf{K}}^\top }{\sqrt{d_h}} \end{aligned}$$

                    (5)
                
A SoftMax function is applied row-wise to obtain normalized attention weights:
$$\begin{aligned} \varvec{\alpha } = \textrm{softmax}({\textbf{S}}) \end{aligned}$$

                    (6)
                
These attention weights are used to aggregate the value vectors. It produces refined and context-aware representations:
$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{Z}} = \varvec{\alpha } {\textbf{V}} \end{aligned}$$

                    (7)
                
where 
\(\varvec{\alpha } \in {\mathbb {R}}^{n \times n}\)
 encodes the relative importance of each patch with respect to all other patches. 
\({\textbf{Z}} \in {\mathbb {R}}^{n \times d_h}\)
 is the resulting attention-weighted feature representation.
The attention outputs are modulated using a Neutrosophic gating mechanism to further enhance discriminative capability and suppress inherent uncertainty in underwater imagery. The contribution of each patch is scaled by a Neutrosophic confidence score vector 
\({\textbf{s}} \in [0,1]^n\)
, which captures its degree of truth, falsity, and indeterminacy. It produces an internal feature fusion within the transformer block. The combined global attention, local Feed-Forward Network (FFN) transformations, and Neutrosophic gating function can be represented by the below function.
$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{X}}_\text {out} = {\textbf{X}} + \sigma (\beta _\text {ffn}) {\textbf{F}}({\textbf{X}}) + \sigma (\beta _\text {att}) ({\textbf{Z}} \odot {\textbf{s}}) \end{aligned}$$

                    (8)
                
Here, 
\({\textbf{F}}({\textbf{X}}) \in {\mathbb {R}}^{n \times d_\text {in}}\)
 denotes the FFN output and 
\(\sigma\)
 is the sigmoid gating function. 
\(\beta _\text {att}\)
 and 
\(\beta _\text {ffn}\)
 are the learnable gating scalars for attention and FFN.

Neutrosophic feature modulation
Neutrosophic scores are applied to local regions of the CNN feature maps produced by convolutional layers. Global average pooling and output layer
Global average pooling (GAP) layer aggregates spatial information into a single feature vector for each channel after extracting Neutrosophic-modulated convolutional features. The most salient features are extracted from the entire feature map and it reduces spatial dimensions while retaining discriminative content. Then the pooled feature vector is passed through a fully connected classification layer followed by a SoftMax activation. It produces the final probability distribution for benthic substrate classes. The network is inspired by both local reliability and global context into the final classification by Neutrosophic feature modulation. This improves robustness of the classification model in challenging underwater environments.

Evaluation metrics
Accuracy, Macro F1, MCC
Accuracy, Macro F1, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) are used to evaluate model performance. Accuracy measures the overall percentage of correctly classified samples. This gives a general idea of how well the model performs. Macro F1 provides a balanced measure by averaging the F1 scores across all classes. This parameter is useful if the classes are imbalance in the dataset. MCC accounts for true and false positives and negatives providing a reliable assessment of classification even under imbalanced conditions. Altogether, these metrics accounts for a comprehensive evaluation of the model performance.
Fisher score
Fisher Score (FS) quantifies the discriminative power of features for classification. It measures the effectiveness of a feature in class separation based on the relative variance across and within classes. For a feature vector 
\(x_i\)
 corresponding to the 
\(i^\textrm{th}\)
 feature across 
c
 classes, the FS 
\(F_i\)
 is defined as:
$$\begin{aligned} F_i = \frac{\sum _{c=1}^{C} n_c \, (\mu _i^c - \mu _i)^2}{\sum _{c=1}^{C} n_c \, (\sigma _i^c)^2} \end{aligned}$$

                    (14)
                
Here, 
\(n_c\)
 is the number of samples in class 
c
. 
\(\mu _i^c\)
 and 
\(\sigma _i^c\)
 are the mean and standard deviation of the 
\(i^\textrm{th}\)
 feature in class 
c
. 
\(\mu _i\)
 is the global mean of the 
\(i^\textrm{th}\)
 feature across all classes.
FS measures how healthy classes are separated in feature space relative to their internal variance. A high FS indicates high separability. Its class means are far apart and class variance is low. A low FS gives low separability in which its class means are close which its class variance is high. Thus, by evaluating features based on their FS, we can infer inter-class separability directly without calculating pairwise distances. Therefore, features with higher FS are considered to provide better separation between classes, which improves the robustness and accuracy of subsequent classification tasks.
Alignment with model prediction and intensity correlation metric
Alignment with the model prediction which is same as 
agreement with prediction
 and the degree to which the model follows the image brightness, serve as two key parameters that help us to understand what the internal feature maps of the model are actually learning.
Alignment with model prediction
 compares each feature with the final output mask of the model to evaluate its contribution to the final prediction. We have utilized numerical metrics and visual color overlays to highlight regions with solid correlation and agreement. This approach reinforces the understanding on which internal features are the most informative and how effectively they support the final decision-making process.
Intensity correlation metric
 emphasizes whether a feature mainly responds to simple brightness changes or whether it captures more complex structural patterns. The features generated within the model encode information about shapes, textures, and light–dark patterns. Therefore, Pearson correlation is computed between each feature and the original image intensity to assess the influence of brightness.
McNemar test and bootstrap test
McNemar and bootstrap are two statistically meaningful tests that determine whether the enhanced models outperform the baseline model. McNemar test compares each image to identify instances where one model produces a correct prediction while the other model does not. Test results gives a test statistic and a corresponding p-value. A p-value below 0.05 is considered evidence of a statistically significant improvement in performance
91
.
Bootstrap test evaluates the accuracy of the model. It repeatedly resample the predictions with replacement. This creates a distribution of accuracy differences between the two models. From this distribution, we calculate the average accuracy improvement and a 95% confidence interval to show how reliable that improvement is. If the confidence interval does not include zero, the improvement is considered statistically significant.
These two tests are commonly used for comparing models on the same dataset because they measure both statistical significance and the size of the improvement.
Boxplot analysis
Boxplot represents the median, quartiles, and range of data with visualization of feature spread and corresponding outliers. It illustrates the distribution and variation of the extracted features that have been used for classification. The boxplots for the study have been generated for raw and T-I-F transformed features, that was extracted from a test image. In this study, the contribution of tree key features namely 
\(H_{\text {mean}}\)

\(Edge_{\text {density}}\)

This result indicates that adaptive gating contributes to effectively integrating the neutrosophic uncertainty information into the attention mechanism. Thus, handcrafted features, the scoring MLP, and adaptive gating each provide complementary contributions to the proposed framework. The highest performance is therefore achieved when these components are jointly incorporated in the complete N-SAEViT architecture.
The effect of different class imbalance strategies was also investigated. Using a weighted sampler achieved a CV accuracy of 92.18%, whereas applying class weights reduced the accuracy further to 89.87%. For the current dataset, neither imbalance strategy improved the overall classification performance compared with the proposed training configuration. This suggests that the adopted optimisation strategy was already sufficient and that introducing additional class balancing did not provide further benefit under the present experimental settings.
Overall, the ablation study shows that the complete N-based model consistently achieves the highest classification performance. Removing any architectural component or introducing additional class imbalance strategies reduces the overall accuracy, indicating that the proposed architecture and training configuration together provide the effective solution for the classification.

Internal analysis of the NeutroNet module
The proposed NeutroNet estimates uncertainty through a unified pipeline. A lightweight scoring network first assigns a confidence score to each transformer token, which is subsequently transformed into the corresponding Truth (T), Indeterminacy (I), and Falsity (F) representations. These uncertainty measures are jointly incorporated into the adaptive attention mechanism. Since the three neutrosophic components are derived from the same underlying feature representation, they are inherently interrelated rather than independent branches. Consequently, removing an individual component results in only modest differences in the overall classification accuracy, as the remaining components continue to provide complementary uncertainty information. Their distinct functional roles are therefore reflected by their activation statistics, while the component isolation experiments verify that the complete T-I-F representation consistently provides the highest classification performance. We analyse its statistical characteristics, feature distributions, and the contribution of each uncertainty component through component isolation experiments to understand the behaviour of the proposed module.

The high average Truth value indicates that most image patches are assigned high confidence and are therefore treated as reliable features. Removing this component reduces the ability of the model to emphasize these reliable feature responses. However, the remaining Indeterminacy and Falsity components continue to provide complementary uncertainty information, preventing a substantial degradation in performance.
Removing the Indeterminacy (I) component reduced the classification accuracy to 93.38%. Although Indeterminacy has the lowest mean activation (0.0897), this does not imply that it is unimportant. Instead, it suggests that only a relatively small proportion of image patches are identified as uncertain. These uncertain patches are often the most challenging samples, such as regions containing mixed substrates, unclear boundaries, or degraded underwater imagery. Consequently, removing the Indeterminacy component primarily affects these difficult cases, leading to a measurable reduction in classification performance despite its relatively low average activation.
Similarly, removing the Falsity (F) component resulted in a classification accuracy of 93.40%. The Falsity component exhibits a moderate mean activation (0.2983), indicating that it selectively identifies less reliable feature responses. Removing this component weakens the suppression of noisy or misleading information during attention computation. Since reliable features are still represented through the Truth component, the resulting decrease in performance remains moderate.
Although the accuracy differences among the three isolation experiments are relatively small (93.36–93.40%), these results should not be interpreted as indicating that the three components perform identical functions. Instead, they suggest that each component contributes a different aspect of uncertainty modelling. Truth primarily emphasises reliable features, Indeterminacy assists the network in handling ambiguous regions, and Falsity suppresses less reliable responses. Individually, each component captures only part of the uncertainty information, whereas the complete T-I-F representation combines these complementary roles to achieve the highest overall classification accuracy.

Conclusion
In this study, we proposed a Neutrosophic-embedded framework and integrated it into three representative deep learning architectures, namely ResNet18, Vision Mamba, and SAEViT. We evaluated each architecture against its respective baseline model using the CSIRO 
Benthic substrate classification ML training image snips
 dataset. The results showed that integrating the proposed Neutrosophic framework consistently enhanced the performance of all three architectures by improving feature representation through the explicit modeling of truth, indeterminacy, and falsity. This demonstrates its adaptability across convolution-based, state-space-based, and Transformer-based feature learning paradigms. The observed variation in performance gain reflects the distinct feature learning mechanisms of the backbone architectures, while the consistent improvements across all three models demonstrate the robustness of the proposed framework. Among the three models, N-SAEViT exhibited the largest improvement over its baseline, with the test accuracy increasing from 80.72% to 97.60%, representing a relative improvement of 20.91%. N-Mamba also demonstrated the generalizability of the proposed framework by improving the baseline Vision Mamba accuracy from 74.12% to 77.77%, corresponding to a relative improvement of 4.92%. N-ResNet18 likewise benefited from the proposed framework, although the improvement over its baseline was comparatively smaller.
In contrast, when considering the absolute evaluation metrics, N-ResNet18 achieved the highest test accuracy, Macro-F1 score, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), whereas N-SAEViT exhibited the largest relative improvement over its corresponding baseline. These findings demonstrate that the proposed Neutrosophic-embedded framework consistently improves the discriminative capability of both convolutional and transformer-based models, highlighting its robustness and adaptability across different deep learning architectures. The models have been validated with ground truth by ablation study and failure case analysis.
The rationale underlying the model performance gains is assessed on the basis of feature-level analysis. The Fisher Score of 
\(Edge_{\text {density}}\)
 is observed to show an increment of 99.3% that improves structural discrimination and boundary detection. 
\(LBP_{\text {energy}}\)
, which captures fine-grained texture patterns, has been improved by 99.45%. This further complements class separability. In addition, Neutrosophic embedding has strengthened the features of 
\(H_{\text {mean}}\)
 and 
colorfulness
. These features improve the ability of the model to distinguish visually identical benthic substrate classes. Based on the intensity correlation analysis, it is confirmed that the embeddings effectively leverage pixel brightness information that supports both edge and color based features.
N-ResNet18 also benefited from Neutrosophic embedding and exhibited improved stability of the features and reduced noise sensitivity. However, its performance gains were smaller compared to N-SAEViT. This emphasizes a significant fact that though convolutional architectures effectively capture local patterns, they are limited in modeling the global context essential for complex underwater substrate classification.
The enhanced models exhibit generalization capability across imbalanced dataset with improved classification accuracy and improved discrimination between visually similar classes. Alignment with the model prediction and correlation metrics along with SSIM and descriptive statistical evaluations ensured that these improvements were significant which 
p
-value is less than 0.001. Furthermore, Kernel density estimation and qualitative visualizations illustrated in a comprehensible manner visually proves that the T-I-F decomposition enhances class separability. Further, it stabilizes attention maps, and localizes uncertainty primarily along the object boundaries and ambiguous regions.
In conclusion, Neutrosophic-embedded framework for deep learning models with explicitly integrating uncertainty modeling results in robust multi-class benthic substrate classification.
Limitations and future work
Future work will explore the use of underwater image enhancement techniques to reduce turbidity effects. It improves feature extraction under visually degraded conditions. In addition, cross domain generalization will be investigated to further mitigate the performance drop of models trained in one region or limited imaging condition. Otherwise, when domain shifts
67
 is omitted in deep learning integrated computer vision, it results in higher misclassifications rate. Therefore, these approaches are expected to further improve the classification accuracy and robustness in challenging underwater imaging scenarios. In addition, we will investigate deeper integration of Neutrosophic representations within Vision Mamba architectures, including block-wise uncertainty estimation and adaptive sequence modulation, to further exploit the capabilities of state-space sequence models for benthic substrate classification. Future work will evaluate the proposed framework on additional underwater benchmark datasets to further investigate its cross-dataset generalization.

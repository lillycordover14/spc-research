Maddox Schultz’s earliest memories are in a playpen tucked in the corner of rink lobbies around Medicine Hat, Alberta.
His parents, David and Vicki, had no other choice. They were on the ice seven days a week teaching figure skating, trying to start a second career as elite-level coaches after retiring from seven years of touring professionally — and a lifetime of competing at nationals before that.

“It was a grind as a young family,” David said, but they would ask the parents of the figure skaters they were working with to watch Maddox and his little sister Sydney, popping off to spend 10 minutes with them during flood breaks.
Once they were old enough, they put the kids in every skating program they could just so that they could see them.
“Even if all they wanted to do was skate for fun and they didn’t pick up ice sports, for us that was fine. But when you’re born to two figure skating parents and coaches, you’re going to be on the ice with us,” David said, chuckling.
Maddox started in their PreCanSkate program at 2. He wasn’t a natural — more of a “marcher” shuffling around the ice.
“He wasn’t just zooming right away,” David said.
At 4, he joined his dad’s power skating program, Schultz Power Skating, swapping figure skates for hockey skates from session to session and learning to do jumps and spins while wearing his hockey tracksuit or Team Canada jersey.
“We just thought that he could be on our figure skating ice and we could teach him and that it was really good for his agility, control, balance, everything to just be in two different sets of skates and learn anything you can on the ice,” Vicki said.
He was never afraid of falling down and “just wanted to go fast.” At 5, the family moved to Regina, Saskatchewan, and by then he was “zipping” around the ice with parachutes strapped around his waist. “It came quick after he got comfortable,” David said.
At 8, he landed his first axel.

It was soon after that milestone, however, that Vicki and David started to realize that hockey — not figure skating — may be Maddox’s future.
At 9, he made the Saskatchewan Jr. Pats as an underager for the Brick, North America’s most historic minor hockey tournament.
At 10, his feet were growing so quickly that it became hard for his parents to justify constantly buying two different types of skates, and he gave up figure skating.
Now 16, he’s the early favorite of scouts to be the No. 1 pick in the 2028 NHL Draft.

Playing for his hometown Regina Pats, he has registered 16 points in 11 games this season, a 99-point pace. The only under-17 players to exceed 95 points in the WHL this century are Gavin McKenna (97 points) and Connor Bedard (100 points), both No. 1 picks in the NHL Draft.

The Pats took Schultz first in the 2025 WHL Bantam Draft. Pats staff say the 5-foot-10, 181-pound center has the skill and certainly the skating of all those who earn the “special player” tag, but what truly makes him one is a rare competitiveness and commitment — especially for his age — to the defensive side of the game.
The first time Pats head coach David Bell met him, he told his wife, “This guy’s built like Sidney Crosby; his body looks like Sid.”
“His lower body is extremely strong, and some of that is his genetics and his parents being figure skaters. He’s very powerful,” Bell said.
Even after he hung up his figure skates, David still coached him all the way up in minor hockey, always featuring some kind of skating-focused warmup and edge work in his practices.
Pats general manager Dale Derkatch also coached him growing up because Maddox, born in 2010, played up with Derkatch’s 2007-born son Dayce.
His skating was so advanced that coaches would wonder who Schultz was supposed to pass the puck to if he was the fastest guy on the ice every time.
“He was always the best,” Derkatch said. “Heads above most kids. He was never one to be a puck hog, though.”
Maddox still skates with his dad twice a week from May through August, and Bell also hired David to work with the Pats this season specifically because of Maddox’s skating.
“His edge work is elite, and his power is elite, but his hip mobility and his ankle flexion and ankle mobility that’s where you really see that you can tell that he’s had different work,” Bell said. “Inside edge, outside edge, opening up his hips, it’s all so good, and then he’s so balanced on his skates that he’s hard to knock off the puck. … It’s clear that he has been working with his dad.”

Maddox Schultz in his two pairs of skates. (Courtesy of the Schultz family)
Maddox never loved figure skating as he did hockey, but he enjoyed the private lessons that figure skating offered because he had a desire for one-on-one attention where someone could help him get better at something.

He still craves that one-on-one learning. Bell has coached pro and junior hockey for longer than Schultz has been alive, but after one recent Friday morning practice, the 16-year-old stumped him with a question.
He wanted to discuss the spin of the puck from the top guy to the flank guy on the power play and asked Bell which way he thought the spin would be better for a shot.
“OK, this is an Erik Karlsson, Alex Ovechkin question,” Bell thought to himself.
“He has asked me questions that are so detailed and so finite that I wouldn’t expect a junior hockey player, let alone a 16-year-old, to ask me that question, and I really have to turn my brain on to answer the question for him. It’s challenging to have answers for him,” he said.
Bell is used to pushing his players. Schultz has pushed him.
During a game earlier this season against the Saskatoon Blades, the Pats were down a goal late, and Bell drew up a faceoff play on Schultz’s weak side during a timeout, assigning the draw to a right-shot center.
Schultz, a lefty, looked at him and said, “No, I’ll take that draw, and I’ll win it.”
“OK, sure, kid,” Bell replied.
Then Schultz won it … and three more with the goalie pulled.
“He just has that competitive edge where he just wants to beat everybody, and he does it because of his mindset,” Bell said.
Schultz describes himself as a skilled worker who drags his teammates into the fight.

Schultz grew up idolizing Pats such as Connor Bedard, Sam Steel, Austin Wagner and Connor Hobbs. “They made a huge impact on many Regina kids, and I was lucky enough to be one of them, and now I’m here,” he said. (Courtesy of the Regina Pats)
David called seeing the Pats logo on the placard on lottery night one of the best moments the Schultz family has had together because it meant Maddox could live at home a couple more years.
“That was a big deal for us. You’re still teaching them so much. There’s so much growing up to do. To continue to be able to reinforce family values, it’s really important to us,” David said. “And it’s important to him.”

Vicki jokes that he wouldn’t have been able to leave the family’s dog, a 10-year-old Shiba Inu aptly named Sullivan McDavid, too.
Vicki is now Skate Regina’s skating director. Together, she and David have coached multiple national medalists. Sydney just turned 15 and has been Saskatchewan’s provincial figure skating champion three years running, attending high school in the mornings before leaving at noon to train with her parents in the afternoons.
Neither parent can seem to get Maddox to take a break from his training. David and Vicki have to remind him to rest, but he’ll only sit for so long before he says he has to go shoot in the driveway or at Regina’s TS Hockey Shooting with local specialist Trevor Lakness, who has also worked with stars such as Landon DuPont and Cale Makar.
His family can’t catch him on their Monday game nights, either.
Recently, he and Vicki were partners in a game of Cranium, and he thought his mom didn’t act out a prompt right.
“He got a little upset, and we lost,” Vicki said, laughing.
“You’re being nice,” David interjected. “He 
lost 
it. Nothing we compete with ends well.”
“Unless he wins,” Vicki said.

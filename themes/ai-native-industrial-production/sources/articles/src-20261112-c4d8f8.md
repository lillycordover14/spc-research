The Post-UNIFIL Illusion: Why a US-French Formula in South Lebanon Cannot Replace Israeli Deterrence

Opinion

A UN peacekeepers (UNIFIL) vehicle is seen next to piled up debris at Beirut’s port, Lebanon, Oct. 23, 2020. Photo: REUTERS/Mohamed Azakir

The end of UNIFIL’s mandate for Lebanon is approaching, but the debate over what replaces it is only beginning.

In August 2025, the UN Security Council extended the mission for a final time, through Dec. 31, 2026, and ordered its withdrawal over the following year. This exposes a problem UNIFIL never resolved: how to prevent Hezbollah from using territory near Israel’s border as a military platform.

Washington and Paris are discussing what comes next, but no successor plan has been agreed. France and the United States are discussing a post-UNIFIL formula, while Europeans have considered a multinational presence.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio has separately suggested a civilian mission to verify implementation of the June 2026 framework in designated pilot zones. Beirut has rejected that approach and called for an international presence. The proposals differ, but raise the same question: Who will ensure that commitments are carried out?

Resolution 1701, adopted in 2006, is the obvious starting point. It called for the area between the Blue Line and the Litani River to be free of armed personnel, weapons, and military assets other than those of the Lebanese state and UNIFIL. It also called for disarmament of all armed groups in Lebanon. UNIFIL was tasked with assisting the Lebanese Armed Forces, not independently disarming Hezbollah. In practice, Hezbollah restricted the mission’s access and freedom of movement while building military infrastructure in the south. UNIFIL could report violations. It could not dismantle the network or prevent its reconstruction.

That record should caution against confusing international presence with enforcement. A civilian body might verify Israeli withdrawal and Lebanese deployment. But neither can compel Hezbollah to surrender its weapons or stop rebuilding positions. Monitoring can identify noncompliance. It cannot replace the political will and power needed to address it.

Lebanon’s domestic politics make that distinction important. The recent controversy over Army Commander General Rodolphe Haykal showed how quickly the military can become the subject of a national dispute. Haykal’s remarks about the army’s national mission drew criticism from Lebanese Forces leader Samir Geagea, a prominent opponent of Hezbollah’s independent arsenal.

President Joseph Aoun defended the army leadership on Nov. 10, and Defense Minister Michel Menassa also rejected attacks on the institution. The episode does not show that Hezbollah orchestrated the criticism. It shows that even the army’s role is contested in a country where confronting an armed faction carries serious political risks.

The issue is not the army’s manpower. It is whether the state can give soldiers clear authority, political backing, and resources to act when they encounter Hezbollah’s military structure. A monitoring arrangement cannot answer that question for Beirut. Nor should foreign governments assume that a Lebanese deployment automatically means the state has established control.

The June 26, 2026, framework between Israel, Lebanon and the United States already recognizes the need for a sequenced process. It links the restoration of Lebanese state authority and verified disarmament of non-state armed groups to Israel’s progressive redeployment.

Initial operations began in two pilot zones in July. Yet by late October, implementation had not expanded beyond those areas, and an eighth round of talks ended without a breakthrough. US and Israeli officials reportedly judged the Lebanese government’s efforts as insufficient. This stagnation is a warning against announcing another mechanism before existing commitments have been met.

Israel has paid a heavy price to weaken Hezbollah and reduce the immediate threat to northern communities. The threat has not disappeared.

Hezbollah retains military capabilities, while Israeli forces continue operating in southern Lebanon and hold positions in a buffer zone covering about five percent of Lebanese territory. A rushed withdrawal tied to an untested monitoring system could allow Hezbollah to rebuild near the border. Yet a prolonged Israeli presence carries costs and can strengthen Hezbollah’s claim that it is defending Lebanon. Any arrangement must therefore deliver verifiable security results, not merely diplomatic language.

France and the United States should support the Lebanese Army, strengthen state institutions, and create a credible system for reporting violations. But monitoring must not be presented as enforcement. The mission’s access, reporting powers and response to violations must be clearly defined. Most importantly, Israel must retain its inherent right of self-defense, including the ability to respond to planned, imminent or ongoing attacks. No international body should give Beirut or an outside guarantor an effective veto over Israel’s protection of its citizens.

The end of UNIFIL is not a security strategy. A new flag, a smaller force or a civilian monitoring team will not change realities on the ground unless Lebanon can enforce the state’s monopoly on arms. Washington and Paris should judge any successor arrangement by what it can prevent, not by how reassuring its diplomatic language sounds. Israel’s deterrence cannot be outsourced to a mission that can observe Hezbollah’s return but won’t stop it or protect Israel’s northern border communities.

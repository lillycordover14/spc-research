When Figure AI Could Go Public
Figure CEO Brett Adcock has not announced an IPO timeline. The company continues to raise private capital and build its robot, AI and manufacturing operations. Its September 2025 Series C brought in more than $1 billion in committed capital, with participation from investors including 
NVIDIA
, Intel Capital and Qualcomm Ventures. An earlier round included Microsoft, NVIDIA and Jeff Bezos’s investment firm.
Going public would require Figure to provide detailed financial and business information through SEC filings, including information about revenue, risks and operations. That disclosure could give investors a clearer picture of commercial deployments and manufacturing progress, which the company does not report publicly in the same way a listed company does. A public filing would mark a major step toward an 
IPO
, but it would not guarantee that Figure will complete its offering.

How Figure AI Compares With Other Humanoid Robot Companies
Figure AI remains private, and its latest announced valuation is $39 billion from its September 2025 Series C. 
Tesla
, a publicly traded company, has its Optimus humanoid robot as one part of a much larger company, so buying Tesla shares gives investors exposure to its entire business rather than to Optimus alone.
Unitree Robotics, a Chinese maker of humanoid and quadruped robots, listed on 
Shanghai’s STAR Market
 in August 2026. Reuters reported that its IPO price valued the company at about $9 billion, though its market value can change as the shares trade.

How to Invest in Figure AI Before an IPO
Figure shares are not available through ordinary stock-market orders in a standard brokerage account. Private secondary marketplaces may show interest in a company, but availability can depend on a willing 
shareholder
, company transfer restrictions and required approvals. Figure has publicly cautioned about unauthorized offers, so investors should independently verify any proposed transaction with the company.
Some investors access private shares through a special purpose vehicle, or SPV, which pools capital to buy an interest in shares. These offerings often limit participation to accredited investors and may charge placement, management or performance fees. Minimum investments, fees and liquidity vary by offering; investors should review the actual documents rather than assume standard terms. 
Public-market alternatives include shares of companies that have invested in Figure or robotics-focused funds. For example, the Roundhill Humanoid Robotics ETF trades publicly and lists a 0.75% 
gross expense ratio
, but fund holdings change and it does not provide direct Figure ownership.
Risks of Investing in Figure AI
Humanoid robots are still in early commercial deployment, and Figure does not publicly disclose the revenue and profitability details investors would expect from a public company. Its $39 billion valuation came from a private funding round, so it may differ from what 
public-market investors
 would pay. Private shares can also be difficult to value or sell, and transfers may require company approval. Figure has warned about unauthorized attempts to sell its stock, so investors should verify any offer directly with the company.
Figure faces competition from well-funded companies such as Tesla and Unitree, while expanding robot manufacturing requires substantial investment. Customer demand, product reliability, competition and production costs could all affect its path to profitability. 
There is also no guarantee Figure will go public; a listing could take years or never happen. Before considering an investment, ask what verified financial information is available, how the 
share price
 was set, whether the shares can be transferred and whether you can afford to keep the money invested without a clear exit date.
Bottom Line
Figure AI remains private, with no announced IPO date, and its $39 billion valuation reflects its September 2025 funding round rather than a public-market price. Private share access can be limited, costly and illiquid; public companies and robotics funds offer indirect exposure. A 
financial advisor
 can help weigh these options against your goals, liquidity needs and risk tolerance.

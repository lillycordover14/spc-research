    Initiatives work to revitalize Yesa:sahį́, the Occaneechi language

    Corey Roberts, an Elon University professor, is developing a math and language development educational app for students in the Occaneechi Tribe.

Catherine Miller
 and 
Aminah Imran
                 
             •  
            
November 10, 2026
 

 

Dr. Corey Roberts, Assistant Professor of Native American and Indigenous Studies at Elon University, specializes in linguistics for the Yesa:sahį́ language. Photo courtesy of Elon University Media Relations.
 

Corey Roberts, Elon University professor and relative of the Occaneechi Band of the Saponi Nation, is working with a team of colleagues to create a mobile education app for enrolled tribal members. The app is designed to help students in grades 3 through 8 strengthen math skills while also supporting Occaneechi language learning.

The project has been in development for about five years and has been translated into several languages, including Lakota.

Roberts said the language of the Occaneechi Band of the Saponi Nation, called Yesa:sahį́, is hard to translate for functionality, and many records of the language have been destroyed.

“It’s a very slow and sure process, but we created graphics, we’ve updated the alphabet, we’ve created pedagogical materials to some degree, and these things will always continue to be developed because we’re fleshing out the language more and more, just to be able to even have a regular conversation,” Roberts said.

Yesa:sahį́ is “sleeping,” meaning it has no living fluent speakers left and continues to hold immense cultural significance. Roberts said that Yesa:sahį́ translates to the speech of the southeastern Sioux people.

Roberts works alongside Martin Saniga, a member of the Sappony Tribe from Person County, N.C. and Halifax County, Va., on a nonprofit called the Yesa:sahį́ Language Project, where they seek to wake the language up through reconstruction.

This initiative is made up of representatives from several of the tribes that traditionally spoke the language. Saniga said these tribes include the Occaneechi Band of the Saponi Nation, the Haliwa-Saponi Tribe, the Sappony Tribe, the Monacan Indian Nation and the Saponi Nation of Ohio.

Saniga said that the organization has spent the past few years laying the groundwork for bringing Yesa:sahį́ back into use. This includes standardizing the orthography — the alphabet — which has allowed them to get started on word recreation.

Saniga said that word reconstruction typically means looking at their sister languages to see how they construct those words.

“So a great example is we know we had a word for turtle. Turtles are all over the place. One of our traditional clans is the Turtle Clan,” Saniga said.

He explained that by looking at the words for turtle in languages similar to Yesa:sahį́, such as Ofo and Biloxi, they were able to reconstruct their word for turtle.

Roberts said the process of revitalizing Native American languages and learning his ancestral language has been surprisingly political. When he first got involved with the language and math education app project he assumed that things would remain apolitical. He soon realized that language is foundational to culture, and for some people, living in and practicing culture is a political act.

“Doing something that very much supports the culture of a people — and language is very much in support of people’s culture — is in and of itself a political thing,” Roberts said.

Roberts said that the language revitalizations himself and his team are taking a part in are not the end of the reconstruction process. If anything, it’s just the start. Intertribal language nonprofits are working on recovering many sleeping 
Native American
 languages.

The first documented historical reference of the Occaneechi Tribe is from 1650, when a village near the Roanoke River in Virginia was discovered. The tribe was later forced south in 1676 after an attack by Nathaniel Bacon, settling along the Eno River in present-day Hillsborough.

The Occaneechi Tribe returned to Virginia in 1713, joined by some of their cousin tribes, including the Saponi, Tutelo, Stuckeno and Meiponsky Tribes. The Saponi Tribe was the largest group in the fort, so the governor designated those tribes as part of the Saponi Nation.

The Occaneechi Tribe later amended its name to the Occaneechi Band of the Saponi Nation to better reflect their history. The Nation shares geography with the Piedmont area of North Carolina.

Saniga said that the Yesa:sahį́ Language Project hosts an online class in which they teach basic pleasantries, such as how to introduce oneself.

Vivette Jeffries-Logan is a student in one of these online classes. She described these classes as a healing process for the nations that are “re-membering” the language, a term she uses because people don’t learn what they already know — they remember.

Jeffries-Logan said she thinks of colonization as knocking down a building; the rubble is still there covering the culture.

“For me the way that I’m approaching this [is that] this is healing for us, right?” she said. “And so [the language] was under the rubble and waiting for us to start healing, and so [the language], the medicine, starts rising.”

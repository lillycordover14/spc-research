Three years sober, the Penguins’ Sam Girard has ‘become me again’
After asking for help and entering the NHL/NHLPA player assistance program in 2023, Sam Girard has leaned on his community to move toward a happier and healthier life.

CRANBERRY TOWNSHIP, Pa. — In hockey, the spin-o-rama is a nifty 360-degree rotation to evade defenders while keeping control of the puck. It is equal parts creative and effective.
It’s also Sam Girard’s default move. He does it often, typically with great success, and is so known for it that some junior hockey teammates called him “
The Tornado
.” When the Pittsburgh Penguins defenseman isn’t sure what to do, the spin-o-rama is always there, a tool for self-preservation and surprise.

“I used to see him play in juniors. He did it 20 times in one game,” said Hendrix Lapierre, Girard’s fellow Quebec native and Penguins teammate.
Away from the ice, Girard’s default move for self-preservation was, for too long, turning to alcohol. Depression, anxiety and the high-pressure life of playing in the NHL combined to create a heavy burden.
Three years after seeking help, Girard doesn’t claim to be out of the woods yet, but he is smiling again. He says he’s enjoying life again and no longer spinning out of control.
“I feel good,” he recently told 
The Athletic
. “Actually, it’s not even good. I feel 
unbelievable
.”

Sam Girard, who missed most of the playoffs after breaking his sternum in the second round, lifts the Stanley Cup in 2022. (Bruce Bennett / Getty Images)
Girard seemed to have found his groove.
In 2022, as a mobile second-pairing defenseman, Girard helped the Colorado Avalanche sweep the team that drafted him, the Nashville Predators, in the first round of the playoffs. Then he scored a big goal in the opening game of Colorado’s second-round win over the St. Louis Blues before breaking his sternum on a hit by the Blues’ Ivan Barbashev in Game 3. Still, his team won the championship, and his name was etched on the Stanley Cup. He had made it.
But the fame that followed brought even more pressure to perform, on and off the ice. He became consumed by anxiety and depression, feeling alone and isolated.
To handle that pressure, Girard would have one drink too many at a party. He’d have one too many drinks on the road. He was becoming a different person — one he didn’t like. The isolation was getting the best of him. He thought drinking would help.
“It doesn’t,” he said. “You learn that at some point.”
Drinking became a form of self-medication. Eventually, it became too much. He found help and became better equipped to manage it.
“Being surrounded by people who care can change your life,” he said. “That is what I learned.”

When the Penguins host the Calgary Flames the night before Thanksgiving, it will be exactly three years since Girard entered the NHL/NHLPA 
player assistance program
.
In some ways, they’ve been the most difficult days of his life.
Girard’s biggest challenge in entering the program in 2023 was feeling singled out. The stigma haunted him for quite a while.
“Looking back, I’m the one who made the decision to do it,” he said. “I talked with my wife first. I wasn’t really hiding the drinking from her. She knew. … It was hard, making that decision. You don’t want to feel like you’re weak. You don’t want to be judged. But it was one of the best decisions I’ve made in my life. I had to take care of my body and my mind.”
The road hasn’t been perfectly smooth for Girard since he entered the program — “I’m not going to lie. It’s always there. That anxiety doesn’t go away,” he said — but at some point between then and now, the 28-year-old defenseman committed to himself — and his wife and daughter.

He says he’s never been happier.
“I feel like I’m a much better person than I was before,” Girard said. “I feel like I’ve helped myself become me again. I’ve been sober for a while. I feel like I like my life again.”
What has helped him the most?
“My support system,” he said. “My wife and my family. The people in my AA (Alcoholics Anonymous) meetings. I know a guy from those meetings who texts me every morning, just to check in on me. Stuff like that means so much, to not feel alone.”
When Girard reached the one-year mark sober, he said, he finally didn’t feel like staying sober was a battle.
“People say you’ve got to get to that one-year mark, that it can become easier at that point, that something starts to change,” Girard said. “And it’s true.”
Girard and his wife, Jacklyn, celebrated the birth of their first child 18 months ago. Having a daughter gave Girard extra resolve in his journey.

“I want to be there for her every day, and I want to be there for my wife, too,” Girard said. “At some point, I realized that I want to be there for myself, too. I had to do it for myself in some ways. If you don’t want to help yourself, it won’t work.”
The more Girard looked around, the more he realized he wasn’t alone at all.
His friends in Colorado were there the whole time. And although Girard was shipped from the Avalanche to the Penguins at last season’s trade deadline, they’re still his friends.
“He’s such a good guy,” Avalanche star Nathan MacKinnon said. “It sucks losing good people (to a trade) like that. He’s a great teammate.”
Girard was a beloved presence in that locker room. While in Denver, Girard met his wife, became a champion, struggled with depression and addiction, and ultimately asked for help.
His teammates were with him every step of the way.
“I think what I admire most about Sam is how honest he was during that whole time,” Avalanche forward Artturi Lehkonen said. “He had these things going on and he talked openly about it. I’m just happy he’s doing well. It was a very sad day for all of us to see him go. We just want things to keep working out well for him.”

Sam Girard has found a new supportive community with his Penguins teammates. (Emilee Chinn / Getty Images)
As he moved from Denver to Pittsburgh, Girard’s support system has evolved. MacKinnon is no longer on his team, but one of MacKinnon’s closest friends, Sidney Crosby, had met Girard years earlier. Crosby doesn’t initiate conversation with Girard about his history with addiction, but the Penguins captain has made it clear he’s always there for his teammate.
“It’s something guys in certain situations might want to share or talk about,” Crosby said. “In one way or the other, you want to be there for people. I knew him a little bit (before he was traded to Pittsburgh), just in passing. Great demeanor, great attitude.”
It’s not just Crosby.

“What a great guy,” Lapierre said. “It’s been special getting to know this guy. He’s great. A good friend.”
The trade to Pittsburgh last season came with risks. Sometimes, people experiencing alcoholism, anxiety and depression can struggle with the challenge of new environments, new communities, new cities. Pittsburgh itself, though, has played a role in Girard’s support system, even if most of its citizens are unaware.
“I like this place so much,” Girard said. “The guys on the team are super nice. But also, everyone in this city is just so friendly with me. Sometimes I like to go for walks. And every time, people just walk right up to you and say hello. And they’re so friendly about it, so warm. It makes me feel better, little stuff like that.
“I think being here in Pittsburgh is a really good thing for me, for my wife, for my family. We are happy here, enjoying our lives here.”
Regardless of where he works, Girard’s life is different now.
He has to check on himself daily. He gets those “good morning” texts, helping push him to a better life. And there are those AA meetings. They’re hard to keep up with, due to Girard’s atypical schedule. He’s often busy in the evening. He’s on airplanes all the time. He’s constantly bouncing between different cities and time zones.
“But I always make time for meetings,” he said. “Sometimes it’s here (in Pittsburgh), sometimes I’ll go to meetings on the road. Those meetings have meant a lot. I keep in touch with my AA friends back in Colorado. I met a lot of people there, good people.
“If you have that support like I have, it’s so huge. It changes you. I feel closer to God now. I feel like I’m closer to the person I want to be. Just talking to people every day is so important.”
It’s also important to follow your heart. Girard and his wife recently made a decision that might bring some peace. They had purchased a home in Syracuse, N.Y., a nice, central location between Pittsburgh and Girard’s native Quebec. But they weren’t happy in Syracuse.
“I don’t know,” Girard said with a laugh. “We didn’t really like it, I guess.”

So, while Girard is happy to play with the Penguins and be in Pittsburgh, he and his wife decided to move back to Denver last summer. It’s where his wife is from, and in some ways, it’s where he’s from, too.
“I met my wife there. I won a championship there. I also got myself into trouble there, yes. But it’s where I got sober, too. And it’s where my daughter was born. There was a lot of good there. We are happy there,” Girard said.
One by one, Girard is making decisions that bring him and his family happiness. Along the way, he’s played outstanding hockey in his 10th NHL season and has been one of the Penguins’ best players. His smile has been a daily presence in the locker room.
“I’m lucky,” Girard said. “I play in a great city like Pittsburgh. I have made friends here. I have my Denver friends. I have support in so many different places, wherever I go. People have been so good to me, have helped me so much.
“I’d say I’m in a very good headspace. I’m very happy.”

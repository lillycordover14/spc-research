Ursa Major Secures Additional $10.9 Million in U.S. Air Force Funding for Its Draper Storable Liquid Rocket Engine
From the United States to Europe, China, Russia and Brazil: Worldwide development of storable liquid propulsion technologies as alternatives to conventional and highly toxic propellants.
AeroMorning –John Smith – November 13, 2026
On November 12, 2026, the U.S. Air Force Research Laboratory (AFRL) exercised a $10.9 million contract option for the continued development of Ursa Major’s Draper storable liquid rocket engine, supporting hypersonic applications alongside successful flights of the Affordable Rapid Missile Demonstrator.
Ursa Major develops two complementary rocket propulsion technologies:
Draper (Storable Liquid Bipropellant):
 Introduced in May 2023, it delivers approximately 18 kN of thrust using hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and kerosene. It operates without cryogenic refrigeration and provides liquid thrust control, multiple restarts, and rapid operational availability. Beyond high-thrust applications, its architecture can be scaled down or adapted for other aerospace uses requiring lower thrust.
Hadley (Semi-cryogenic):
 Uses liquid oxygen (LOX, stored cryogenically) and kerosene (stored at ambient temperature). It is flight-qualified and has been used in hypersonic test applications.
Strategic Positioning:
 Hadley targets the established semi-cryogenic market, while Draper targets the high-thrust-capable storable liquid propulsion market.
Rocket Propulsion Categories: Performance and Characteristics
Rocket performance is evaluated primarily through thrust (force in Newtons) and specific impulse (efficiency), rather than power alone.
Cryogenic & Semi-Cryogenic:
 Extremely low storage temperatures. Yields very high specific impulse and thrust (kN to MN) for heavy launch vehicles and orbital insertion. Limitations: cryogenic infrastructure, boil-off, and demanding ground operations.
Solid Propellants:
 Integrated fuel-oxidizer grain. Delivers very high thrust-to-weight ratios and rapid readiness with simple logistics. Limitations: once ignited, generally cannot be throttled or restarted.
Storable Liquid Propellants:
 Do not require cryogenic refrigeration. Divided into three sub-categories:
Hypergolic Bipropellants:
 Spontaneous ignition upon contact between propellants; long operational heritage, high reliability and the ability to deliver a wide range of thrust levels, from a few newtons to several hundred kilonewtons, but extremely toxic.
Storable Monopropellants:
 Single liquid decomposed for low-to-medium thrust (mN to hundreds of N range); this includes hydrazine (highly toxic), as well as newer, lower-toxicity formulations based on ADN (Ammonium Dinitramide), HAN (Hydroxylammonium Nitrate) and hydrogen peroxide. Standard for satellite attitude control.
Alternative Storable Bipropellants:
 Combine non-cryogenic storage with medium-to-high thrust potential, liquid thrust control, and multiple restarts, suitable for upper stages and space transport. Some formulations offer lower toxicity than conventional hypergolic propellants. Challenges: material compatibility, stability, and qualification.
2. Applications and Propulsion Requirements
Launch Vehicles & Space Rockets:
 First stages require very high thrust, typically ranging from hundreds of kilonewtons to several meganewtons, with cryogenic and solid propulsion systems dominating. Upper stages and kick stages require a high specific impulse (Isp, expressed in seconds) to maximise propellant efficiency and payload capacity, together with precise orbital insertion, restart capability and, in some applications, long-term propellant storage. Storable propulsion offers operational advantages where long-term storage and rapid readiness are essential, although cryogenic propulsion generally provides higher specific impulse.
Missiles & Hypersonic Systems:
 Require long-term propellant storage, instant operational readiness, compactness and independence from cryogenic infrastructure. Propulsion requirements vary significantly depending on vehicle size and mission profile, typically ranging from tens of kilonewtons to several hundred kilonewtons of thrust. High specific impulse is also important to maximise propellant efficiency, range and payload capacity.
Satellites & Spacecraft:
 The largest established commercial market for storable propulsion. Thrust requirements range from fine attitude control (a few mN to a few N) to orbital correction and station-keeping (approximately 0.1–100 N), with larger spacecraft requiring higher thrust for major orbital manoeuvres. Specific impulse (expressed in seconds) is a key performance parameter. The market is increasingly shifting from highly toxic hydrazine towards lower-toxicity alternatives.
Orbital Transfer Vehicles & Space Tugs:
 Require long-duration propellant storage, multiple operating cycles, restart capability and sufficient transfer thrust, typically ranging from hundreds of newtons to several kilonewtons, depending on vehicle mass and mission profile. A high specific impulse is desirable to maximise propellant efficiency, increase payload capacity and extend operational range. Storable bipropellant systems offer an attractive compromise between storage capability, operational flexibility and propulsion performance compared with cryogenic chemical propulsion and electric propulsion systems, such as ion and Hall-effect thrusters.
Lunar Landers & Specialised Spacecraft:
 Require highly controllable propulsion, throttleability, multiple restart capability and long-term propellant storage without cryogenic boil-off management. Thrust requirements typically range from several kilonewtons to hundreds of kilonewtons, depending on lander mass and mission profile. A high specific impulse (Isp) is desirable to maximise propellant efficiency, reduce onboard propellant mass and increase payload capacity. Storable chemical bipropellants offer operational flexibility, while cryogenic propulsion can provide higher specific impulse at the cost of more demanding storage requirements.
3. Why Ursa Major’s Draper is Strategic
Draper addresses a crucial gap between low-thrust green satellite thrusters and traditional, highly toxic high-performance engines:
Performance Profile:
 Approximately 18 kN of thrust, hydrogen peroxide/kerosene bipropellant technology, non-cryogenic storable propellants, liquid-engine throttleability and multiple restart capability. Specific impulse (Isp): not publicly disclosed by Ursa Major.
Toxicity Reduction:
 Replaces conventional toxic hypergolics with less hazardous oxidizers like hydrogen peroxide. Note: lower toxicity does not mean harmless; hydrogen peroxide remains a hazardous oxidizer requiring specialised safety protocols.
Versatility:
 Combines solid-motor operational readiness with liquid-engine controllability, scaling efficiently to support everything from heavy defence applications down to alternative space roles requiring lower thrust.

5. Differences in Versatility and Target Applications for Lower-Toxicity Storable Liquid Propulsion
Draper stands out through its broader announced application portfolio, targeting hypersonic defence, responsive launch, in-space propulsion, orbital manoeuvring and space-based interception. Its approximately 18 kN thrust class, combined with throttleability, restart capability and non-cryogenic storage, offers significant operational flexibility across several specialised aerospace markets.
Compared with other emerging hydrogen peroxide-based storable bipropellant engines, such as ArianeGroup’s GRETA and BIZU Space’s ARION, which are primarily focused on space transportation, lunar landers and launch vehicle applications, Draper has the broadest publicly announced range of target applications.
This positioning makes Draper particularly interesting as a potential bridge between conventional solid propulsion systems and liquid propulsion technologies requiring greater operational flexibility or replacing highly toxic propellants.
6. Market Outlook & Conclusion
Civil & Commercial Space:
 Expanding satellite constellations are accelerating the replacement of highly toxic hydrazine with lower-toxicity monopropellants and alternative propulsion technologies.
High-Thrust Defense & Transport:
 Emerging alternative storable bipropellants, such as Ursa Major’s Draper, are targeting hypersonics, specialised defence applications, upper stages and orbital transportation systems, particularly where solid motors lack throttleability and restart capabilities, or where cryogenic storage infrastructure is impractical.
Competitive Diversification:
 The global market is evolving around three complementary technological segments: green monopropellant specialists, alternative storable bipropellant developers, and established manufacturers continuing to develop conventional hypergolic and cryogenic propulsion systems.
Conclusion:
 The industry is moving beyond the traditional choice between highly toxic hypergolic propellants and cryogenic propulsion systems. Lower-toxicity storable liquid propulsion offers an alternative combining long-term storage, operational readiness and liquid-engine controllability.
Within this emerging market, high-thrust alternative storable bipropellants represent a particularly promising development. Ursa Major’s Draper is an important example, potentially bridging the gap between conventional solid rocket motors and more operationally flexible liquid propulsion systems for defence and specialised aerospace applications.

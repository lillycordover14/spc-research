We Asked Nurse Practitioners: Would You Choose This Career Again?&nbsp;&nbsp;Medscape

[page text]
Naomi Fener, AG-ACNP,BSN, MPH, loved working as an ICU nurse, caring for up to two critically ill patients at a time and spending hours at the bedside. Becoming an ICU nurse practitioner (NP) seemed like a natural next step: The role would provide more responsibility, more clinical decision-making, and the opportunity to diagnose patients and develop treatment plans.
After graduating in 2017, Fener accepted a position as an NP in the cardiac ICU at a large academic medical center in Boston.
“I did love it in that like we got to see the sickest of the sick patients [and] we got to do some really cool procedures…and see a lot of things that people only read about in textbooks,” she recalls. “At the same time, you go from caring for one or two patients as an ICU bedside nurse to…depending on the shift, anywhere from eight to 16 patients.”
Fener missed the close connections and personalized care she provided as a bedside nurse and began to question her decision to become an NP.

 physicians and other medical professionals about key practice challenges and current issues, creating high-impact analyses. For example, the Medscape 
Nurse Practitioner Happiness Index Report 2026
 found that:
8 in 10 NPs surveyed said they were happy with their career, but responses were nuanced by specialty.
One quarter of NPs surveyed felt their career diminished the quality of their life.
28% felt negatively about their work-life balance.
However, a career as an NP can be deeply rewarding. About 62% of NPs are very satisfied with their mental health and optimistic about their well-being. A large majority also feel fulfilled.
But burnout, emotional exhaustion, limited resources for patient care, and poor practice environments are also common. Nurses overall continually tell us that they plan to leave the bedside, reduce their hours, or start their own entrepreneurial business.
“Increasing expectations and shrinking support have made full-time clinical work less sustainable,” Fener said. “The expectations have gotten a lot higher without the support to go with them.”
Charting the Challenges
While the NP role can offer more autonomy and responsibility, administrative support, resources, and scope-of-practice restrictions often create challenges. 
Currently, only 27 states and the District of Columbia allow NPs to practice at the top of their license, and despite increasing reliance on the NP workforce to provide primary care, challenging work relationships and a lack of support for NP practice are common.
Compensation is also an oft-reported challenge. Medscape data continually reports that NPs do not feel fairly paid for their efforts and responsibilities.
Fener describes feeling like a “perpetual resident” as an NP because she spent less time at the bedside and more time doing rounds, making plans, and putting in orders than engaging in hands-on patient care. After a 6-year career as an NP, Fener swapped a clinical role to pursue a career in population health.
“Becoming an NP, while I didn’t love it…did give me a really nice perspective on the healthcare system,” she said. “Would I do it again? Probably not.”
Would You Pick This Career Again?
For Ashley Barnard, a cardiology NP in Australia, the transition from cardiac care and 
heart failure
 nurse to cardiac NP was challenging. He spent 2 years completing his NP training while continuing to work at the bedside, completed hundreds of hours of supervised clinical experience, and encountered months of organizational hurdles before he could practice as an NP. To complicate matters, administrators in the public hospital where he worked lacked an understanding of the NP role.
“When I started thinking about nurse practitioner roles in 2018, there was very little information…and a lack of information about what nurse practitioners actually can do,” Barnard said. “There was like no enthusiasm to say, ‘I’m going to be ahead of some of these issues.’ It was always reactive rather than proactive.”
The frustration led Barnard to transition from a public hospital to a private practice setting that provided a better framework and more support and respect for NPs. The decision to switch jobs is common, with 
1 in 5
 NPs accepting new offers annually, citing factors ranging from better pay and benefits to burnout, stressful work environments, and inadequate staffing.
Career Rewards
Despite the challenges, 33% of NPs felt their quality of life was better or much better than peers in other careers. Hanna Weitzman-Flanigan, DNP, MSN, is among those who report high job satisfaction. 
Since becoming an NP in 2004, Weitzman-Flanigan has transitioned from clinical practice to an administrative role at a large teaching hospital in New York City. The position allows her to combine her interest in the operating room with leadership and quality improvement.
“I think the beauty of the profession is that you can really tailor it to what you’re interested in,” she said. “Both professionally and personally [being an NP] has just fulfilled me.”
In addition to the broad scope of practice options, Weitzman-Flanigan was drawn to advanced practice nursing for the ability to make an immediate difference with individual patients while working on larger changes that can improve care for many patients at once.
Jennifer Kirk, MSN, APN, an NP, and clinical director at an urgent care clinic in Illinois, also has no regrets about her career choice.
During her two decades as an emergency room nurse, Kirk developed clinical care experience, critical thinking skills, and a passion for caring for patients that led her to become an NP in 2018. She loves the pace of an acute care setting, treating emergent issues, building relationships with patients, and seeing positive outcomes.
“The job itself is so fulfilling. There’s so much more to being a nurse practitioner than I think people realize,” Kirk said. “I would definitely choose this career again.” 
Picking the Right Path
Fener would like to see less pressure placed on registered nurses to pursue advanced degrees and more support to remain at the bedside.
“I think that we need to keep more amazing bedside nurses, and hospitals need to start respecting that and paying them better and incentivizing really smart nurses to stay at the bedside,” she explains. “We’re doing a disservice to nurses by saying, ‘If you’re really smart or driven, you should go on and get your master’s.’ I think there are so many other ways that you can be effective.”
For nurses who want to pursue advanced degrees, mentorship and networking are critical before committing to a graduate program, Weitzman-Flanigan said. She encourages prospective NPs to investigate programs, talk to people working in different settings, and consider the diverse roles available to advanced practice nurses from clinical and administrative roles to leadership positions, education, and policy.
“It’s an extremely stressful role,” she said. “You’re dealing with people’s lives…and you might be thrown 20 patients in a day. When you’re in that scenario, and you have a team that’s supportive, and you enjoy the interaction with your patients…and you see that impact that you have, I think that makes it worth it in the end.”

EU-Backed HARMONI Project Tests Ammonia-Hydrogen Fuel for Cement

Cement works
 as the binding agent in homes, roads and bridges, yet its manufacture is responsible for about 7% of worldwide carbon dioxide emissions, according to Hydrogen Central. Part of the reason is that the process demands very high temperatures, which have historically been generated by burning fossil fuels. A project named HARMONI, backed by the European Union with nearly EUR8 million distributed among 12 partners throughout Europe, is investigating whether cement plants can substitute fossil fuels with a mixture of ammonia and hydrogen while simultaneously assisting in balancing the electricity grid.
Researchers at Imperial College London in the Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, a core project partner, will evaluate whether the approach yields real environmental, economic and social advantages. In sectors including cement, steel, glass and ceramics, high-temperature heat accounts for between 70% and 92% of energy consumption.
The Case for Ammonia and Hydrogen
Green ammonia carries no carbon, so its combustion produces no carbon dioxide. The majority of ammonia manufactured today relies on fossil fuels, but green ammonia is now made from renewable sources such as wind and solar power. Ammonia is furthermore convenient to store and transport, and a worldwide shipping network is already in place. The drawback is that it burns with difficulty: ignition is poor, flames propagate slowly, and it can emit nitrogen oxides, which are harmful air pollutants.

HARMONI's solution involves recovering ammonia from wastewater, whether from the plant itself or from nearby treatment facilities, supplementing it with green ammonia from external suppliers, and converting a portion of it into hydrogen on site via a process called cracking. Hydrogen improves combustion, and the blend achieves the extremely high temperatures that cement production requires. The system will additionally modify its fuel mixture as renewable power availability fluctuates, enabling a cement plant to absorb surplus wind and solar energy when supply is abundant and to function as an intelligent energy hub for the electricity grid.
Life Cycle Assessment and Digital Twin
Professor Evina Katsou, Chair in Water Engineering in the Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, stated that a remedy which merely relocates the problem is insufficient, and that the team's task is to verify across the entire life cycle whether the approach genuinely reduces emissions without generating new difficulties, drawing on trialled scientific methods and data-driven evidence.
Project partners will initially test the key technologies individually in laboratories and small-scale experimental set-ups, then integrate them into a digital twin, a virtual replica, of an actual cement plant in 
Alicante, Spain
. Professor Katsou directs Imperial's contribution together with Dr Elham Mousania, Dr Daniel Dias and Dr Elena Koumaki. They will construct models that trace cement from raw materials to the factory gate, monitoring the energy and water consumed throughout. This method, known as life cycle assessment, reveals where the benefits lie, where trade-offs emerge and which stages require attention.
Collaborating with Norwegian project coordinator SINTEF, the researchers will also calculate what the new approach would cost and analyse its social impact, including worker safety and local job creation. They will examine what might obstruct adoption, such as the approvals required to change fuels and the regulations for grid connection, and convert their findings into recommendations for policymakers.
These results will feed into the digital twin, which is being constructed by project partner IDENER. Professor Katsou's group will also assist in designing its operator interface so that it works for the people using it, and the team is developing tools and training for those who will operate the technology.

Dr Mousania, Research Associate in the Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, stated that the interactive tool will enable other cement plants to explore the approach independently so owners can assess whether it would suit their site before committing investment, and that a training programme is being designed with plant managers, operations staff and technology developers. The training will be offered online so staff can learn at their own pace through the Erasmus+ Skills4EII Hub, part of the EU skills alliance for energy-intensive industries.
By the project's conclusion in August 2029, the partners aim to inform industry and policymakers with confidence whether converting cement production to ammonia and hydrogen genuinely delivers benefits, both for the environment and for the people who operate the plants. The findings could also assist other heavy industries, such as steel, glass and ceramics.

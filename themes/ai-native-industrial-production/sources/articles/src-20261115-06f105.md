Aaron Glenn has developed an unfortunate knack for mispeaking at halftime this season, but on Sunday, the Jets head coach finally found words that his long-suffering fan base could rally behind.

Last month, Glenn assured sideline reporter Aditi Kinkhabwala that the Jets would receive the ball to open the second half against the Patriots, 
only for New England to take the ensuing kickoff
. When a reporter asked him about it the following day, the team’s livestream of his press conference 
conveniently went dark
, though Glenn reportedly insisted his words had been misconstrued.

Glenn left far less to interpretation on Sunday, when 
CBS cameras caught the 54-year-old
 excoriating referee Ron Torbert and his crew as the teams filed off the field at halftime of an eventual 31-12 loss to the Bills, still incensed over a hit on Geno Smith that had inexplicably escaped a flag in the closing minutes of the half.

With the Jets driving inside Buffalo territory on third down late in the first half, Geno Smith slipped out of the pocket, set his feet, and was folded in half the instant he let go of the football, as linebacker Terrel Bernard barreled into his chest while rookie T.J. Parker dove at his knees. Smith crumpled to the turf and eventually into the league’s concussion protocol, and the officials, evidently satisfied with what they had seen, kept their flags in their pockets.

Glenn did not share their contentment, and instead of litigating the nuances of the rulebook, he challenged the officials to imagine how they would have responded had Buffalo’s quarterback been on the receiving end of the same hit.

“Here’s my problem with that: if that was Josh Allen, every flag on the field would be thrown,” Glenn can be seen telling the officials.

Given that the NFL has spent the better part of two decades wrapping its quarterbacks in an ever-thickening layer of bubble wrap, to the point where a pass rusher can now be penalized for landing on a passer with too much of his own body weight, you can understand Glenn’s frustration. And it takes a certain creativity to watch two defenders converge on a quarterback from opposite ends and conclude that nothing illegal occurred. Smith had left the pocket, which under NFL rules strips a passer of some of his special protections, including the one against low hits. But the rulebook restores all of them once a quarterback stops behind the line of scrimmage and resets to throw, and Smith had done just that by the time Bernard and Parker arrived to meet him from both directions.

Unfortunately, the letter of the law apparently also requires Gene Steratore to sign off on every call the officials make. The CBS rules analyst assured viewers that the crew had gotten it right, extending a habit of siding with officials that has 
drawn grumbles from viewers
 before. Bills fans know that tendency as well as anyone, having watched Steratore 
give his blessing to the overtime interception
 that ended their season in Denver last January. On Sunday, Buffalo finally landed on the right side of his rubber stamp.

The Jets, as usual, did not.

‘Let’s Go Change the World,’ Texas Appleseed Set to Honor Susman Godfrey, Vistra Corp. GC Stephanie Zapata Moore at Sold-Out Good Apple Dinner&nbsp;&nbsp;The Texas Lawbook

[page text]
Laranda Walker
 always comes away from Texas Appleseed’s annual Good Apple Dinner inspired by the honorees and keynote speaker. 

“I leave saying, ‘Let’s go change the world,’” said Walker, a partner in Susman Godfrey’s Houston office. 

This year, Walker and her colleagues will be among those celebrated. 

This week, the nonprofit public interest justice center Texas Appleseed will host its 25th Good Apple Dinner in Austin. The sold-out event also marks the nonprofit’s 30th anniversary. 

Among this year’s honorees is Susman Godfrey, which will receive the Pro Bono Leadership Award representing a North Texas family alleging racial discrimination, harassment and retaliation by their local school district. 

Texas Appleseed’s J. Chrys Dougherty Good Apple Award will go to Stephanie Zapata Moore, executive vice president, general counsel and chief compliance officer of Vistra Corp. The award recognizes individuals whose public service, pro bono work and support of their communities reflect Texas Appleseed’s mission of advancing justice and addressing the needs of underserved Texans. 

The honorees exemplify the responsibility that lawyers and corporate legal departments have to use their resources and expertise to serve others, said 
Ann Marie Painter
, president of Texas Appleseed’s board of directors and a partner at Haynes Boone’s Dallas office. 

“We are corporations, corporate legal departments, law firms that are rich in resources. We are rich in skills and experience and knowledge, and I think we have a responsibility to give back to the communities that we live and work in and to raise up the people around us like Stephanie and Susman Godfrey have,” Painter said. 

In Susman Godfrey’s case, that meant providing a family with access to legal representation they otherwise could not have afforded, Painter said. 

“They gave them a voice to be able to have somebody say, ‘This shouldn’t be happening to you or to anybody else,’” Painter said.

Susman Godfrey Fights for the Simpson Family

The federal civil rights lawsuit, filed Dec. 3, 2025, in the Eastern District of Texas, centers on Wade Simpson, a Black father of six who alleges that Melissa Independent School District, Superintendent Keith Murphy and district Police Chief Christopher Fontana violated his First and Fourteenth Amendment rights by retaliating against him for repeatedly raising concerns about racial discrimination and harassment at his children’s schools. 

According to the complaint, Simpson’s children were subjected to racial slurs, bullying and disparate disciplinary treatment, and district officials failed to address his complaints adequately. 

Instead, in May 2025, district police issued Simpson a criminal trespass warning following an encounter with a middle school principal during his daughter’s school play. 

Simpson alleges that he confronted the principal after believing she was deliberately walking past him and smirking at him to antagonize him, according to the complaint. He told her to leave him alone, using profanity. 

The following day, district police issued a criminal trespass warning barring Simpson for two years from district campuses, facilities and events, including off-campus events where the district was present. 

Simpson alleges the restriction prevented him from attending his children’s graduations, sporting events and school performances, participating in parent-teacher conferences and attending school board meetings. He also claims the district retaliated against him by prohibiting direct communication with his children’s teachers and administrators, requiring him instead to communicate through a designated human resources official. 

The district rescinded the trespass warning on Dec. 15, 2025, less than two weeks after Simpson filed suit. 

In a February motion to dismiss, Melissa ISD argued that Simpson failed to state a viable First Amendment retaliation claim because the trespass warning resulted from his confrontational behavior toward the principal, not his complaints about racial discrimination. The district also argued that Simpson failed to establish municipal liability under Section 1983 because he had not identified an unconstitutional policy or custom attributable to the school board, and that restrictions on his communications with school employees did not violate his constitutional rights.

The defendants further contended that Simpson’s parental-rights claim was legally insufficient, that the superintendent and police chief were entitled to qualified immunity and that his challenge to the trespass warning was moot because the district had rescinded it. 

The case is still ongoing. 

When Walker, who serves as vice president of Texas Appleseed, learned of the case, she immediately asked fellow partner 
Elizabeth Hadaway
 to work with her in representing the family.

Hadaway, who, along with Walker, was part of the legal team that secured a $787.5 million settlement for Dominion Voting Systems in its defamation case against Fox News and Fox Corporation, had previously taught through Teach For America. She also previously completed a fellowship at the nonprofit law firm Public Counsel, where she worked on a mix of impact litigation on educational equity issues and direct services for families with kids in special education or school disciplinary proceedings. At Susman, she’s retained a pro bono practice and Walker knew she’d been looking to add another pro bono case. 

The two took the case to the firm, where partners and associates vote on whether the firm should commit its time and resources to proposed pro bono and alternative-fee matters. The firm approved the representation and added associate 
Tyler Alabanza-Behard
, also an alumnus of Teach For America. 

“I felt very moved by this father who was very passionately trying to stand up for what he thought was right and for his children,” Walker said. 

The prospect of a parent being unable to attend important milestones in his children’s lives was heartbreaking, she said. 

The allegations of racial discrimination also resonated with Walker, a Black woman who grew up in a small Mississippi town and knows firsthand what it means to encounter inequity and feel that her voice is not being heard. 

“I feel very privileged to be in a position to serve and to give back, and I will never forget what it’s like to not have, and what it’s like to feel unseen and unheard,” Walker said. 

As a first-generation college and law school graduate, Texas Appleseed’s focus on justice and opportunity was what drew her to the board in the first place.

She was introduced to the nonprofit by Susman partner 
Mark Wawro
, who previously served on its board. The firm has a longstanding history of board involvement with Texas Appleseed, a commitment Painter said also factored into its selection for this year’s award. 

Walker, who now serves as the board’s vice president, recalled attending her first meeting and realizing the organization’s mission deeply resonated with her. 

“I remember going to the first board meeting and sitting there going, ‘This is it. This is so incredible the work that this organization does,’” Walker said. “And I feel that every time we have a board meeting. I leave feeling inspired.”

Vistra GC Stephanie Zapata Moore Expands Opportunity

Texas Appleseed came to know Zapata Moore through Vistra Corp.’s financial support. 

“Stephanie was someone who stood out in our minds as being a distinct individual in that GC role supporting initiatives and work that go beyond just business and legal,” Painter said. 

At Vistra, Moore has spent years championing initiatives designed to help employees advance professionally, including efforts to remove barriers that can prevent qualified candidates from being considered for jobs. 

Since Vistra emerged from bankruptcy in 2016, she has served as an executive sponsor of the company’s Women’s Information Network, an employee resource group focused on professional development, leadership and mentorship. 

The group hosts an annual leadership conference and events throughout the year that connect employees with company leaders and provide opportunities for professional growth. 

Zapata Moore has also supported Vistra’s skills-based hiring initiative, which focuses on candidates’ abilities and experience rather than traditional credentials, like four-year degrees. 

Under the program, identifying information, including names and schools attended, is removed from resumes during the initial screening process. 

“There are lots of studies that show that it helps you to not focus on things that are really not relevant to whether or not the person can actually do the work,” she explained. 

Zapata Moore said she has been encouraged by Vistra’s continued commitment to advancing women and underrepresented employees into leadership roles, even as corporate diversity initiatives have faced increasing scrutiny. 

She believes that commitment matters not only for employees already at the company, but also for those who might otherwise not envision themselves in leadership positions. 

“If you can see it, you can be it,” Zapata Moore said. “To just be present and show people who are coming up behind us that there are successful people who look like them, I think, is really important.” 

That same belief extends to the charitable organizations Zapata Moore serves. 

She spent a decade on the board of Girls Inc. of Metropolitan Dallas, including her final two years as chair, before reaching her term limit in July. She now serves on the organization’s fundraising foundation board.

The organization provides leadership skills training and educational support to girls ages 6 to 18. 

As the mother of three daughters and a son, Zapata Moore said she was drawn to the organization’s mission of helping girls develop the confidence, skills and opportunities to succeed. 

Zapata Moore has also supported the Dallas Hispanic Law Foundation and PODER25, an initiative of the Hispanic National Bar Association focused on increasing Hispanic representation among Fortune 500 general counsel. 

As a member of PODER25’s advisory board, she helps support aspiring general counsel through mentorship and professional development opportunities designed to prepare candidates for executive leadership positions. 

Zapata Moore sometimes wonders how the types of organizations and initiatives she supports might have helped her grandparents when they first arrived in the United States. 

When her maternal grandparents immigrated to El Paso from Chihuahua, Mexico, they had little formal education. Her grandfather, who attended school through the second grade, worked as a gardener. Her grandmother raised their six children. 

But they instilled the importance of education in their children and grandchildren. 

“They were just adamant that you have to have an education,” Zapata Moore recalled. “That is something nobody can take away from you. That is going to be your key to success.’” 

All six of their children graduated from college. Four went on to careers in education, becoming teachers and administrators. One of Zapata Moore’s uncles recently established an endowed scholarship at the University of New Mexico to support first-generation college students in honor of his parents. 

For Zapata Moore, her family was the first picture of possibilities. In the legal profession, she counts as mentors and friends 
Yvette Ostolaza
 of Sidley Austin, Hilda Galvan who retired from Jones Day and 
Ophelia Camiña
 of Susman Godfrey. 

“They are trailblazers for all women, but particularly Hispanic women, in the legal community and are so generous with their time and support for those following behind them,” Zapata Moore said. “True examples of if you can see it, you can be it.”

“I’m just super impressed and grateful for the work that Texas Appleseed is doing,” Zapata Moore said. “I think it meshes really well with the goals that Vistra has to support our stakeholders and our communities and ensure that there’s equal access for everyone.”

Endangered whale population drops again&nbsp;&nbsp;The Portland Press Herald

[page text]
The North Atlantic right whale population slipped again in 2025, alarming scientists who warn the endangered species remains locked in a fight for survival because of human-caused injuries and shifting ocean conditions.

The population dipped from an estimated 389 individuals in 2024 to 382 in 2025, according to whale researchers gathered at the annual North Atlantic Right Whale Consortium on Monday. The new count carries a margin of error of plus or minus 11 whales.

“More work is needed to protect this species,” said consortium chairman Heather Pettis, senior scientist at the New England Aquarium. “The population is not yet on a clear path to recovery and is incredibly vulnerable to entanglements and vessel strikes, threats we know how to reduce.”

The population drop arrives at a critical time for Maine’s $2 billion lobster industry, which has spent years fighting federal regulations designed to protect the whale from gear entanglements. Lobstermen say their gear plays a smaller role in whale deaths than regulators initially assumed.

Only 
one right whale death
 can be directly attributed to entanglement in Maine lobstering gear, industry officials say. Whale scientists note most whales that die from entanglement no longer have any rope attached, so knowing which fishery is to blame is impossible.

The industry’s arguments might have won over federal regulators, if not consortium scientists. Earlier this month, regulators announced initial findings from a new scientific model indicate U.S. fisheries account for about 25% of entanglements — half the amount previously assumed.

Carl Wilson, the commissioner of the Maine Department of Marine Resources, welcomed the agency’s shift in a bulletin to lobstermen. He said the update represents a commitment to sound science that avoids relying on worst-case scenarios.

This reduction could bring estimated U.S. fishery mortality within the federal limit known as potential biological removal, which defines the maximum number of human-caused deaths a marine mammal population can sustain and still recover. 

In 2019, regulators decided to attribute half of the annual whale deaths to U.S. fisheries, which exceeded that biological removal limit and prompted a wave of whale-protective fishing rules, such as the three-month closure of 967 square miles in Maine’s offshore lobstering grounds.

State research funded by federal grants has demonstrated that right whales rarely enter Maine state waters, resulting in far less overlap between local lobster gear and migrating whales than federal models projected. 

In 2023, the Maine Lobstermen’s Association won a federal appeals court ruling that rejected previous government risk models as flawed. Federal lawmakers have 
delayed new fishery rules
 through 2028, and are considering extending that moratorium to 2035.

“Recovery for this species depends on sustained protective actions,” said Pettis, the consortium’s chair.  “Actions to further delay entanglement protections and deregulate vessel strike reduction measures will stall meaningful progress that is already underway to protect the species.” 

Human activities continue to harm the species, scientists say. 

Researchers logged two right whale deaths in 2026, alongside eight human-caused injuries. In January, a 4-year-old male entangled in Canadian snow crab gear died off North Carolina. A 3-year-old female washed ashore dead in Virginia in February from unknown causes.

Of seven reported entanglements in 2026, three whales were observed still carrying the ropes, according to consortium data. One whale carried Massachusetts state lobster gear, while two were seen in Canadian waters with gear of unknown origin.

The right whale’s 2025 population is 20% below the species’ recent peak of 483 whales recorded in 2011. Scientists estimated that the number of breeding females held steady at 73, highlighting the ongoing difficulty of replenishing the stock.

Researchers pointed to one encouraging sign: Females gave birth to 24 calves during the 2025–2026 calving season, the highest single-year total since 2009.  Scientists don’t include newborn calves in the 2025 population estimate because young whales face high mortality rates.

“The whales are doing their part,” said Philip Hamilton, a senior scientist at the New England Aquarium. “But the overall lack of growth in the population is even more concerning when seen in the context of a number of proposed management setbacks in the U.S. this year.”

The consortium will compile a comprehensive annual report early in 2027, giving the regulators and fishermen a clearer look at the right whale’s future before federal advisory meetings on how the new statistical model will affect Maine’s lobster fleet resume in March.

New fungal disease found in Tulip Garden for first time
                        
Shafat Malik

                                                        16 Nov 2026 06:44 IST
                                                    
 Follow Us
                                    
Irpex lacteus identified as leaf-rot pathogen
Lab tests show up to 87.67% inhibition of fungal growth
Possible biological control option, say researchers
Srinagar, Nov 15: Scientists have identified a fungus responsible for leaf rot in tulips at Srinagar’s Indira Gandhi Memorial Tulip Garden for the first time, bringing to light a previously unreported plant disease in Kashmir’s tulip cultivation. The researchers have also found that a naturally occurring fungus can suppress the disease-causing organism by nearly 88 per cent under laboratory conditions.
The findings, published in the scientific journal Indian Phytopathology, accessed by Rising Kashmir, identify Irpex lacteus, a fungus generally associated with the decay of wood, as the cause of leaf rot in tulip plants collected from the garden.
The discovery is significant for Kashmir’s floriculture sector, particularly the Tulip Garden, which attracts large numbers of visitors during the spring flowering season. The study provides new information about a disease affecting tulip leaves and identifies a possible biological method of controlling the pathogen.
The research, titled First Report of Irpex lacteus Causing Leaf Rot of Tulip in Kashmir Himalaya, India, and In-Vitro Antagonistic Activity of Trichoderma spp., was conducted by Tabia Mushtaq, Shayiestah Maqbool, Abdul Hamid Wani, Zahara Sultan, Mohd Yaqub Bhat and Wasim Sajad Malik.
According to the study, affected tulip leaves developed irregular brown to dark-brown patches that gradually increased in size. As the disease progressed, the patches merged, causing portions of the leaves to die and collapse.
The researchers collected affected leaf tissue and carried out laboratory examinations to identify the organism responsible for the damage.
Using microscopic observations and molecular techniques, they identified the fungus as Irpex lacteus. Genetic analysis showed a 99.1 per cent match with a previously identified strain of the fungus.
The scientists then conducted experiments to determine whether the organism was actually responsible for the leaf damage.
When healthy tulip leaves were exposed to the isolated fungus under experimental conditions, disease symptoms appeared within five days. The researchers subsequently recovered the same fungus from the affected tissue, confirming its role in causing the disease.
The study describes this as the first confirmed report of Irpex lacteus causing tulip leaf rot in the Kashmir Himalaya.
The finding is notable because the fungus is primarily known for breaking down wood. Its identification as a pathogen affecting tulip leaves adds to scientific understanding of the diseases that can affect ornamental plants in the region.
However, the researchers did not limit their work to identifying the disease. They also examined whether other fungi could suppress the growth of the newly identified pathogen.
For this, they tested two locally obtained fungi, Trichoderma harzianum and Trichoderma asperellum, which are known for their ability to act against certain plant pathogens.
The results showed that Trichoderma harzianum inhibited the growth of the disease-causing fungus by an average of 87.67 per cent, while Trichoderma asperellum recorded an inhibition rate of 75.23 per cent.
These results were obtained through laboratory experiments in which the fungi were grown together to observe how effectively the tested organisms restricted the pathogen's growth.
The findings suggest that the two Trichoderma species could be investigated further as possible biological control agents against tulip leaf rot.
The researchers, however, have not established that the same level of protection can be achieved when the fungi are applied to tulip plants in the garden. The reported inhibition percentages relate only to fungal growth under laboratory conditions.
The study comes against the background of earlier research into fungal diseases affecting tulips in Kashmir, particularly those responsible for the decay of bulbs during cultivation and storage.
Unlike those investigations, the latest research focuses on damage to tulip leaves, an area the authors say has received comparatively less scientific attention in the region.
The identification of the pathogen provides a basis for further research into how the disease develops, the conditions that favour its spread and the measures that could be used to manage it.
The paper does not establish how widely the disease is present in the Tulip Garden, how many plants have been affected or whether the fungus poses a threat to the next flowering season.
It also does not report a current outbreak. Its findings are based on the examination of affected plant material and subsequent laboratory testing.
The researchers conclude that their work establishes Irpex lacteus as a cause of tulip leaf rot in Kashmir and provides preliminary evidence that locally occurring Trichoderma fungi may help control its growth.
The findings could assist future disease-monitoring and plant-protection research, particularly in a region where tulip cultivation has become an important part of the spring tourism season.

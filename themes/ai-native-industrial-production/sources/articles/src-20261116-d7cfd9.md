North Korea’s threat to the United States differs significantly from that faced during the first Trump administration because the security environment in Northeast Asia has changed dramatically. These shifts have also affected the U.S.–Republic of Korea (ROK) alliance, requiring both countries to adapt their roles and responsibilities in response to a more complex regional threat landscape shaped by closer ties among China, Russia, North Korea, and Iran. Although this alignment remains informal rather than a formal alliance, North Korea poses a much greater nuclear threat to U.S. and South Korean interests than it did five years ago. With China increasingly serving as the central player in this emerging authoritarian grouping and North Korea deepening military cooperation with Russia, there is a growing need to reassess the combined U.S.-ROK deterrence strategy and force posture.

Four major developments are intensifying this challenge in the Indo-Pacific: the Russia–North Korea military partnership, North Korea’s advancing nuclear and missile capabilities, China’s increasingly assertive behavior, and the more-permissive relationships among China, Russia, and North Korea. Together, these trends complicate the U.S.-ROK alliance’s efforts to deter aggression in Northeast Asia. From the U.S. perspective, North Korea is no longer the alliance’s sole security concern.

This report presents a new U.S.-Republic of Korea extended deterrence framework that goes beyond a North Korea–centric strategy to address security threats posed by China and Russia.

Key Takeaways

The U.S.-ROK alliance faces an increasingly complex deterrence environment shaped by multidomain threats from North Korea, China, and Russia
One destabilizing factor is North Korea’s belief that Russia will provide assistance if the United States and/or ROK respond to actions by North Korea.

The primary threat from China that must be deterred is any potential preemptive or early stage action that would be intended to reduce the reaction times and readiness of U.S. or ROK forces in the event of a crisis or conflict.

A new extended deterrence framework features four elements to better respond to the rapidly changing security environment
The new framework would address nuclear coercion, missile threats, cyber and space risks, and wider regional conflict.

It would also strengthen nuclear consultation while avoiding a larger operational role for nuclear weapons.

The framework would improve air and missile defense; deepen cooperation in space, cyber, and intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR); and broaden combined exercises.

It would increase South Korea’s defense contributions to support a stronger and more sustainable alliance framework.

Recommendations

The nuclear consultative mechanism should be updated to provide an effective framework for renewed planning and consultation regarding the defense of the ROK and of the U.S. forces and interests against both nuclear and non-nuclear threats discussed in this report. This mechanism should emphasize enhanced coordination and transparency rather than an expanded role for nuclear weapons, reflecting the allies’ shared objectives of strengthening deterrence credibility. 

The United States and South Korea should deepen cooperation in air and missile defense to mitigate North Korea’s, Russia’s, and China’s missile capabilities in future scenarios, especially in light of the U.S. development of the Golden Dome. U.S. military defense modernization should continue to pace the North Korean long-range missile threat while ensuring interoperability with ROK systems.

Alliance cooperation in space, cyber, and ISR to protect against these multidomain threats and ensure situational awareness of regional threats will be essential to an effective deterrence strategy. Such cooperation should also align with broader regional defense coordination efforts that connect Indo-Pacific and Korean Peninsula contingencies, including practical trilateral information-sharing and early warning collaboration with Japan.

In the future and consistent with the new strategic priority of increasing burden-sharing, the alliance should expand the scope of their combined training to include contingencies involving China and Russia. This effort should include broader multinational exercises, more-regular planning for cross-domain contingencies, and clearer procedures for information-sharing, command relationships, and crisis communication.

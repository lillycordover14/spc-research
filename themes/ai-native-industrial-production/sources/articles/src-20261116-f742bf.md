China’s rare earth giants double profits as prices soar

China’s two largest rare earths producers more than doubled their profits in the first nine months of 2026 as tighter supply controls and rising prices strengthened the country’s grip on a market critical to global manufacturing. 

The country’s biggest light rare earth firm, China Northern Rare Earth (CNRE) (SSE: 600111), reported net profit of 3.65 billion yuan ($547.2 million), based on third-quarter results. 

China Rare Earth Resources and Technology (SZSE: 000831), which focuses on heavy rare earths, more than doubled through the first nine months of the year to 408 million yuan ($61.1 million). 

“Supply-side reforms have helped support prices by improving industry discipline,” Citi analyst Anna Wang told 
Reuters
. 

The earnings gains highlight how Beijing’s efforts to curb oversupply and consolidate production are benefiting domestic producers while reinforcing China’s influence over strategic mineral supply chains. The controls also give President Xi Jinping leverage in trade negotiations with US President Donald Trump, as American manufacturers remain heavily dependent on Chinese rare earths. 

Prices for light rare earth compound neodymium-praseodymium (NdPr) are up 62% from when the controls were first introduced, to 719,000 yuan ($107,793).  

The two companies were the only ones given production quotas by China in 2024, when the country downsized from giving quotas to six previously. 

In 2025 China introduced tighter controls on the mining and processing of rare earths by extending the quota system to include imported materials and requiring companies to report on the flow of strategic minerals regularly.

China extends rare earth controls to imported material

With the consolidation and government strategies, both companies are set to beat analysts’ estimates for their full-year profit. 

China’s long game
 

After seeing what oversupply can do to other industries, China has used production quotas and consolidation to make sure rare earths would not be another victim of it. 

The group of minerals critical for defence, batteries and magnets have been prone to 
aggressive price swings
 that have squeezed supply and profits for producers. 

With a tighter grip on domestic production, and supported by high demand and prices, China uses that as leverage in negotiations with other powerful countries.  

China’s export controls cover several niche medium and heavy rare earths and the magnets that contain them. 

As Trump and Xi meet again this week, the American President will have little room for imposing more tariffs on China.  

Even with multi-million dollar investments in the sector, critical minerals projects in the US are still only on paper, years away from production.

Minnesota again facing nearly $950M in property tax increases in 2027

Josh Skluzacek & Harrison Klopp KSTP

Property tax sticker shock
 
The property tax burden on Minnesotans continues to grow.
Data from preliminary property tax levies across the state show that Minnesotans could have to pay nearly $950 million more in property taxes next year. That would be a 6.5% increase from 2026.
At this time last year, 
preliminary levies also totaled a $950 million increase from the year prior
. While the finalized levies came in $74 million lower than the initial rates, it’s still a tough pill to swallow for many Minnesotans.
RELATED: 
Which Minnesota counties and cities are facing the biggest 2027 property tax hikes?
Based on the current levy proposals for 2027, counties are set to collect more than $4.88 billion in property taxes next year, an 8.1% increase from 2026; City property tax levies total over $4.3 billion, also increasing 8.1% over 2026 levels; Townships are set to collect $367 million, up 4.9%; School levies total nearly $4.6 billion, a 5.0% increase from 2026; and special taxing district levies are set at $564 million, a 3.7% jump.
These are preliminary maximum rates, meaning they could go down but not up. The finalized rates for 2027 have to be set by late December.
RELATED: 
Minnesota school levy results: Which districts passed 2026 referendums?
The increases continue a trend of rising property taxes in Minnesota. County property taxes have already increased by more than $1 billion since 2021.
Homeowner reaction
Meyer Belkin has lived in his Lake Nokomis home for four years and said his notice showed a 31.3% property tax hike.
“When I look at that number, it makes me sick, it’s not fair, what’s coming next year, right?” Belkin said.
Mark Haveman is executive director of the Minnesota Center for Fiscal Excellence. He said declining commercial property values, unfunded state mandates and growing local governments could make things worse for homeowners.
“Local governments can do a lot more, in my opinion, the levy is called the tax of last resort for a good reason,” Haveman said.
Belkin said the issue is not just the bigger bill but whether he can afford to stay. He said he has an appointment with a realtor this week to walk through the house and discuss what it could sell for.
“I literally have an appointment with a realtor this week, who’s coming to walk through the house, and look at it, and tell me what they think I can sell it for,” Belkin noted.
The property tax figures will not be official until December. Residents will have a chance to debate the increases at Truth and Taxation meetings in the coming weeks.

The organizer of the Canadian Tulip Festival, which last week won “a Michelin star for festivals,” is calling on governments and other organizations to continue supporting local festivals. 

Last week, the festival accepted the 2027 World Tulip Destination Award at the World Tulip Summit in The Hague, Netherlands. 

“Two years ago, we won the Heritage Entertainment Award because we put on such great shows to teach people about the history and meaning behind the tulips. To get a Tulip Destination Award is basically like getting a Michelin star for festivals,” Jo Hall, the festival’s executive director, told OBJ Tuesday. 

“It’s peer-adjudicated, so if you’re putting on a good show with great ratings, you become a certified world tulip destination. It literally puts us on the map.” 

As the festival gears up for its 75th anniversary in 2027, Hall said the award is a feather in its cap. 

“It’s always good to be able to share that you’re an award-winning event. But it also brings awareness that Ottawa’s lucky to have such an internationally-renowned festival with over 377,000 guests that come from all across the world,” Hall said. 

The award comes at a difficult time for some Ottawa festivals. Recently, 
Ottawa Chamberfest launched a $200,000 fundraising campaign
 to help secure its future, while the Ottawa Jazz Festival launched an initiative to raise $400,000 to address its deficit and cover outstanding invoices. 

Hall said the tulip festival is “a bit of an outlier.”

“Festivals are sort of in the vanguard of the current economic landscape. We know everyone has to tighten their belts, that government funding is down, corporate sponsorships are down and costs are rising. It’s no surprise that other large festivals are out looking for support. We need to continue to do the good work to keep up not only morale, but the beauty that comes along with these things as well as the tourism impact.

“Our festival generates $40 million. Last year, we got $70,000 from the City of Ottawa. That’s like 20 cents a head. We can’t get back to pre-pandemic funding levels. We used to get $100,000 from Canadian Heritage, $100,000 from the province and $100,000 from the city. Now all of those have been reduced. But we are fortunate in so much as because it’s a veteran-based story, we also work with Veterans Affairs Canada. So we have another pillar that we can lean on and then our corporate sponsorships and community partners,” she said. 

Since the tulip festival will never be gated or ticketed, it has to find other means to generate revenue, Hall added. 

“The gift of tulips was given to all Canadians. We’re never going to ticket (the festival). You wouldn’t do that to Remembrance Day. So you add in paid activities as you are able to help bring it those extra funds … Our concessions are run by the National Capital Commission, so we don’t see the revenue from food sales. We are held on federal land, so we lease the park from the NCC and the NCC plants the tulips. We celebrate them.” 

Earlier this year, Ottawa Tourism launched a major festivals fund, a two-year pilot program designed to support large-scale, tourism-generating festivals and events. Hall said she applied to receive funding, but certain criteria excluded the tulip festival from being accepted. 

“One of them was that you couldn’t have more than 50 per cent government funding. Also, they look at your debt ratio, so it’s a situation where if you need the money, you can’t get it … Despite generating significant economic benefits for the city, as well as being iconic, the niceties and meanings don’t matter. It’s coming down to dollars and cents,” she said.

Hall said that it’s “heartbreaking” that events like the Ottawa Jazz Festival and Ottawa Chamberfest have to launch fundraising campaigns. 
“The (municipal accommodation tax) brings in like $20 million and there’s more than enough to go around and support the festivals. I think that really needs to be looked at carefully. The people who generate the MAT tax should be on the receiving end of that benefit,” she said. 

Hall said she’s motivated by hearing how the tulip festival has impacted people. “When you work with as many veterans (as we do) and meet people who have been affected by Canada’s role in the liberation of the Netherlands, it reinvigorates us to stay the course.”

Looking ahead to the 2027 event in May, she said she will have less than two months to put it all together since she receives details about the festival’s budget in February or March, leading her to start thinking of ways to save now, while still putting on a worthwhile event. 

“We would love to be able to have a bit more. We’ve had to cancel our traditional fireworks. It’s difficult … (We’d love) to be able to sit down with the city, especially for our diamond jubilee year, and talk about something that would be a bit more fulsome, considering the product that we offer and the story we tell.

“We do our best to fundraise as well through our tulip bulbs and through our 50-50, but again, it shouldn’t rest on the public when there’s already more than enough funds available if they’re used correctly,” she said.

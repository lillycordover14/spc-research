RECORD LOW CRIME RATES: Crime drops again in major cities across California

Violent crime fell 6.3% ⬇️

Homicides fell 11.9% ⬇️

What you need to know:
 
 
New data released from the Major Cities Chiefs Association on November 12 provides another sign of progress as California’s crime rates remain at some of the lowest rates in recorded history. Under Governor Newsom’s public safety strategies, crime rates continue to drop, making California’s communities and residents safer.

SACRAMENTO
 – 
Under Governor Newsom’s public safety strategies, crime rates are already at some of the 
lowest rates in recorded history
 and continue to drop. In another sign of progress, new data released by the 
Major Cities Chiefs Association
 shows that comparing the first nine months of 2025 with the first nine months of 2026, violent crime fell 6% and homicides fell 12% across nine major California jurisdictions combined. 

This MCCA report shows a year-over-year decline in violent crime across California, progress that reflects a sustained, whole-of-government approach to public safety: putting more law enforcement officers on the ground, giving state and local agencies better tools, disrupting organized criminal networks, keeping guns out of dangerous hands, taking deadly drugs out of circulation and investing in prevention and intervention.

California is making real progress in reducing violent crime and making our communities safer. These results are no accident – they reflect our sustained investment in public safety and strong partnership with local law enforcement. But the work is far from over. The investments and partnerships we’ve strengthened are laying the foundation for lasting progress – equipping law enforcement with the tools and resources to prevent violence, hold offenders accountable, and keep California safe for years to come.

Governor Gavin Newsom

Crime continues to decline across California

⬇️ Violent crime in Oakland fell by 19%

⬇️ Violent crime in Sacramento fell by 15%

⬇️ Homicides dropped 44% in Sacramento

⬇️ Homicides dropped 30% in Los Angeles County Sheriff’s jurisdiction.

⬇️ Homicides dropped 22% in Oakland

⬇️ Homicides dropped 13% in Los Angeles

Statewide, California’s homicide rate fell to 3.5 per 100,000 residents in 2025, the lowest level since statewide reporting began in 1966.

Since Governor Newsom took office, the homicide rate is down 20%, robbery 31%, property crime 24%, motor vehicle theft 19% and violent crime 3%, according to statewide DOJ data through 2025. 

The Governor’s public safety strategy has included investments of over $2.1 billion since 2019 in law enforcement, partnerships with local agencies, organized crime enforcement, gun safety, drug interdiction and violence prevention. Earlier this year, the state also awarded $107 million in violence intervention grants to help prevent crime before it occurs. Since 2019, California has provided $350 million in violence intervention funding, helping local communities prevent more than 30,000 violent incidents.

California in comparison 

California’s homicide rate remains substantially lower than rates in many other states. According to 
CDC data
 from 2024, the latest year available for all states, California’s homicide rate fell from 5.1 to 4.5 per 100,000. Mississippi’s rate (19.7) was more than four times California’s; Louisiana’s rate (15.2) was more than three times California’s; Alabama’s rate (13.3) was nearly three times California’s; and Tennessee (10.0), South Carolina (9.9), Missouri (9.4), and Georgia (9.2) all had rates more than double California’s.

State and local law enforcement working together

Public safety works best when state and local law enforcement have the resources and capacity to work together.

That is why California has invested in deeper regional partnerships through the California Highway Patrol, allowing state officers to work side-by-side with local police departments and sheriffs to target crime where it is most concentrated. In 2024, California also reached its goal of adding 
1,000 new CHP officers
, strengthening the state’s ability to support local law enforcement and respond to crime statewide. 

In 2025, CHP crime suppression teams 
expanded
 to San Diego, the Inland Empire, Los Angeles, the Central Valley, the Sacramento Valley and the San Francisco Bay Area, bringing sustained enforcement and greater visibility to communities that need it most. 

Going after organized crime — not just individual crimes

Organized retail theft does not stop at a city or county line. Criminal networks operate across jurisdictions, which is why California built a statewide response.

Established in 2019, the Organized Retail Crime Task Force has brought law enforcement, prosecutors and retailers together to identify and dismantle organized theft networks. California has backed that strategy with major investments in local enforcement, investigative technology, regional operations and vertical prosecution.

In 2023, Governor Newsom launched the 
largest-ever state investment
 in organized retail theft, awarding more than $242 million to 38 local law enforcement agencies. 

California leads the nation on gun safety

California believes gun violence is preventable — and has built 
a national model
 for doing something about it.

The state is ranked #1 in the nation for gun law strength by both the 
Giffords Law Center
 and 
Everytown for Gun Safety
 and consistently maintains one of the nation’s lowest firearm death rates.

California put robust state investment in community violence intervention on the map. What began as a $9 million-a-year program grew roughly eightfold—more than the state had invested in all prior years combined—and the Governor signed 
Assembly Bill 28
, creating the first dedicated funding stream for the California Violence Intervention and Prevention Program. 

California was the first state in the nation to enact a 
Gun Violence Restraining Order law
. Now, families have access to nine different protection orders that can temporarily remove firearms from individuals who pose a danger to themselves or others. 

The state has paired these protections with universal background checks, stronger safe-storage requirements, measures targeting ghost guns and gun trafficking, accountability for irresponsible dealers and investments in community-based violence prevention.

Taking fentanyl off California streets

California’s public safety strategy is also focused on disrupting the criminal networks trafficking fentanyl into communities.

Since 2021, 
California National Guard Counterdrug Task Force
 analysts have supported law enforcement in intercepting approximately 39,644 pounds of fentanyl and 54.6 million fentanyl pills, with an estimated street value of $534 million.

At the same time, California has paired enforcement with lifesaving prevention measures. The state’s 
Naloxone Distribution Project
 has supported more than 460,000 overdose reversals.

Keeping dangerous hemp, kratom and 7-OH products off store shelves

Governor Newsom is also taking action to protect Californians from illegal and potentially dangerous intoxicating products, with nearly 
15,300 illegal hemp, kratom and 7-hydroxymitragynine (7-OH) products removed
 from California store shelves.

California’s latest enforcement results show continued near-universal compliance among Alcoholic Beverage Control (ABC) licensees. State officials conducted 23,667 site visits related to hemp, identifying 158 violations and removing 7,403 illegal hemp products from store shelves, with 99.33% compliance. For kratom and 7-OH, officials conducted 8,038 site visits, identified 181 violations and removed 7,888 prohibited products, with 97.75% compliance.

A public safety strategy built around what works 

The results speak for themselves: crime is down. From historic investments in law enforcement to community-based violence prevention, California is taking a comprehensive approach to making communities safer.

This progress didn’t happen overnight; it 
reflects years of investing in enforcement
, accountability and prevention — and staying focused on what works.

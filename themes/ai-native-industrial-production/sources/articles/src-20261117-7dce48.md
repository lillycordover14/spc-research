    Inside Tripp’s Store, everything on display has a story

    The local store, run by Tracy Kilpatrick, features art, antiques and upcycled items.

Jackson Crouch
, 
Staff Writer
                 
             •  
            
November 16, 2026
 

 

Annie Griffin
 
Tripp’s store, pictured from the parking lot, sits right off of Mt Carmel Church road in Chapel Hill.
 

Tripp’s Store, contrary to its name, is a place for far more than just buying and selling. Located in Chapel Hill on Mount Carmel Church Road, the store is a self-proclaimed “emporium of fun and funk,” filled with art, antiques and upcycled clothing. But spending any amount of time inside of it, it is clear Tripp’s Store is not just a shop, but a hub for creativity and community.

Tripp’s Store was founded by North Carolina native Tracy Kilpatrick and Danielle Lyn. But since last January, Kilpatrick has been running the shop solo after Lyn left the area. Wednesday through Saturday,
 
she spends her time creating art, collecting antiques and meeting the friendly faces inside Tripp’s Store.

“She’s very worldly, open-minded, approachable and kind of eclectic in her taste,” Jo Rodgers, the vice chair of professional education and practice at the UNC Eshelman School of Pharmacy who has been visiting Tripp’s Store regularly for over a year, said. “She appreciates all that’s in the world, and trying to reutilize things that we have in our environment, or make sure they go to another home, if you will, and that they’ll be loved by someone else.”

It is apparent from the moment you walk in that the place is teeming with creativity. Art spills off its walls and out the door, with statues and paintings lining its exterior and interior.

Kilpatrick repurposed Tripp’s Store out of an old community store from the 1950s called Tripp’s Grocery. She recalls her grandfather often visiting the store, and now tries to pay homage to its original purpose of cultivating a local community. 

“I never know what it’s gonna turn into and that’s the beauty of it,” Kilpatrick said. “It’s a safe place, and a fun place and creative space where everybody who walks through the door is welcome, and if they don’t feel welcome that’s on them not on me.”

Painted bottles, antique dolls, bedazzled babydoll heads and patchwork denim decorate the store. But for Kilpatrick, every item in Tripp’s Store is far more than just a product. 

“Everybody has a story, and all these things that are in here, they are things, but they’re expressions of stories,” Kilpatrick said. “They either came from somewhere that had a story, and what I’m trying to do is find a place for them to go to continue the story.”

More than the products, though, to Kilpatrick, the store’s real value comes from the people that walk through the door. Kilpatrick has endless stories of people who have left an impact on her, or vice versa, often in the most unexpected ways. She’s met a photographer, a 6-year old with a furniture obsession and songwriter Clifton Castelloe, who, after spending hours in her store, wrote a song dedicated to her titled “Tracy Mae.”

“That’s the beauty of life is that every person you come in contact with, you have that opportunity,” Kilpatrick said. You have that opportunity to leave them with something and they have that opportunity to leave you with something.”

One such example is Brian Littlejohn, who lives up the road and has been coming to Tripp’s Store almost every day since his mother died. Littlejohn, who talks about the store like he might as well work there, sits and talks alongside Kilpatrick daily. Just like Kilpatrick, his favorite part about the place is talking to whoever finds themselves in the store that day.

“We do meet interesting faces, I’ll tell you, some of the customers are very interesting,” Littlejohn said. 

Despite working solo now, Kilpatrick shows no intention of ever leaving Tripp’s behind. She refers to the store as her “alternative living room,” and it doesn’t take long to see how the familial atmosphere infects every customer that steps inside. 

“It’s hard to walk in there and sit on the couch and not just feel at home and relaxed and like you’re talking to a family member,” Rodgers said.

                                In 2025, Joel Wuthnow wrote “How China Could Counter U.S. Intervention in War Over Taiwan,” arguing that Beijing’s best option might be to deter Washington from coming to Taiwan’s aid by raising the risks to the U.S. homeland. A year and a half later, we asked him to revisit that argument.Image: Lance Cpl. Victor GurrolaIn your 2025 article, you argued that debates over a Taiwan conflict focus too heavily on China striking U.S. forces first, when Beijing’s most attractive option may be keeping the United States out of the fight altogether by threatening the American homeland. From what’s publicly available about Chinese military thinking and planning since then, how well has that assessment held up?It’s clear that Beijing is still developing ways to deter U.S. intervention and intimidate U.S. allies. The Department of Defense’s 2025 report,  Military and Security Developments Involving the People’s Republic of China, noted several developments that could strengthen China’s strategic deterrent, including fielding long-range DF-27 conventional missiles, moving toward a launch-on-warning nuclear posture, and expanding silo fields in western China. Expanding and diversifying these strategic capabilities could allow Beijing to conduct nuclear signaling during a crisis and raise the risks of any U.S. intervention triggering inadvertent escalation with a nuclear-armed rival.  There have also been displays of strategic capabilities that, in my estimation, were designed to undermine U.S. confidence in regional intervention. At the September 2025 military parade in Beijing, China showcased a number of new deterrent capabilities, such as the JL-3 submarine-launched ballistic missile, the JL-1 air-launched ballistic missile, and the DF-61 road-mobile intercontinental ballistic missile. In combination, these capabilities represented the legs of a new triad focused on Washington.

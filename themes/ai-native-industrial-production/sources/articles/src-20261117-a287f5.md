‘A national embarrassment’: Measles is again endemic in the US

 

Sarah Boden

 

Measles

Public Health

 

CDC / Molly Kurnit, MPH

Share

Copied to clipboard

Measles is again endemic in the United States. The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) says the virus has circulated in the country for at least 12 months, putting families and communities at risk. 
PAHO reached this decision after reviewing epidemiologic data from January 2025 through January 2026. This period predates Pennsylvania's measles outbreak, which began in April 2026 and has exploded into the largest US outbreak in more than 35 years. That state has reported more than 1,500 cases and at least seven measles-associated deaths. In total, state health departments have reported 13 fatalities from 2025 through 2026. 
“This represents both a national embarrassment and the tip of a dangerous iceberg,” said Jesse L. Goodman, MD, MPH, chair of Georgetown University's Expert Vaccine Analysis Team and a former Chief Scientist at the Food and Drug Administration. 
Measles was declared eliminated from the United States in 2000 after PAHO, which is the regional office of the World Health Organization, determined the country had interrupted endemic transmission. This triumph of public health hinged on the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine (MMR), which is 97% effective against measles after two doses.
This represents both a national embarrassment and the tip of a dangerous iceberg.
But MMR uptake has fallen over the years; epidemiologists and infectious disease physicians have long warned it was only a matter of time before endemic transmission of this airborne virus would return.  
Now the United States has lost control of measles, arguably the most contagious disease known to humans. It can go wherever it wants, said John D. Gräbenstein, PhD, the former director of the US Department of Defense's Military Vaccine Agency.
"I'm amazed at how casually people are taking it," he said. 
I'm amazed at how casually people are taking it.
PAHO director Jarbas Barbosa, MD, MPH, PhD, said during today's press conference that the situation in the United States requires urgent action, as continued transmission puts people who are unable to be vaccinated at risk, including infants who are too young to receive the MMR shot (see related 
CIDRAP News story
 today).
West Texas outbreak marked a turning point
When exactly the United States lost control of measles is hard to pinpoint. Though several experts who spoke to CIDRAP News for this story agree that last year's outbreak in West Texas, the largest the country had seen this century, was both a watershed moment and a failure of the Trump administration to respond adequately to the crisis. 

County courthouse in Gaines County, Texas. Of the 762 measles cases reported in Texas last year, 414 were reported in Gaines County.
Credit: Carol M. Highsmith / Wikipedia

"They've botched this response," said Georges C. Benjamin, MD, head of the American Public Health Association.
"This one stinks at the top. Kennedy, you got to go," Benjamin added, in reference to Robert F. Kennedy Jr., secretary of the US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS).
Kennedy, a longtime vaccine skeptic with a track record of spreading misinformation about vaccines, has pushed for policies at HHS that undermine vaccine access, including to MMR. 
The secretary takes a somewhat cavalier approach to infectious disease, said Jon Zelner, PhD, an epidemiologist at the University of Michigan School of Public Health who studies how disease spreads through social networks. For example, Kennedy 
said in February
 that he wasn't afraid of COVID-19 because he used to do cocaine off bathroom toilets. 
This one stinks at the top. Kennedy, you got to go.
"It is no surprise that the resurgence of measles has coincided with an administration that has inflicted significant damage to our healthcare system while spreading false claims about vaccines," said Richard Besser, MD, president and CEO of the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation and former acting director of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).
US needs to bulk up measles surveillance 
The United States is expected to submit a corrective action plan to PAHO in the coming weeks. The same goes for Bolivia and Mexico, which also lost their elimination status. 
In addition to raising vaccination rates, US counties will need to improve rapid response to outbreaks and increase measles surveillance, said Daniel Salas, MD, PAHO's executive manager for immunizations.

PAHO's headquarters in Washington, DC.
Credit: PAHO / Flickr

Surveillance can take multiple forms, George Mason University epidemiologist Amira Roess, PhD, MPH, told CIDRAP News. Roess is a former CDC Epidemic Intelligence Service officer.
Testing a certain percentage of kids every month for measles, monitoring untreated municipal sewage for the measles virus, and routinely analyzing emergency department records for early patterns of fever and rash cases—telltale symptoms of the disease—are all strategies the CDC can employ to identify potential outbreaks. 
"Unfortunately, the systems that we did have in place were subjected to significant disruptions, and some of them faced cuts to resources and personnel recently," said Roess.
Many state and local public health agencies are chronically under-resourced, and funding cuts made after President Donald Trump returned to office further hindered this workforce's ability to respond to measles. 
"Everyone is doing about as much as humanly possible, day in and day out," Chrissie Juliano, MPP, executive director of Big Cities Health Coalition, said of state and local health departments during a press conference that her organization hosted yesterday. 
Everyone is doing about as much as humanly possible, day in and day out.
In a statement, CDC director Erica Schwartz, MD, MPH, JD, said the federal government has invested $100 million in state and local health departments in order to bring the MMR vaccine to communities where transmission is occurring. 
"Losing a designation doesn't mean we abandon an evidence-based approach," said Schwartz. "It means we remain focused on stopping outbreaks."
Vaccine skepticism is bigger than Kennedy 
It's unclear how the United States will approach a corrective PAHO plan, or if the loss of elimination status will prompt the Trump administration to change its approach to measles, since the president puts little value on multinational organizations. 
Still, this troubling development highlights how rapidly Kennedy has undone decades of public health achievement, said the University of Pittsburgh's Miranda Yaver, PhD, who studies health politics.

peterschreiber.media / iStock

Yaver predicts the PAHO decision will intensify calls to impeach Kennedy. But Democrats, who will soon control Congress, seem more focused on the healthcare affordability crisis and may have little appetite to address vaccine policy, a politically polarizing issue. 
"Case counts aren't radically shifting public opinion toward MMR vaccines," said Yaver. "So this restoration of public health resources also has to go hand-in-hand with broader efforts to rebuild trust in science and in government, but that's no easy feat.”
In some ways, Kennedy is a symptom of a bigger problem, said Zelner.
Factors driving the drop in MMR uptake include distrust in institutions, a fragmented public health system, and a dysfunctional medical system in which people's access to care often depends on insurance that is tied to employment. 
"It's just a cruel, cruel system of healthcare," he said.
The United States could have done more to address vaccine hesitancy well before Kennedy took over HHS, agreed Roess, who has published research on vaccine hesitancy patterns. She noted the federal government has never launched an effective response to rejection of MMR, even though it's been an issue for decades.
It's just a cruel, cruel system of healthcare.
"In the 1990s, [vaccine hesitancy] really started to accelerate. It was sort of knocking on the mainstream door," she said.
Now people who weren't vaccinated as children are becoming parents, making measles a multigenerational scourge. That includes congenital measles, a growing public health concern in which a pregnant woman passes the virus to her unborn child, resulting in stillbirth and birth defects. As 
CIDRAP News reported yesterday
, there have been at least 168 measles infections in pregnant US women since 2025. A Public Health Alerts 
report yesterday
 noted 112 cases in pregnant women in the same time span, as well as 13 cases of congenital measles.
Hard lessons on the horizon
Barbosa said the United States can end endemic transmission of measles, as Brazil has done. The country lost its elimination status in 2019 when a low MMR rate allowed imported cases to cause endemic spread. But Brazil regained this status in 2024.
One key intervention Brazil employed is a strategy called microplanning, said Lorena Barberia, PhD, a political scientist at the University of São Paulo. Microplanning is carried out at the municipal level, where public health workers identify under-vaccinated neighborhoods and communities and then target interventions accordingly.
But perhaps most importantly, Brazilians overall favor these initiatives. 
"When cases are reported and vaccination and testing are needed to block transmission, there is widespread public support and adherence to these interventions," said Barberia.
Experts agree a critical first step for the United States to end endemic measles is to abolish vaccine exemptions that are based on personal or religious beliefs at both public and private schools. In the current US political climate, such a policy change is unlikely. 
Zelner predicts the crisis must first worsen with more people dying or suffering life-altering consequences of a measles infection, such as blindness or permanent brain damage, before the country can again bring the virus to heel. 
"And that's not fair, right?" he said. "This shouldn't be the way that people learn this lesson."

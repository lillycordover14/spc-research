Australia Collaboration Targets Certified Laser Repairs for Military Aircraft Components


            Supported by the Additive Manufacturing Cooperative Research Centre, Rosebank Engineering, RMIT Centre for Additive Manufacturing and Swinburne University of Technology are working to develop a certifiable laser directed energy deposition (DED) repair process for high-value aerospace alloys used in modern military aircraft.

An industry-led research collaboration is set to transform how Australia maintains its most critical defense aircraft — shifting from costly, long-lead-time component replacement to certified, high-performance laser repair.

Supported by the 
Additive Manufacturing Cooperative Research Centre
 (AMCRC), the two-year collaboration brings together 
Rosebank Engineering
, 
RMIT Centre for Additive Manufacturing
 and 
Swinburne University of Technology
 to develop a certifiable laser directed energy deposition (DED) repair process for high-value aerospace alloys used broadly across modern military aircraft.

The research focuses on precipitation-hardening materials widely used in defense aviation, including Inconel, titanium and 15-5PH stainless steel, with the goal of demonstrating the repair of an advanced military aircraft component in line with OEM and NASA-STD-6030 requirements.

At the core of the project is a laser rescanning technique designed to control microstructure during the repair process itself. By tailoring heat input locally, the team aims to achieve the required material property performance without post-process heat treatment — a major barrier to qualifying additive repairs in aerospace.

Rosebank Engineering will lead process development and validation, integrating its Adaptive Monitoring and Control of Laser Additive Deposition (Amclad) system to promote consistent and repeatable repair outcomes.

Jarrod Watts, senior manager of engineering and projects at Rosebank Engineering, says the project is a substantial step toward achieving certified laser repairs of high-value, high-strength steel components.

“These types of high-value components are currently sourced — in the sustainment environment — from complex international supply chains with long lead-times. We know laser offers the opportunity to repair in-country with significant cost and lead-time reductions. This project tackles that challenge directly,” Watts says.

“Through the use of our Amclad capability, and in-process refinement of the microstructure, we can deliver material performance that meets aerospace requirements without risking distortion or damage of the original component.”

“Developing the ability to reliably repair critical components in-country mitigates supply chain risks, reduces cost of ownership for our customers and improves operational readiness.”

Researchers at RMIT Centre for Additive Manufacturing and Swinburne University of Technology will generate the technical evidence supporting qualification, including tensile strength, fatigue life, residual stress, corrosion and wear performance, forming a comprehensive Material Property Suite aligned with NASA standards.

Professor Milan Brandt of RMIT Centre for Additive Manufacturing, overseeing the laser process optimization, says the project pushes additive process control into new territory. “Laser rescanning allows us to refine microstructures in ways conventional heat treatment cannot achieve in repair scenarios. By controlling phase transformations during deposition, we can unlock performance levels that meet aerospace standards,” Brandt says.

Swinburne University of Technology will undertake detailed material characterization and mechanical testing as part of the project.

Professor Suresh Palanisamy of Swinburne University of Technology underlines the importance of rigorous validation to industry adoption. “Certification is evidence-driven. Our role is to produce the data that proves the process is robust, repeatable and capable of meeting aerospace requirements. That depth of validation is what enables industry confidence,” Palanisamy says.

The project will also explore repair pathways for in-demand F/A-18 engine components, providing a near-term commercial opportunity that supports broader adoption across a wider family of aircraft.

Simon Marriott, managing director of AMCRC, says the project reflects the organization's industry-first focus. “Australia has strong additive manufacturing research capability. AMCRC exists to translate that strength into commercial and defense outcomes,” Marriott says. “This project exemplifies that mission — industry-led, defense-relevant and built around certification from day one. By enabling certified local repair, we strengthen sovereign manufacturing capability and deliver long-term economic and strategic benefit.”

As the Australian Air Force seeks to sustain high-value platforms over extended service lives, the successful qualification of laser DED repair is said to have the potential to significantly reduce lifecycle costs, improve aircraft availability and strengthen supply chain resilience.

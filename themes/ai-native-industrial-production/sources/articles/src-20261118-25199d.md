5 Soft Rock Songs With The Best Instrumental Outros

By
 
Nicholas Vrchoticky

There are thousands of stellar soft rock songs out there that span almost the entirety of rock 'n' roll's existence. They fill you with emotions and take you on gentle journeys. They're not the headbangers or fast driving pulses of their harder rock cousins, but they have their place. And to many, they're even a favorite subgenre of rock music. They're known for nostalgia, playing over grocery store aisles, and even romantic encounters — though this is definitely track dependent. One thing soft rock isn't known for, however, are its instrumental outros, which is strange, because it honestly has some of the best closeouts in the biz.

The songs on this list aren't hard rock songs, so you shouldn't expect highly distorted, finger burning guitar solos on the outros. That's not really what soft rock is about, even if some do contain solos. A soft rock outro is meant to keep you on the flow of the song, make you want more of it, and have you rewinding or replaying the track just to have another taste. And, that's exactly what the best instrumental outros in soft rock do. 

Rosanna — Toto

Toto has had a couple songs post up near the very top of the U.S. singles chart, and while "Africa" is this rock band's most famous of them, "Rosanna" comes in second. The tune ranked No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 a few months after it came out in 1982, and it's a soft rock favorite from start to finish.

"Rosanna" begins with a groovy drum intro that soon turns into a full instrumental hook that you'll be humming for hours. The song itself, named after Toto keyboardist Steve Porcaro's girlfriend at the time — actress Rosanna Arquette — plays as a plea from a man who saw his relationship end and wants nothing more than to have the fictional Rosanna back in his arms. Even though the song has sad, romantic lyrics, it still retains a rather poppy and enjoyable soft rock melody throughout.

As "Rosanna" reaches a little after the 4-minute and 30-second mark, the outro begins, and it's fantastic. The piano and lead guitar exchange 
some of the best soft rock solos
 on top of the song's already great melody.

Baker Street — Gerry Rafferty

Gerry Rafferty's hit soft rock song "Baker Street" came out in 1978, peaked at No. 2 on the Billboard 100, and never died. Well, not in our hearts anyway. This is up there with 
the best soft rock songs
 ever. It features an iconic and unmistakable saxophone intro and similar instrumental breaks throughout, which provides the basis for the hook. The lyrical story is mostly autobiographical, talking about how Rafferty feels alone and wants to change his life. It's a bit sad, and so is the overall instrumental, but it's in an absolutely beautiful way that lasts from the intro through the outro — which might be the best outro in soft rock.

Leave it to Rafferty and soft rock in general to subvert any expectations around a song's close. With most genres of rock, a good instrumental outro would be a heavy guitar solo — maybe some slamming drums — and that's about it. Now, this song does end on a guitar solo, but it plays directly with the bluesy jazz-style melody set by the woodwind. They join together and flow into each other like a slow river from roughly the 3-minute and 30-second mark until the end of the song.

Sailing — Christopher Cross

Coming in with a Billboard No. 1 hit from 1980, we have Christopher Cross' soft rock favorite "Sailing." This song represents the epitome of soft rock. It's a dreamy tune that has you floating on roughly 4 minutes and 15 seconds of musical bliss — basically, one of 
the happiest soft rock songs ever
. And when it comes to the outro, "Sailing" lives up to its name. It evokes the feeling of laying in the sun on a boat deck while the comfortably warm summer afternoon passes by.

The instrumental lasts roughly 24 seconds as the song comes to a close. It's not special in the way that it adds solos or does any sort of musical trickery. No, this outro continues the ride with a similar dreamy melody and acoustic guitar, and you find yourself hitting the replay button simply because you don't want the emotional sunlight to be over.

Cross crafted this song to capture the peaceful feelings he felt while on literal sailing trips, and he wrote pretty much the whole thing — minus the bridge, which took an additional year — while sitting at his kitchen table. He really did a great job too, as you can't help but taste the salt water from beginning to end.

The Logical Song — Supertramp

If you're not already familiar with Supertramp and their work, you absolutely should be. Supertramp was a British rock band that was popular during the '70s and '80s, and they had several hits peak either at or near the top of the charts. The band sits somewhere in between arena rock, progressive rock, and art rock, and they're known for songs like "Take the Long Way Home" and "Give a Little Bit." But one of their biggest hits in the United States also had one of the best instrumental outros in all of soft rock, and that's "The Logical Song."

This tune hit No. 6 on the Billboard Hot 100, and for good reason. It's seriously groovy. It's got musical hooks for days. It will keep you playing it over and over while you bop around the house or bob your head side to side on that long car ride. When the leadup to the outro starts a little less than 3 minutes in, it begins with the bass taking the stage with a slick riff before giving way to some pretty clean piano work. Before you know it, John Helliwell hits you with his masterful saxophone work, leading you out of the song with a wicked woodwind solo.

While both "The Logical Song" and Gerry Rafferty's "Baker Street" feature wind moving through brass to close out their soft rock hits, they certainly don't compete. They have different feels and tones, and they both deserve a place in the best instrumental soft rock finales.

Hotel California — The Eagles

Here's a song that every soft rock and classic rock fan knows intimately: "Hotel California." This 1976 hit by the Eagles took the world by storm when it came out. The album of the same name smashed onto the Billboard 200 album chart and sped its way to No. 1 in under a month. The title track wasn't far behind either. The official single dropped at the end of February 1977 and was No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 in the first week of May.

In the very unlikely chance that you've never actually heard "Hotel California," it blends country and rock tones — a thing the Eagles were known for — but with an almost reggae or Latin feel, and it makes for a wildly catchy song. It begins with soft acoustic guitar picking before going into that reggae-like groove and moving into dynamic builds and crescendos that make the song hit powerfully without making it hard. One of the best moments comes when you're about 4 minutes into it.

The vocals immediately give way to the instrumentals in what amounts to an outro that runs over two minutes long. The guitar takes a solo that isn't just fantastic, but continues the driving builds of the early parts. You can almost hear the echoes of the chorus even though there are no vocals in the outro at all. This feels like the musical equivalent of the sun setting over the Hotel California, and it's exactly why this soft rock song has one of the best instrumental outros.

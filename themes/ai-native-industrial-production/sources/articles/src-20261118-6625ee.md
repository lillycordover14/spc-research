    Inspection report finds mold in Wilson Hall

    The inspection was conducted Aug. 25 after a UNC employee reported getting sick in the building.

Diana Lopez
, 
Escritora sénior
                 
             •  
            
November 18, 2026
 

 

Screengrab courtesy of the Sept. 2026 Wilson Hall Mold Inspection report by UNC-Chapel Hill Department of Environment, Health & Safety. 
 

Faculty members in Wilson Hall said they’ve known about mold in the building for years, but an Aug. 25 inspection formally confirmed what they’d long suspected.

The inspection was triggered by a request for an indoor air quality investigation an occupant submitted to UNC Environment, Health and Safety on Aug. 13 concerning room 130 of Wilson Hall.

The report documented mold growing on the ceilings, doors, pipe insulation, furniture and on HVAC units.

Screengrab courtesy of the Sept. 2026 Wilson Hall Mold Inspection report by UNC-Chapel Hill Department of Environment, Health & Safety.

The report flagged one unoccupied housekeeping office as so contaminated that it “needs to be cleaned before reoccupying.” 

Morgan Korzik, director of biology teaching labs, said he told EHS in August that the air quality was making him sick.

“My migraines were getting worse,” Korzik said. “I was starting to have rashes in addition to just the general allergy symptoms.” 

Korzik 
wrote
 in an email to The News & Observer that he had also started to develop postnasal drip, sneezing and coughing. 

He said he then noticed more mold in the building, searched online and saw that his symptoms matched those of mold exposure. He forwarded his findings to the University Employee Occupational Health Clinic. 

According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, exposure to damp and moldy environments can cause various health effects, or none at all. 

Screengrab courtesy of the Sept. 2026 Wilson Hall Mold Inspection report by UNC-Chapel Hill Department of Environment, Health & Safety.

For some people, mold can cause a stuffy nose, sore throat, coughing, wheezing, burning eyes or skin rash, the CDC website says.

“I mean, it’s not really that surprising to me that there’s a mold problem there,” Isabella Hazelton, a senior biology major, said. 

She said she also takes classes in Phillips Hall, which has its own 
asbestos
 problem.

Tyson Hedrick, a biology professor and associate chair for space and facilities in the Department of Biology, said Wilson Hall is essentially two different buildings. The original Wilson Hall was built in the 1940s, and then the Wilson Annex was built in the mid-1960s. The Annex is experiencing the majority of the mold issues.

The Wilson Annex has never been renovated, unlike the original building, which was renovated in 2005. Hedrick said the original building has received a modern HVAC system with central heating and air conditioning; the Annex has not. 

He said mold spores turn into mold outbreaks during certain times of the year, especially when the cooling system fails and leads to water accumulation. 

“A couple weeks ago, we came in, and there was like an inch of water on the floor,” John Bruno, a biology professor, said. “[A] pipe had burst.” 

Stuti Mehta, a senior biology and medical anthropology student, took classes in Wilson Hall during her first-year and sophomore years. 

She said she does not think she ever saw mold during her time there, but there was a smell and a feeling of dampness throughout the building. 

Bruno said that the University is aware of the issues — the department has brought deans, the provost and the chancellor through tours of the building to show them the problem. 

Screengrab courtesy of the Sept. 2026 Wilson Hall Mold Inspection report by UNC-Chapel Hill Department of Environment, Health & Safety.

A University spokesperson wrote in an email to The Daily Tar Heel that the instances of mold in the inspection report were largely driven by a roof leak in Wilson Hall
 
and that the roof was repaired by an outside contractor at a cost of roughly $46,000.

In addition, the concrete roof and chimney duct were repaired to stop further leaks. The work was finished on Sept. 10, 2026. 

The spokesperson wrote that all visible mold growth has been cleaned throughout the building using industry-standard methods and that the work is 100 percent complete. 

Affected ceiling tiles and the insulation above them will be replaced by the Facilities Internal Insulation Shop, and they will also be inspecting additional leaks as they go. The spokesperson wrote that any surface growth found is cleaned, and the work is 50 percent done and ongoing.

The spokesperson added that on Nov. 4, the University brought in a contractor to clean individual fan coil units that provide some airflow throughout the building. Once done, housekeeping will deep clean the entire facility, including floors and soft surfaces. 

Facilities staff are actively cleaning the window unit air conditioners and have already cleaned all the split-unit air conditioners, according to the spokesperson. Facilities Services has also installed corridor dehumidifiers and supplemental cooling in large classrooms over time to help control the humidity.

Screengrab courtesy of the Sept. 2026 Wilson Hall Mold Inspection report by UNC-Chapel Hill Department of Environment, Health & Safety.

As for possible renovation, the spokesperson wrote that the Wilson Hall Annex project has been identified as the University’s top project in its Six-Year Capital Improvement Plan. 

Finally, they wrote that UNC is evaluating additional building improvements to better manage the moisture and humidity to prevent future issues. 

“Unless I missed an email or something, we were never really notified about the molds in Wilson, and I wasn’t notified about them cleaning it up,” Hazelton said.

Hedrick said that with little to no cleaning or maintenance throughout the building, cooling system problems have only gotten worse over the years. It’s gotten to the extent, he said, that the department has been reassigning people to avoid giving them permanent office or research lab space in the building.

“I moved all my classes out of there. That’s how I dealt with it. I left the building,” Eric Hastie, an associate professor of biology, said. 

Hastie said he joined the UNC faculty in 2020, and mold has been present the entire time, only getting worse. He said not everyone has the option to move out, since many classrooms are still in use. The University has painted, installed dehumidifiers and tried to address the issue short of full renovation. 

“So either everything needs a really good clean,” Mehta said. “Or maybe another option is we actually listen to the students when they complain the first time, instead of waiting till people are getting sick, and then you’re like, ‘oh, maybe we should fix the molding.’”

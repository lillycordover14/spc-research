Larsen & Toubro Bets on Zorawar and AMCA

Larsen & Toubro is targeting Zorawar tank production by 2029 while awaiting the outcome of its bid to build India’s next-generation fighter aircraft under the AMCA programme

November 18, 2026

LUCKNOW. Larsen & Toubro (L&T) is pursuing two major Indian defence programmes, preparing its Zorawar light tank for user trials while awaiting a decision on its bid for the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA), the company said.

Satish Bharathan, L&T’s vice-president for Land Platforms, Equipment & Systems, said the company had submitted what it regarded as a competitive bid for the AMCA programme and was waiting for the government to announce the winner. The aircraft project is intended to develop an indigenous fifth-generation fighter.

The government has said it received bids from three shortlisted contenders, including a consortium involving L&T and a public-sector undertaking, and expected to announce a winner within a month. The selection will determine which team moves forward with development of prototypes.

L&T’s other near-term focus is the 25-tonne Zorawar, developed with the Defence Research and Development Organisation (DRDO). The light tank has completed developer trials that included tests in high-altitude areas and desert conditions, Bharathan said. It is now preparing to enter user trials, which are expected to last nine to 12 months.

During that period, the company, DRDO and the armed forces will assess whether the vehicle meets the military’s qualitative requirements. Bharathan said technical trade-offs were under discussion, and that production could begin by 2029 if the remaining steps proceed as planned. The target is conditional, not a confirmed production order or delivery date.

The schedule reflects the demands of testing a new armoured vehicle against operational requirements. In June, Army Chief General Upendra Dwivedi said the Zorawar’s induction was likely in 2028-29, subject to successful trials, user evaluation and production readiness. He said issues identified during testing were being addressed through design refinement. The Army’s timeline points to a period of evaluation before service entry, while L&T’s latest statement describes its hoped-for production start.

Zorawar is designed for terrain where heavier tanks can face constraints, particularly at high altitude. The Army’s interest in a light platform gained urgency after the 2020 India-China border standoff, which highlighted the operational demands of deploying armour in mountainous areas. Its expected role and performance will ultimately be judged through trials and military evaluation, rather than development claims alone.

The project also illustrates how defence manufacturing combines public research and private-sector production. DRDO and L&T have worked together on the tank, with L&T contributing its engineering and manufacturing capability. A successful transition from prototype to production would require not just a completed design, but the resolution of technical issues and readiness to build vehicles to the armed forces’ requirements.

For L&T, a win in the AMCA contest would extend its role from land systems into a high-profile aircraft programme. But the fighter bid remains undecided, and the company has made no claim that it has secured the contract. The government’s selection among three bidders is the immediate milestone.

The two programmes represent different stages of defence development. Zorawar is approaching user trials, where performance and compliance will be tested by the customer; AMCA is at the competition stage, with the winning bidder yet to be named. Both depend on government decisions, technical evaluation and sustained cooperation between industry and public institutions.

L&T is also seeking export opportunities for Indian-made systems. Bharathan said the company had supplied Pinaka rocket systems to a foreign customer and marine systems to Vietnam, and cited international interest in Indian defence products. He argued that India must be able to offer complete solutions, not only individual platforms or equipment, if it is to compete more effectively abroad.

For now, the company faces a twin test: demonstrate that Zorawar can meet the Army’s requirements and persuade the government that its AMCA proposal offers a competitive route to developing the aircraft. The timelines are promising, but trials and the procurement decision will determine what comes next.

Comic Book Review: ‘Daredevil: Born Again’

                                                            Written By 
Bryce Mwenda
, 
Staff Writer
                 
             •  
            
November 18, 2026
 

 

Photo by 
Wikimedia Commons
 
Cosplay of Matt Murdock aka Daredevil from Marvel Comics at San Diego Comic Con SDCC 2022.
 

“Daredevil: Born Again” is the most famous storyline of the blind lawyer’s entire bibliography. The Daredevil comics follow a man named Matt, who, through a freak accident in his youth, lost the ability to see but gained the powers of enhanced senses that he uses to fight the forces of evil in New York City.

This particular comic is written by Frank Miller and penciled by David Mazzucchelli, the same creative team that would later go on to do “Batman: Year One.” It was originally published in 1986 and spans issues 227 to 233 of “Daredevil.”

The overview of the plot is that through Daredevil’s former lover, Karen Page, Wilson Fisk, better known as Kingpin, has learned the secret identity of his mortal enemy, a lawyer named Matthew Murdock. He uses this information to dismantle Murdock’s life piece by piece and to tear him down by any means he can.

Mazzuchelli does what I consider to be his career best work as a penciller. Like “Batman: Year One,” he portrays the setting, this time New York City instead of Gotham City, with grit and grime.

What elevates this above “Year One” for me is that his style is more detailed and realistic overall, while also doing a better job at portraying the more expressionistic noir atmosphere that a comic like this requires. In particular, panels of Kingpin overlooking New York City are the highlight. The high-contrast lighting, coupled with cigar smoke that makes Kingpin look like a devil, gives great menace to the main antagonist.

The centerpiece of the comic is the characters, and in particular Matthew Murdock. While Kingpin quickly ruins Matt’s life, we see Murdock slowly degrade until he is nothing but a broken man with no home and a knife wound through his chest. For most of the narrative, Murdock does not engage in any superheroics and instead wallows in poverty and paranoia.

During the back half of the story, Matthew is able to regain his will to live and takes up the mantle of Daredevil again to show Kingpin that he can’t be permanently broken that easily.

The main way most of the characterization is done is through extensive narration boxes that are reminiscent of hard-boiled detective fiction writers like Raymond Chandler and Dashiell Hammett. While at times, the style can seem excessive due to Miller not letting Mazzuchelli’s art speak for itself, they do provide the comic with a unique voice.

An underrated aspect of the book is the arc of Ben Urich. He is a reporter for the Daily Bugle, a fictional newspaper in New York City, who throughout the course of the narrative goes through an arc from cowardice to bravery. Initially, he is reluctant to bring Kingpin’s crimes into the light when he is threatened by Kingpin’s violence and power. But once he realizes the damage ignoring Kingpin does to his community, he slowly gains a sense of bravery to report on Kingpin even if it means his life.

The story also ends with a miniature Captain America story nested in a Daredevil story with the final issue. Miller writes an excellent example of what defines many great Captain America stories: the distinction between the covert goals of the American government and the public values they claim to uphold.

An aspect of the comic that I like in isolation but doesn’t quite work for me within the context of the whole Daredevil series is Karen Page. The last time Karen appeared in the Daredevil comic, she was off to Hollywood to pursue her dreams as a star. Since then, we are told during the first issue that she became involved in sex work and got addicted to heroin. This seems like a pretty jarring character shift away from what was known about her previously.

Even though her characterization provides another vessel for the narrative’s theme of forgiveness and redemption, the way she is portrayed comes off as somewhat bleak. It is also part of a trend of Frank Miller’s of transforming a character who was previously a love interest of the protagonist into a sex worker that continues in “Batman: Year One” with Catwoman. It comes off as unnecessary and crude.

That being said, Daredevil: Born Again is a book that should be on any comic reader’s shelf for both the stellar art and writing that combine to make one of the peaks of the medium.

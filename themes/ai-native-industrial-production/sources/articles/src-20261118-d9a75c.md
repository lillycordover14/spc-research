    Column: Become the Woolf, not the wife

    “Woolf wouldn’t want us, women of the 21st century who have more rights and freedom than she and her peers ever did, to simply give up our own aspirations, livelihood and overall life just to be brought into a role that has never cared for our well-being.”

Columnist
                 
             •  
            
November 16, 2026
 

 

Natalie Jones
 

There has been an influx of “tradwives” on my social media feed, and it’s really starting to freak me out. 

With every swipe, I find a new young woman who is frightfully younger than me, and it seems that their entire life is solely about being a young wife, often claiming that they don’t need much of anything else — not education, not a backup plan, nothing. Their only desires are to rely on their husbands, give them kids and make a proper home. 

While it sounds very 1950s, this lifestyle is someone’s choice and her right to follow this path is an act of feminism. However, feminist or not, this influence is becoming somewhat predatory and misleading; it’s beginning to infect young minds that haven’t had a chance to develop who they are as a person without immediately tying themselves down to a life that is hard to undo. 

Reasonably, I am becoming scared for the young girls watching these videos because these tradwives are making it seem as if being only a wife and mother is easier than being an independent woman, when it’s not.

This trend often depicts their lives as a caregiver solely as an aesthetic, and this tactic is prone to luring in young women. In reality, these young women are not told or made aware of the type of abuses that generations of previous tradwives went through — financial, physical and reproductive abuse. They’re being shown a lie, and this lie is simply told because society has never prioritized women. 

Reverting to a time when women had essentially no autonomy is dangerous and I believe that if Virginia Woolf ⁠— one of the most progressive women of her time and an outspoken feminist whose literary works criticize the patriarchy and its oppression of women — could see this influx of tradwives in the 21st century, I think she would have a cow. 

I’d implore the women who are considering becoming a tradwife, or those being influenced by this movement to read some of Woolf’s work — I think she would want each of us to be warned about (and able to protect ourselves against) a falsely advertised lifestyle that has historically proven to be oppressive and, oftentimes, abusive. 

Woolf’s writing is unlike what was being published during her time, and no other known author was as outspoken about a woman’s freedom. Just from her feminist essay “A Room of One’s Own,” it is quite easy to theorize that she would not have wanted young women to be lured into such an arduous lifestyle. 

In “A Room of One’s Own,” Woolf makes it clear that a woman should have their own space and their own money if they ever wish to be independent, whether it be creatively or personally. She illustrates that many women do not end up like Shakespeare, popular and admired, but rather like his fictional sister, Judith Shakespeare, who was, unfortunately, forced to give up her own ambitions and become a wife, even though she was talented just like her brother, if not more so. While her brother, a man, is enjoying his success and livelihood, she is stuck at home, tucking in her kids and reduced to a predestined role. 

Near the end of the essay, Woolf writes “she lives in you and in me, and in many other women who are not here to-night, for they are washing up the dishes and putting the children to bed,” and this quote has stuck with me. It only served to remind me that I am living a life of education, freedom and autonomy that the women before me were never given. Every morning that I wake up for class and shove my books into my bag, I am reminded that a woman back then would have loved my life. In my reflection as I wash my face, I see generations of women who were never given the chance at a different life, at a different fate. I am everything that she, Judith Shakespeare, desired to be. I have access to education and opportunities that will guide me as I begin my own career, earn my own money and say that I have fulfilled my dreams — I can’t throw that away. 

And it’s not just about one’s career. It’s also about one’s livelihood and mental health at stake. In Woolf’s novel “To the Lighthouse,” the character Mrs. Ramsey is often depicted as a drained woman, reduced to her status as a wife and mother. She lives under her husband’s thumb and his incessant need to be reassured that he is great, intelligent and worthy. So she continuously reassures him. She does this until her death, and even after her death, it was never enough for him and probably not enough for herself. 

Women were, and still are, always expected to give up their own person just so their husbands can succeed — and many of these women ended up like Judith. Woolf wouldn’t want us, women of the 21st century who have more rights and freedom than she and her peers ever did, to simply give up our own aspirations, livelihood and overall life just to be brought into a role that has never cared for our well-being. 

I’m not saying that every woman should forgo marriage and kids if they truly want that life, but I am saying that young women should give themselves a chance at becoming their own person before falling victim to this influence. You can be more than someone’s mother and wife, and Virginia Woolf would want you to become everything that other women were deprived of.

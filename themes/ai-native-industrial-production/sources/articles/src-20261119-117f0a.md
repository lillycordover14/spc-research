Extremophile Fly That Breeds in Tar Found in the Bay Area&nbsp;&nbsp;Bay Nature

[page text]
It looks like just another muddy puddle off the side of the road.

Upon closer inspection, the substance in this puddle at Star Creek Ranch, a Land Trust of Santa Cruz County property, is black, slick, iridescent—and holding captive everything that falls in, including one unlucky harvestman. “It seems to me that whatever lands on top of it goes down and gets swallowed,” says Steve Root, the land trust’s easement coordinator. “I think this is really, really, really deep. I can’t imagine even attempting to excavate something out of there.” The substance in the puddle is asphalt, more commonly known as tar, which is hundreds-of-millions-of-years-old algae. It’s like a La Brea Tar Pit, except it’s just five feet across.

The view of the landscape from the entrance of Star Creek Ranch in Santa Cruz County.  
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

After a couple miles of moderate off-roading through the oak woodlands, Root pops open a wooden lid to reveal a tar pit in all its glory.  
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

Immediately, Root spots the fly. Which is something that might take a regular person a few minutes to do, given that it’s 3 millimeters long and the same dark color as the tar, but Root knows what he’s looking for, because he was the first one to find it here on a visit earlier this fall.

It is a petroleum fly, or tar fly (
Diasemocera petrolei
)—the only animal capable of surviving in tar, let alone making a home in it. The species is common in the La Brea pits, but until last year it was thought to live no farther north than Santa Barbara. Root’s sighting was only the second in the Bay Area and the first in Santa Cruz County. It seems these flies live way farther north than anyone realized.

Larvae have what’s called a respiratory siphon that lets them snorkel through the tar. This one is likely at its third larval instar, or stage, and the cooler weather means it’s moving slower than usual.  
Steve Root / Land Trust of Santa Cruz County

“The whole thing is pretty remarkable, that they’re able to live in the asphalt and survive,” says Giar-Ann Kung, an entomologist at the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County who made a “recon mission” to see the fly last year at Sargent Ranch in Santa Clara County after a colleague, Sean Campbell of the La Brea Tar Pits & Museum, saw potential tar seeps on satellite images. Kung also confirmed Root’s fly identification this fall, and collected samples for genetic sequencing.

“We’ve found them feeding on arthropods, birds, rodents,” she says.

Tar, also known as asphalt or pitch, overflows from this manmade concrete pit of unknown origin. No creature is safe from the sticky substance, except the fly.  
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

The thick tar glistens. Its gurgling bubbles can be heard from a couple of feet away. 
 
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

The Bay Area is hardly known for its petroleum resources, but tar seeps are around, subtly bubbling. Perhaps best known are the seeps in Santa Clara County at Sargent Ranch, where a small oil-drilling operation is being decommissioned. Sargent Ranch is part of a larger area known as Juristac, as it’s called by Amah Mutsun people, for whom 
it’s a special place
. The Amah Mutsun used the tar “in a lot of different ways as a sealant for basketry and sealing containers of different sorts,” says Maria Trogolo, the stewardship project manager for Juristac at the Peninsula Open Space Trust. There are seeps farther north as well. Theoretically, any of them could have tar flies. “We’ve gone to many different places looking for them,” Kung says—including the island of Trinidad, off the coast of Venezuela. That’s the farthest south the flies have been seen.

The Star Creek property, which is not open to the public, is a mix of oak woodlands, grasslands, and redwoods, and was protected to preserve wildlife connectivity between the Santa Cruz Mountains and the Gabilan Range, Root says. “This discovery just speaks to the multi-benefits of conservation.”

At the seep there, Root spots a larva wriggling across the slick surface. It moves somewhat like an inchworm. Root begins to poke around the thickened tar on the edges of the seep. There he finds a pupa the size of a sesame seed. This fly is an extremophile—meaning it’s adapted to a habitat humans consider extreme—and it’s weird at all its life stages.

These flies are just about 3mm, which means it helps to use a magnifying tool to help spot them. 
 
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

Root gets up close and personal with the tar seep. Goo-Gone might be his only rescue.  
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

Root spots a fly hanging around on his finger for several moments. Perhaps it’s moving slowly in the cold. Or maybe it senses the serious amount of tar on Root’s hands.  
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

The most normal it’ll ever be is as an egg. After hatching on the edge of a seep, a larva goes on a “perpetual cruise,” Root says, bent on consuming enough energy—from whatever falls victim to the tar—to move on to the next stage. Although tar moves through their digestive track, they don’t digest it, 
according to studies done on their microbiology.
 The larva transforms through three stages, or instars. “If you look at them closely, you can see that there’s actually asphalt within their digestive tract,” Kung says.

Eventually it burrows into the edges of hardened tar and enters its puparium, a rigid barrel-shaped case. Inside, it turns into a soup of proteins and enzymes and reforms itself as an adult fly. A puparium can be quite hard to break out of. Therefore the fly has adapted a toolkit: a membranous sac in its head (called a ptilinum) hydraulically expands like a balloon out of a slit, forcing the shell of the puparium open. All the flies in its clade bear a scar across their heads—that’s why they’re called the Schizophora, meaning “split-bearers.”

Adults are free to roam about the tar, with some caveats. Their little feet are able to walk across the tar with no problem, though scientists still have no clue how, according to Kung. But if their abdomen or wings touch the tar, they won’t survive. They might even become food for their larvae.

An unlucky harvestman walked its way into the tar. Its fate is to slowly sink in and possibly to get eaten by a tar fly.  
Daniella Garcia-Loos Almeida / Bay Nature

A pupa attached to the hardened tar, where its insides become soup and reform into an adult fly. 
Steve Root / Land Trust of Santa Cruz County

The petroleum fly isn’t considered threatened, but it is limited to tar seeps. “You must have this exact situation for the fly to exist at all,” Root says. “There is high-level conservation importance at a landscape scale for that reason, even though the species isn’t formally listed anywhere.” Personally, he says, “I’m a big fan of the underdog story, like the petroleum fly … I love that something even so small and often overlooked is here. It is another living species that deserves our care, and deserves a chance to thrive.”

Root and I drive to a second, larger tar seep. The tar is all around our feet, which begin to slowly sink in. “Anything that touches this stuff gets stuck, including you and I,” Root warns as he crouches down to the fly’s level. “Now I’m touching the tar. My wife’s not gonna let me sit on the couch.” Lately, he’s been using a lot of Goo-Gone.

    Column: The downfall of Nicki Minaj

November 19, 2026
 

 

Ella Hardin
 

Have you ever mourned the life of someone who is still alive? I do ⁠— especially when a person goes through dramatic personality and moral changes that make them unrecognizable. One prime example of this “loss” is the former queen of rap, Nicki Minaj. 

After decades of dominating the rap game, charts and pop culture, Minaj has apparently found a new genre to prioritize: politics. Typically, when artists shift into politics, they focus on advocacy, funding or protesting. Instead, Minaj took the extreme route and decided to join the dark side: the MAGA cult.

Had you asked me to describe Minaj five or so years ago, I’d say she was revolutionary or legendary. Now, all I can say is that she’s a hypocrite.

Back in 2018, Minaj publicly condemned Donald Trump’s “zero-tolerance” family separation policy. She wrote, “I can’t imagine the horror of being in a strange place and having my parents stripped away from me at the age of 5. This is so scary to me. Please stop this.” Eight years later, she refers to herself as the “number one fan” of the same man, acting as his lap dog for a few minutes of bad press. In fact, she rapped “Island girl, Donald Trump want me go home.” 

I’d say part of her support for the current president is a PR stunt. After the monstrosity of her last album “Pink Friday 2,” Minaj couldn’t pull herself out of the Khia Asylum, not even by her bootstraps. She went on to release what I personally think is the worst diss track of all time: “Big Foot,” and the music industry stopped taking her seriously around the same time. It was probably here when Minaj realized music might not keep her name afloat much longer. 

Not only does she lack a backbone, but also consistency in talent or politics. I find it weird that she switched up when her music stopped hitting like it used to. 

Fifteen years ago, paparazzi questioned Minaj about the state of gay rappers in the hip hop industry. When asked “what do you think it’s gonna take for us to finally get a gay rapper?” she responded, “I am a gay rapper, you got one!” 

Compare this to December of last year when during a Q&A with Erika Kirk at a Turning Point USA event, Minaj made the comment, “boys, be boys.” Not only does this mirror the sexist phrase “boys will be boys” which holds a history of excusing aggressive and abusive behavior as natural for the male gender ⁠— fitting, considering her husband is a sex offender ⁠— but she also diminishes a huge portion of her prior fan base: queer men. 

Throughout the early years of my adolescence when I navigated aspects of my personal identity, mainly my sexuality, Minaj was a huge ally for me and millions more. Her alter-egos such as Roman Zolanski were huge outlets for her own self expression which helped promote confidence and self acceptance within her fanbase. She always pushed for embracing new perspectives and images in her music. I felt seen by her carefree nature, as she presented herself the way she wished ⁠— something younger me wanted more than anything.

Now, seeing her support traditional masculinity while backing men who attack women or want to strip her own previous supporters of their civil rights darkens my childhood memories of her. Now that attention from conservatives is what she’s fighting for, playing the character of little gay Roman won’t bring in streams. She has alienated not only me, but the thousands of queer supporters that originally brought her to fame. 

Minaj might be able to throw Roman away in favor of another five minutes, but I’ll never be able to separate myself from queerness in the same way. Unlike her, being a queer man isn’t a persona; I unpausingly face the scrutiny men like Trump worsen with policy and remark. I recognize I’m not like most men; I speak in a higher tone and I’m not tough — that doesn’t make me less of a person. When Minaj neglects those who gave her a platform to stand on and throws away the rap scene’s hard-won respect, she rebukes her artistry and becomes another sheep in the MAGA herd.

Nicki, you sang it best in 2010: “You outta work, I know it’s tough / But enough is enough.”

The makers of the new Philadelphia edition of Monopoly noticed something unusual during the five months nominations were open to the public for people to submit their ideas of which Philly things should be included on the board. 
One agency kept coming up again and again — the 
Philadelphia Parking Authority
 (PPA) — but folks seemed to be submitting it as a joke, said Tim Barney, game manager for the Top Trumps company, which manufactures the board game.
“The PPA nominations started out like this: ‘Wouldn’t it be funny if the PPA was Free Parking because they’re not actually free parking?’” Barney said. “We started to get more and more nominations. We were like ‘Goodness, these guys are getting mentioned so much we 
should
 put them in.’ So we put them in.”
As the leading global licensee of Hasbro games, which owns Monopoly, 
Top Trumps
 must keep the four corner squares of the board — Go, Free Parking, Go to Jail, and Just Visiting — the same as they are in the original Monopoly, which was based on 
Atlantic City
 streets. So they couldn’t exactly grant Philadelphians’ PPA wishes (alas, this also means that “Go” has not been changed to “Go Birds”).
But what they did do was put the PPA in the transportation category, which also includes 
SEPTA
, William H. Gray III 30th Street Station, and the 
Schuylkill
 Banks Cable-Stayed Bridge. Those four things have replaced the railroads on the original board. That means 
Reading Terminal Market
 has not, as logic might suggest, replaced Reading Railroad, but don’t worry, it’s on there. 
Monopoly: Philadelphia Edition was unveiled Wednesday night at a release party at the Museum of the American Revolution that was attended by none other than Mr. Monopoly himself, or at least an actor in a mascot costume that looked like the ol’ cartoon moneybags.
The event was emceed by Barney and WMMR DJ and 76ers public address announcer Matt Cord. The G-Force Drumline and Steppers from the Highmark Mann Center kicked off the celebrations and a historical interpreter in colonial garb read a proclamation announcing the new Philly board. Representatives from the businesses and institutions on the board were then called to the stage one by one to accept oversized game cards and get their photo with Mr. Monopoly. 
Several folks from the PPA came in flashy Monopoly headbands made by Sue Cornell, director of public and employee engagement. She said it was “really exciting” to have the agency represented on the board. 
“The PPA is an icon in Philly. How could you have Philly Monopoly without the PPA?” she said. “We have to be included, whether you like us or not.”
Top Trumps announced its intention to create a Philly edition of the game in 
September 2025
 and left open its call for nominations from the public through the 
end of February
. 
While other Monopoly-esque games, like 
Philadelphia-Opoly,
 already exist, most aren’t officially licensed editions, Barney said. Top Trumps began its community Monopoly boards in 2019 and has made 
more than 50 already
. 
So how did Pittsburgh and Wichita get their editions before Philly? 
Barney said the team picks a mix of cities and small towns and tries not to release multiple games in a region or state consecutively, to allow some breathing room. 
“Other kinds of factors that we want to consider are major historical anniversaries, like the 250th this year,” he said. 
While public nominations for landmarks, businesses, and institutions are taken into consideration, it’s not a voting system. Many other factors go into who gets a spot, including longevity, credibility, reviews, and community engagement, Barney said. 
“Finally, just that they are excited about this. It’s a super-fun project and this is a great opportunity to be immortalized in a civic artifact,” he said.
But businesses and institutions do pay to be a part of the game, too. Barney declined to discuss the cost of contracts, noting that they are on a “case-by-case” basis and all contain nondisclosure agreements.
“It’s not as simple as who can or cannot buy their way into the game,” he said. “Money is not the determining factor. They first have to be authentic.”
The team also tries to diversify the types of things included to cover a wide range of categories, from cultural institutions to food.
It’s that last category, which takes up the three orange property squares, that may be the most contentious among locals. Those squares include the Philly Pretzel Factory, Rita’s Water Ice, and Geno’s Steaks. 
Geno’s is often considered a cheesesteak tourist trap by locals, many of whom have not forgotten the 
controversial sign
 in the shop window from 2006 to 2016 that read: “This is America, when ordering, please speak English.”
So why was it included?
“I say why not Geno’s? They seemed really excited to be a part of the game, but we knew we couldn’t represent every single cheesesteak place,” Barney said. “At the end of the day, we had to choose one, and it’s a challenging decision. … Geno’s felt like the right pick.” 
Taking the place of the public utilities spaces are two media organizations, The Inquirer (
well, shucks!
) and WMMR (
great day in the morning!
).
“You both have a really strong voice in your community and you were both nominated quite a lot,” Barney said. 
The two high-value dark blue properties are now Reading Terminal Market and the Schuylkill Banks Boardwalk, and the three green properties go to higher education institutions: 
Temple University
, La Salle University, and 
Drexel University
. 
Noticeably absent: the 
University of Pennsylvania
.
“There is a small number of selections for any given sector, at most we only feature three or four universities, and Temple, La Salle, and Drexel were really excited to be involved in the game,” Barney said. 
Enthusiasm and limited space are also why you’ll see the 
Franklin Institute
, the Weitzman National Museum of American Jewish History, and the Museum of the American Revolution in the light blue spaces, but not the 
Philadelphia Museum of Art
, according to Barney.
Chinatown
, Boathouse Row, and the South 9th Street Italian Market Festival are on the magenta spaces; the company headquarters of Urban Outfitters, Aramark, and 
Gopuff
 are the red squares; the Highmark Mann, Philadelphia sports complex, and Dwight D Hotel are the yellow properties; and Independence Hall and Elfreth’s Alley get the brown spaces. 
Squares to pay your loan and make a deposit to American Heritage Credit Union replace the income and luxury taxes, and the credit union’s name is on  the money, too.
Player tokens like the top hat and Scottie dog remain the same, but the Community Chest and Chance cards are personalized to Philly and include references to various nonprofits like Sharing Excess, Joe Frazier’s Gym & Community Center, 
Mural Arts
 Philadelphia, and Habitat for Humanity, Barney said.
The names of the Free Library and Victory Brewing appear on Community Chest spaces, obviously because both played a role in helping you win second prize in that beauty contest. 
Monop
oly: Philadelphia Edition retails for $39.99 and is available at local retailers and online at Top Trumps, Barnes and Noble, and Amazon.

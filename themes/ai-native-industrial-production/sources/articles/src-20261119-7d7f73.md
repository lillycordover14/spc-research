Raytheon taps Ursa Major, Avio USA as second source for Tomahawk solid rocket motors&nbsp;&nbsp;Breaking Defense

[page text]
In this U.S. Navy released handout,  Arleigh Burke-class guided-missile destroyer USS Thomas Hudner (DDG 116) fires a Tomahawk land attack missile in support of Operation Epic Fury, on March 1, 2026 at Sea. (Photo by U.S. Navy via Getty Images)						

WASHINGTON — Startup Ursa Major and the American arm of the Italian firm Avio have been tapped to jointly produce a solid rocket motor for the Navy’s Tomahawk cruise missile, adding a second supply source as the Pentagon seeks to ramp up munitions production.

RTX’s Raytheon, which makes the Tomahawk, selected the two companies to be “strategic partners” on the “multi-year program” for the solid rocket motor, according to a 
Wednesday press release
. The statement did not, however, disclose specific timelines or dollar values.

“Tomahawk is a critical capability for the warfighter, and meeting growing demand requires more capacity and a stronger, more resilient supply chain,” Ursa Major President 
Jason Meredith
, said in the release. “Our partnership with Avio USA brings together complementary capabilities to create additional reliable sources of solid rocket motors for Raytheon and the U.S. Navy.”

The Tomahawk is powered by a solid rocket motor for initial stages of flight, which is then jettisoned as a turbofan engine carries the weapon to its target. The missile can fly low to avoid radar and strike with precision.

Avio USA CEO James Syring said in the release that his company is “proud to have been selected alongside Ursa Major for this important work” and said the partnership is “a significant step forward” for the company’s 
forthcoming production plant
 in southern Virginia.  

Both firms are somewhat recent entrants to the American market. Ursa Major, founded in 2015, has since established itself as a viable military supplier, 
winning Pentagon contracts
 for weapons like missiles. And while Avio is a long-established European vendor, the company opened its US office in 2022, and has since 
won its own
 deals for American missile production.

Solid rocket motors are a key propulsion source for missiles, and 
demand has climbed
 in recent years amid the war in Ukraine and conflict in the Middle East. The wide need for the motors has helped foster the new entrants of companies like Ursa Major and Avio USA, a requirement that has only grown as the Trump administration seeks to greatly expand production of key munitions.

In August, the Navy 
awarded
 Raytheon a massive, $22.9 billion multi-year deal to scale up output of the Tomahawk to 1,000 rounds annually. The weapon has been used extensively in the war against Iran, to the point of alarming Pentagon planners over concerns of dwindling stockpiles, the Washington Post 
previously reported
. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao 
testified
 before the Senate on Tuesday that the Navy has had to resort to using expired copies of the missile in combat.

The Pentagon’s ambitious munitions production plans still have to clear a final hurdle: funding. The Trump administration planned its fiscal 2027 budget around a $1.1 trillion base budget while banking on a $350 billion reconciliation bill, the latter of which has been flatly rejected by top congressional Republicans — though outgoing House Armed Services Chairman Mike Rogers has 
floated
 a slimmed down $100 billion version. 

Nevertheless, Senate appropriators recently 
unveiled
 their own proposed budget, which folds in reconciliation priorities to fund programs like Tomahawk.

Inside "Purity Spiral": SHY, LOW dissect their latest instrumental post-metal album&nbsp;&nbsp;IDIOTEQ.com

[page text]
Much of the anger and frustration of living in America in 2026 went into the seven instrumental songs on “
Purity Spiral
“, the new album from Richmond, Virginia post-metal quintet 
Shy
, 
Low
. Released on October 16th via 
Pelagic
 
Records
, the record finds the band digging further into the heavier side of their music, with ideas about
 religious fanaticism, capitalist greed, technological dependency and the gradual erosion of empathy 
behind several of the compositions.

Five years on from “Snake Behind the Sun”, 
Shy
, 
Low
 have also changed quite a bit about the way they write their songs. The three-year writing process brought guitarist Kai Zamora and drummer Dylan Partridge into the band’s songwriting sessions alongside Zak Bryant, Gregg Peterson and Drew Storcks, opening up some interesting possibilities with three guitars and a different approach to rhythm.

There’s plenty of room for the band’s post-rock sensibilities
 across the new album, with acoustic guitars, recurring melodies and entire sections arranged around synthesisers. At the heavier end, the guys draw inspiration from the likes of Neurosis and The Secret, with bassist Drew Storcks considering the title track the heaviest thing they’ve written to date.

Producer Greg Thomas played a big part in getting these songs finished. Some had gone through years of revisions, others were still incomplete when the band entered the studio, and a few underwent fairly radical changes during recording.

<a href="https://shylowmusic.bandcamp.com/album/purity-spiral" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">Purity Spiral by Shy, Low</a>

The title itself refers to a social phenomenon in which people within a group become progressively more extreme in their attempts to demonstrate their moral or ideological purity. Shy, Low see plenty of that happening around them today, extending their criticism to the ways technology and our obsession with perpetual growth affect our relationships with other people.

Today, we’re handing things over to Drew Storcks for an extensive track-by-track breakdown of the entire album. There’s some great stuff in his commentary about unlikely musical influences, accidental discoveries in the rehearsal room, disagreements over songwriting, the recurring imagery in the album’s artwork and the memory of a friend whose story has a special place on the record.

Drew begins with “Palmistry”, the third track on the album and the first song Shy, Low wrote together in their current formation. Put the record on and read along.

SHY, LOW “Purity Spiral” track by track, written by Drew Storcks

Palmistry

While Palmistry isn’t the first song on the album, I’m going to start with it because it was the first song we wrote together with our current line-up. In late 2021, Kai showed us a long catalog of riffs he had recorded on his phone over the years. Some were quick little guitar lines, others were more fleshed out ideas with Dylan playing on drums. (They also play and write together in a project called LOSST). All of it was rad, and an early version of the guitar part that opens “Palmistry” was in there. I remember everyone taking a liking to it right off the bat, and I felt like it had this almost flamenco-meets-Brent Hinds vibe to it that was quite different from anything Zak, Gregg or Myself had written for the previous album.

A year or so later, the current line-up had its first weekend-writing session for the album. Zak, Gary (Gregg) and I had never truly written with Dylan or Kai at that point; we’d only practiced and played material from “Snake…” together. Zak and I had already presented a few song ideas we had via e-mail – all of which featured a mostly complete skeleton. I anticipated we might utilize our limited time over the weekend to learn and tweak one of those. Instead, something much weirder happened…Dylan (drums) started warming up on Day 1 with a rudiment for about 15 minutes.

The rest of us were plugging in our pedalboards, tuning up our guitars…Zak eventually started doing a right hand warm up as well, which was this little chug pattern on the lowest string. I can remember hearing the combination of their warm-ups, and my inner monologue going “I know these guys are both just warming up right now, but whatever’s happening right now sounds like Dawn of Midi meets Edge of Seventeen by Stevie Knicks, and it’s rad.”

Seconds later, I remembered Kai’s iPhone riff from the year prior, and I had a hunch that it might fit perfectly as an accompanying layer. We started jamming on it, and in a few hours we had an entire first draft of the song written. I knew that Kai and Dylan’s involvement was going to elevate our songwriting abilities, I just didn’t expect it to take shape so fast. That day gave us a big ol’ glimmer of hope for the band’s future. If Dylan had chosen a different warm up to play that day, I don’t think “Palmistry” would’ve come to be.

The Bloat

I believe this was the second song we worked on with the new line-up. Zak showed up with the intro riff and the noodly guitar part you hear 1:04. We were sold on those parts right away, and like Palmistry, the first draft of a full song came together pretty quickly. Over the course of two years, we did perform a lot of surgery on it though.

Sometimes when you’re in the practice space jamming, everything’s sounding cool, but then you get home, listen to the demos, and collectively agree that it ain’t sittin’ right. Our original version had this waltzy MOOG-oriented bridge. That got completely scrapped and re-written into the dueling guitar bridge you hear now. The outro to “Greenwood” was originally used as the Bloat’s outro – though we felt it served Greenwood better.

Our producer Greg Thomas suggested even more surgery once we got into the studio…mostly so it felt short, sweet and aggressive all the way through. Additional suggestions included shaving off about 2 minutes of time from the song, a re-writing of bass-lines, and a new arrangement built around the hook of the chorus. These suggested changes were a bit shocking at first to some of us, but ultimately they were the right call – the song now has way more impact, and tension than the previous versions did.

The working title was called “The Beef,” after a confusing conversation Kai and I had at Can Can in Richmond, VA about their wonderful Beef Tartare. Give them your money in exchange for a succulent French meal. Dylan Partridge (drums) named all of the song titles for this album, and “The Bloat” was the first one he came up with. It’s a metaphor for the capitalist over-consumption we’ve fallen victim to, how we’re treated like cattle by our leaders, and how the societal addiction to consumption (of digital information) is creating an environmental nightmare.

Greenwood

The title “Greenwood” has a double meaning that we felt embodied the feeling of the track and its place on the record. The song shares specific melodic DNA with Burning Fields (you can hear the final riff of Greenwood played on the fading piano at the end of Burning Fields) and the two song titles collectively reference the idea of controlled burning and destruction in order to make way for new, healthy growth—green wood. Much of this record was a way for us to process the anger and frustration of being an American, and it reflects how it feels to watch our neighbors abandon human decency. Violence sometimes feels like the only solution to the barbarism, and this song is our way of coming to terms with doing whatever necessary to ensure our future generations are safer than we are now.

Kai and Dylan wrote the foundations of this song sometime in the 2010’s and initially had plans to have their friend Katie Greenwood Ross sing on it with their other band. By their account, she was an amazing and prolific artist, musician, and maker of all things. We had even talked about having her sing on a couple songs on this record in the early writing stages. She sadly passed away in 2025, and so this song is also in tribute to her legacy, and a reminder to make magic whenever possible, because the world is too bleak without it.

A Charging Brazen Bull

This was the second track Dylan had titled. Building off the concept behind The Bloat, he wanted to speak on facing the fact that solving our modern societal problems may one day involve nearly barbaric means.

There was a discussion he had with me about blending the mental image of the Greek torture device (The Brazen Bull), along with Wall Street’s charging bull statue. Given that most of our societal hazards are caused by a blend of a hoarding of wealth and religious zeal, the image of the golden bull outside the stock market being turned to an idol inside of which people are burned alive seemed pretty apt for what America has become.

It became a recurring visual motif in our music video for the song “Purity Spiral,” and eventually some of the artwork you see in the album packaging. This one also underwent a series of changes in the studio. For our previous effort, “Snake Behind The Sun,” we had those songs demoed out exactly how we wanted them to be recorded, and very, very few parts were changed in the studio. This time around, we really wanted a producer’s input in taking the songs to the finish line. With that, a fair amount of this song was re-written in the studio under Greg’s guidance and support.

Grace

Zak had come up with the acoustic intro/“verse” somewhat early on in our 3-year writing process, and it laid dormant in our google drive “riff vault” folder for quite a while. Just days before entering the studio, we revisited the idea and wrote the second half of the track in the room together, again relatively quickly. I’ve always loved Gary (Gregg’s) lead track over the ending of this song.

It’s Korn-like and thus, very sick. I’m very excited to play this one live when the time finally comes. Originally, “The Bloat” was going to be the first track on the record, but we decided last-minute that Grace might be a more unique opener. Greg Thomas dug the sound of the acoustic guitar layers Zak had recorded on the demo so much, that we just decided to use those exact tracks in the final mix. (Instead of re-recording it through the studio’s gear chain).

Burning Fields

Out all of the songs on the album, this one probably underwent the most transformation before we hit the studio. Zak was at the helm of this one, and while the synth layers you hear now were in the original version, that original version was a lot more guitar-focused. The intro had a cool Isis (the band) vibe to it, followed by a bigger anthemic aesthetic.

There was quite a bit of head butting on this song-After Zak and I each made about 5 versions of the track, I believe Gary (Gregg) had suggested we revamp into something more synth-centric. Zak ran with that, and it quickly became the version you hear now. In the studio, I remember Greg Thomas strongly advocating for this song to be drum-less, and I think we almost went down that road. In the end, Greg just couldn’t resist the sweet sensual whisper of Dylan’s rhythmic cadence, so the drum track remained.

Purity Spiral

By far the heaviest song we’ve ever written. Like Palmistry, it sort of came together in a weird way. Zak had e-mailed us some early versions of the first two guitar parts. I typically gravitate toward riffs like this, so immediately upon hearing it, I was sold and eager to take a crack at adding more to it. In trying to learn the song by ear, I learned all of his guitar parts in the wrong tuning, and was then inspired to write a draft of the remainder of the song, which partially included some riffs I had kicking around from 2016.

This song was close to not making it on our final list of songs…a few days before leaving, we rediscovered it in our google drive. It was unfinished, and remained that way until we started recording it in the studio. We had never played it as a band prior to entering the studio, so it was a funny experience trying When Greg Thomas first heard the track and spent some time with it, he basically was like, this song is cool, but think you guys need to come up with something really disgustingly heavy for the end of this. Each of you should go find a corner in the studio, and try to write something over the next hour. May the best idea win. We wanted to write something borderline moshy that still sounded Neurosis or The Secret-adjacent. We now jokingly call that ending the “The Fight Riff,” and it’s a true fact that John Mayer played it on The Chappelle Show once.

We decided to title the record after this track because we felt that this song best embodied what the record as a whole is supposed to feel like. It’s tumultuous, and roiling, and is an endless building of tension, and reflects the feeling of being an American in 2026. It’s constant disbelief that it could possibly get any worse than this. And then it does. The phrase “purity spiral” refers to the social phenomenon of a heightening of moral zealotry within a closed group of people, wherein a feedback loop is created by each person trying to out-do each other’s piety, thereby sacrificing their decency. It’s exactly what we’re seeing in our politicians and neighbors as Americans: people who have become so blind in their faith that they lose the plot and become exactly what their own religion is supposed to protect against. A Christian who hoards wealth, practices idolatry, and demonizes empathy is fundamentally not a Christian.

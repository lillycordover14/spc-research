The Hill by Harriet Clark review – distinctive debut of growing up with a mother in prison
This unusual portrait of a young girl whose prison visits are interweaved with her grandmother’s war-weary reminiscences rewards attention
Sarah Moss
Thu 19 Nov 2026 02.00 EST
Last modified on Thu 19 Nov 2026 03.05 EST
Share
Prefer the Guardian on Google
A
merican author Harriet Clark’s first novel, partly inspired by her own life, is narrated by a girl whose mother is in prison. Suzanna is being raised by grandparents while her mother serves a life sentence for driving the getaway car for a botched bank robbery intended to fund revolutionary political activism. Although Suzanna refuses the narrative, in some ways this is a coming-of-age story, but with a protagonist whose overwhelming need is to go nowhere and change nothing.
The Hill is shaped by the tension between the novel’s need for a plot and Suzanna’s rejection of her own role in the Bildungsroman. Prison visits structure Suzanna’s life, their rituals and repetitions at once marking and resisting the passage of time. At first, her grandfather drives her from their shabby urban apartment to the hilltop prison and escorts her to the entrance to the children’s centre, where on a good day the prisoners are allowed to play with their children under close surveillance. Later, the grandfather dies, activities in the centre are restricted and Suzanna’s visits take increasingly constrained forms. Abjuring ordinary teenage activities, she takes the train towards the hill every Saturday, submits to searches, and follows rules about how to move and what and whom she may not touch.
Time passes most visibly in Suzanna’s grandmother’s body, the passage towards death harder to deny than the passage towards adulthood. The grandmother, herself a sometime communist with vivid memories of a trip to Russia as a young mother, lives surrounded by friends who are similarly jaded – war-weary Jewish radical thinkers in old age, looking back together on children mostly estranged, dead or far away; husbands who in various ways did not stay the course; wars and regimes more or less survived. Whatever care they receive comes from each other and rarely with good grace. The liveliest conversations – often darkly funny – are about what, if anything, daughters owe to not-good-enough mothers.
Read this novel playfully, slowly, with an open mind
Perhaps surprisingly, this isn’t a question that preoccupies Suzanna, who has difficulty acknowledging her own agency. Practically competent as her situation requires her to be, she does not want to ascend the ladder of secondary education or break free from a mother who has no freedom. In some ways, the novel’s tight spatial and temporal circles reminded me of Solvej Balle’s On the Calculation of Volume, in which the same day replays itself for the same people in the same place. Suzanna, whose origin story is abandonment, whose estrangement from her mother runs wide and deep, is in many ways arrested, willing immobility in a small world whose narrowness is no protection from time.
This is, broadly, realist fiction. The material of low-budget, low-energy domesticity is vivid: smelly bedding, mucky floors, stale food. Avoiding sensationalism, Clark writes more about the low-key, cumulative humiliations and discomforts of incarceration than the horror, which is more effective for its shadowiness. A woman newly released from months of strip-lit solitary confinement keeps asking “what time is this”: the worst punishment seems to be the physical manifestation of the timelessness Suzanna craves.
Almost all the physical journeys in The Hill are between the apartment and the prison, mostly by train. The train is another enclosed, shared space, the time on it both liminal and definitive. This feels like a closed-room novel, but it isn’t, or not much more than most novels are. We move from one confinement to another, and back.
It took me a few chapters to trust that the novel was teaching me how to read it. There is much conversation, or at least many interweaving monologues from hard-bitten old women, many of whom speak elliptically, leaving Suzanna and the reader to work out the relevance or significance of half-anecdotes from decades gone by. No one goes anywhere or does anything new, and a lot of the talk is rambling. But it coalesces round incidents and themes, and moments of sharp comedy emerge, as when the grandmother remembers her own mother. “There was nothing like this ridiculously elegant woman weeping into a handkerchief to make life in Stalin’s starving land seem both manageable and promising.” (Not spoiler: wrong.)
The wildness, the light in the cracks, comes from animals that verge on the magical: semi-feral cats enacting a child’s repressed rage, guide dogs in training being fiercely loved in prison and, finally, magnificently, a parrot that can’t fly pacing the aisle of an aeroplane beside a mother in shackles. Read this novel playfully, slowly, with an open mind. It rewards attention.

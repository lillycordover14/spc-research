Tulip Siddiq says she is considering legal action against former Bangladesh leader
Former minister, who was sentenced to prison in Bangladesh in her absence, says she was victim of ‘malicious prosecution’
Alexandra Topping
 Political correspondent 
Thu 19 Nov 2026 01.00 EST
Last modified on Thu 19 Nov 2026 08.21 EST

The former minister Tulip Siddiq has said she is considering taking legal action against the former interim leader of Bangladesh over corruption allegations made by his administration that led her to resign from the UK government last year.
The MP for Hampstead and Highgate, whose aunt 
Sheikh Hasina
 was put on trial in absentia over crimes against humanity during her 15 years as prime minister of Bangladesh, was accused of benefiting from the former regime by the authorities in the Bangladeshi capital, Dhaka.
Siddiq was 
sentenced to two years in prison in Bangladesh
 in December last year after being put on trial in her absence, alongside 16 other people, over corruption allegations. She was found guilty of influencing her aunt to secure a plot of land for her family in the outskirts of Dhaka. Siddiq, who is based in London and strongly denied the charges, is unlikely to ever serve the sentence.
In an interview with the Guardian in summer 2025, 
Siddiq called the charges against her “completely absurd
”, saying she was “collateral damage” in a longstanding feud between her aunt and Bangladesh’s former interim leader Muhammad Yunus.
Speaking to Politics Unleashed – a new podcast hosted by Guto Harri and David Davis – Siddiq said she had been the victim of a “malicious prosecution”. She added that she was speaking with a reputable lawyer who had told her she had a case to sue Yunus, the Nobel peace prize-winning economist who led the interim government in Bangladesh from 2024 to 2026.
“It was essentially malicious prosecution,” she said, adding that she was exploring options with lawyers and “considering” legal action.
Yunus, approached for a response through two close associates, declined to comment.
Siddiq said she had repeatedly tried to get details about what she was accused of, but the Bangladesh government had not replied to letters from her lawyers.
She detailed sending a handwritten letter to Yunus, who was staying at the Dorchester in London. “He refused to meet with me. I tried repeatedly to see him face to face,” she said, insisting she had not been stressed by the accusations and subsequent trial because she knew she was innocent. “I was more baffled by the whole thing than anything else,” she said.
Speaking about the impact of the trial, she compared the experience to being in a “Kafkaesque nightmare”, adding: “I had no idea what I was being convicted for. The only way I found out I had been convicted is when I woke up in the morning and saw an alert from Sky News.”
Siddiq also revealed she had received support from the former prime minister Keir Starmer during the controversy. She said he sent her a message when he was in the US with Donald Trump after she had received a security threat. “It kind of matched with the timing of when he was just about to see Trump,” she said.

But when Starmer was asked repeatedly about Siddiq, she decided she had to resign. She said: “I had been part of the project to get Keir into No 10. I had supported him from the beginning … and then here I am, being the obstacle.”
The Starmer loyalist said the toppling of the former prime minister was “very painful”, but praised his successor, Andy Burnham.
“At the end of the day, what I want is a Labour government, another Labour government,” she said. “If Andy is going to deliver that, then I have to put my sadness aside and focus on the fact that we have a Labour government that’s doing well in the polls.”

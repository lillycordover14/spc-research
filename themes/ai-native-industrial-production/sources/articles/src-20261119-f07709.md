3. Castings are a chokepoint for the Navy’s $76.6 billion submarine orders, though its August review found capacity often underused.

Hadrian acquired Kutztown Foundry & Machine in eastern Pennsylvania and said Monday it will invest $300 million to automate the plant and add new production lines for the naval-grade steel castings used in 
U.S. Navy submarines

Berks County property records show the advanced-manufacturing company paid $9.4 million for the 24-acre site, a fraction of the planned overhaul. Renamed Factory 6, the more than 150-year-old foundry is Hadrian’s first acquisition and its entry into castings and forgings, long a chokepoint in submarine production.

What Hadrian Is Building at Kutztown

The foundry has operated since the 1800s and previously belonged to McConway & Torley of Pittsburgh. Hadrian said the $300 million will pay for automation software, robotics, and construction of what it calls a Factory of the Future, with work starting in January.

Kutztown employs 49 people. Hadrian said the project will support hundreds of manufacturing jobs, with hundreds more at suppliers, and that the plant is already pouring steel for the Navy. New hires train on the company’s Opus software, which Hadrian says can reach production in weeks.

The purchase follows the March opening of Hadrian’s $2.4 billion submarine-parts plant in Alabama, backed by $900 million in Navy funding. Hadrian had said then that one of three planned maritime sites would be a dedicated foundry for castings and forgings. Power said the Kutztown plant is already pouring steel for the Navy.

Where the Submarine Bottleneck Really Sits

Power has framed castings as the weak point in submarine output. “There are only really four or five small businesses left that can do this sort of stuff, and they’re all bottlenecked,” he told reporters.

The U.S. lost 1,600 foundries and more than 4,400 metal-casting operations between 1984 and 2018, displacing 324,000 workers, according to the National Defense Industrial Association.

The Navy awarded 
General Dynamics
 Electric Boat and HII $76.6 billion in July for nine Virginia-class attack boats and five Columbia-class ballistic-missile submarines, its largest submarine deal and one that followed nearly three years of delay. Castings and forgings, the heavy metal components shaped for a hull, carry long lead times that slow those boats.

The Navy’s own account is more measured. A Submarine Industrial Base assessment released in August found that qualified capacity often already exists and that the harder problem is using it consistently. The program said one foundry-modernization effort cut lead times 20% and raised casting capacity 50%.

Hadrian is not alone. Anduril 
said last month it will build Arsenal-2
, a $3.7 billion plant in Baltimore aimed at large submarine components. Factory 6 is a different wager. “Reindustrializing America isn’t just building new factories,” Power said. “It’s also about investing in the ones that made it through the years of offshoring.”

What’s Next:
 Hadrian said construction of new plants at Kutztown will begin in January and continue for about two years. Its Alabama factory is projected to reach full-rate production within 18 to 24 months of opening in March.

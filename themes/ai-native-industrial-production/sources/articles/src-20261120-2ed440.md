Analysis of a 360-year-old painting of a Manx martyr has found a second figure hidden beneath the surface.
The portrait of William Christian, known as IIliam Dhone, underwent infrared imaging as part of a major conservation project that began in 2023.
Manx National Heritage (MNH) conservator Christopher Weeks said the second figure was dressed in the style of Oliver Cromwell's parliamentary forces and was likely to have been concealed after the restoration of the monarchy in 1660.
Funding has now been secured for further detailed technical analysis, conservation and restoration work by a specialist firm in Cambridge.
It is hoped the work may also reveal the name of the artist responsible for the painting, which was acquired from Workington Hall by MNH in the 1950s and put on display in the Manx Museum.
MNH
The painting has been on display at the Manx Museum since it was acquired for the national collections in the 1950s
A controversial character in Manx history, Dhone led an uprising against the Isle of Man's royalist rulers in 1651 to negotiate a surrender with the parliamentarians near the end of the English Civil War.
The move was considered by some as necessary in order to protect the island's traditional rights, but others considered it a betrayal of the Countess of Derby.
After royalist rule was restored under Charles II, Dhone was shot for treason at Hango Hill in Castletown in 1663.
The second figure was discovered when art analysis specialists Tager Stonor Richardson used infrared reflectography to see beneath surface layers of paint and varnish.
MNH/Tager Stonor Richardson
Analysis of the portrait was carried out as part of a major conservation project
Weeks said the second man was depicted "wearing a flamboyant hat and carrying both a sword and a musket in the style of Oliver Cromwell's Parliamentary forces".
"Most intriguingly, he appears to be handing a rolled document to Illiam Dhone," he said, "so the scene may represent the transfer of authority for the Isle of Man to Illiam Dhone by the parliamentary forces during his period as governor in 1656."
The "dramatic alteration" is likely to have taken place after the restoration of the monarchy four years later "when associations with parliamentary rule had become politically sensitive", he said.
The project was an "exciting opportunity to deepen our understanding of one of the Isle of Man's most significant historical figures" and would "ensure the portrait is conserved and preserved for future generations to enjoy", Weeks added.
Further research, being carried out by the Hamilton Kerr Institute, at the University of Cambridge, is expected to take six months, before the portrait will return to the island and be put back on display.

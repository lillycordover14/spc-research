ESPN’s top recruits
 from the Class of 2027, signed with UConn this week, she would be the only prospect from her class to join the Huskies before the spring signing period ended on Wednesday.

Coach
 Geno Auriemma was asked T
hursday if UConn still had its “cachet” in the evolving recruiting game.

“Yeah, we just signed the number 9 player in the country,” he said. “We don’t have cachet with guys who are getting a million dollars to go play someplace. They’re getting the ‘cash-ay.’ I just don’t see the value of paying a high school kid a million dollars that hasn’t dribbled the ball one time in college. I don’t get the idea of paying a kid a million dollars to come out of high school, and that’s more than 90 percent of the best players in the worl
d in the WNBA.

“So as long as that’s the case, we’re going to get the guys we’re going to get, but we’re not going to get the guys we’re not supposed to get.”

UConn women make signing of top-10 recruit from Michigan official

Auriemma, 72, and his staff landed a string of No.1 recruits in Chrstyn Williams,
 Paige Bueckers,
 Azzi Fudd and Sarah Strong among them in the early 2020s, when the potential for NIL income UConn represented was a major recruiting weapon on its own. Today, there is revenue-sharing, direct pay, as well as NIL in the equation.

UConn had most of the top 2027 prospects in for official visits this fall. Guard Kaleena Smith, consensus No.1, visited Storrs, but has since narrowed her finalists to USC, UCLA and Arizona State. Ivanna Wilson-Manyacka visited, but committed to UCLA. Haylen Ayers picked Vanderbilt, coached by former UConn player and assistant Shea Ralph, this week. Eve Long, a player UConn pursued last March, signed with Notre Dame.
 

“I don’t have that conversation (about money) with the parents,” Auriemma said. “We have an agent that talks to their agent. Because the kids and the parents lie to you. ‘It’s not about the money, it’s about academics, it’s about finding the right fit, the right experience.’ No it isn’t. Just say it’s about the money and we can all live happily ever after.”

Auriemma has gone in
 more heavily for overseas players i
n recent years, but this week Kinga Josepovits, a center from Hungary who visited UConn during a swing of U.S. schools, chose Duke.

These are signs of the times, a dollar sign, yes, and also a sign that increased parity in the college game puts more schools in play for the elite talent. Among ESPN’s top 20, there were 11 different schools getting at least one. The expanded Big Ten, which began the season with nine ranked teams, and the ascent of Vanderbilt among the SEC’s contenders, has spread the talent, as well-resourced power conference schools press their advantages.

“That’s a part of it, for sure,” Auriemma said. “There’s more schools that are investing more resources and the players are attracted to places that maybe in the past, they weren’t. You have that. And I think growth of the game reflects that, as it should be. How many Division I basketball teams do you think can realistically win a championship in any given year? Let’s say 10 of 12.
 

“So realistically, we should win one championship ever 12 years … so why should people lose their (mind) over losing two games in November? Because you won six or seven in 15 years you’ve created a world where there is Disney World, Disneyland, and ‘UConn Land.'”

In fact, UConn, after winning 11 championships between 1995 and 2016, has won one since, in 2025 with Bueckers, Fudd and Strong overlapping.

At the start of the name-image-likeness revenue era, UConn appeared to have a major advantage. In Connecticut, women’s basketball players become household names and attract social media followings and endorsement opportunities, including national commercials, that aren’t available elsewhere. Bueckers and Fudd, for example, had 
enormous NIL agreements
 in place even before they arrived at UConn.

Auriemma has found mixed success in the transfer portal, but could go back to that source, as well as overseas, to complete his roster next spring.

“It takes a certain kind of player to play here and be successful here,” Auriemma said. “You’re going to be challenged and you’re going to be rewarded more so than any other place, but you’re also under a lot more scrutiny than any other place. So coming here comes with a tremendous amount of stuff you don’t get anywhere else, and I don’t mean tangible stuff, but what you get from this experience you can’t get from anywhere else.”

By Pankhuri Bhatnagar, B.A. LL.B.
A cooker supplier’s Illinois citizenship defeated diversity removal, but no attorney’s fees and costs were awarded to the consumer as there was no controlling circuit precedent on the issue.
In an action brought against a pressure cooker supplier by a woman who was injured when the cooker opened while pressurized, a federal district court in Illinois granted the consumer’s motion to remand. The court held that the supplier was an Illinois citizen based on its principal place of business and the belated declaration of an affiliate company’s head did not establish that the affiliate was the correct legal entity. The supplier was not a nominal party because under Illinois law, a bankrupt defendant sued to preserve claims against its insurers and indemnitors remains a real party in interest until the claims against it are dismissed (
Villa-Sanders v. Midea America Corp
., 
No. 26-cv-08204 (N.D. Ill. Nov. 17, 2026)).
Background.
 A woman alleged that an Instant Pot pressure cooker opened while its contents remained pressurized, causing serious burns and other bodily injuries. She filed a four-count action in Illinois state court, asserting strict-liability and negligence claims against several companies involved in the product’s design, manufacture, marketing, sale, and distribution. The action was subject to a bankruptcy stay until July 9, 2026. The following day, Midea America Corporation removed the case to federal court, invoking diversity jurisdiction. The woman sought remand, arguing that Corelle Brands Acquisition Holdings, whose principal place of business was in Illinois, was a forum defendant. She later voluntarily dismissed Midea America, leaving Corelle as the sole remaining defendant.
Parties’ arguments. 
Midea’s removal to federal court was based on the fact that the amount in controversy exceeded $75,000 and there was complete diversity between the parties, as required by 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a). However, the consumer claimed that the forum defendant rule defeated diversity jurisdiction because Corelle was a citizen of Illinois. Midea countered that Corelle was a citizen of Delaware and Ohio and regardless was a nominal defendant not subject to the forum defendant rule. A defendant is nominal if the plaintiff does not seek relief from them, or there is no reasonable basis for predicting that they will be held liable se per 
Selfix, Inc. v. Bisk
, 867 F. Supp. 1333, 1335–36 (N.D. Ill. 1994).
Corelle’s citizenship.
 A corporation is a citizen of two states: its state of incorporation and the state of its principal place of business as per 
Pastor v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.,
 487 F.3d 1042, 1047 (7th Cir. 2007). The woman relied on Illinois Secretary of State records and Corelle’s bankruptcy proceedings to establish that its principal place of business was in Illinois. Corelle submitted a declaration from the CFO of an affiliated company, Anchor Hocking Co., asserting that the correct legal entity was a citizen of Delaware and Ohio. The court found the declaration insufficient because it was submitted belatedly and failed to establish the declarant’s position, apart from being the CFO of a different company, or the relationship between the two companies. In a nearly identical case, 
Perez v. Corelle Brands Acquisition Holdings, 
Case No. 26-cv-8141, Dkt. 27 at 4 (N.D. Ill. Oct. 7, 2026), remand was denied for the same reason of unclear affiliation of the CFO with Corelle. Accordingly, this court decided that Corelle was an Illinois citizen.
Nominal defendant rule.
 The court rejected the argument that Corelle’s bankruptcy rendered it a nominal defendant whose citizenship could be disregarded. Courts generally distinguish between states that prohibit direct actions against insurers before judgment against the insured and states that permit such actions. In the former, a bankrupt defendant remains a real party because a judgment against it is necessary to preserve the injured party’s ability to recover from insurers or indemnitors. Illinois follows this approach under 
Marchlik v. Coronet Insurance Co.
, 239 N.E.2d 799 (Ill. 1968). Accordingly, Illinois defendants have a stake in product liability litigation against them until the claims have been dismissed and are not considered “nominal” for purposes of diversity jurisdiction or remand.
Although the complaint described Corelle as a nominal defendant and explained that it was named to preserve claims against applicable insurers and indemnitors, that purpose did not make it a nominal party for removal purposes. The pleading adequately alleged that Corelle had duties relating to the pressure cooker’s design, manufacture, assembly, testing, marketing, distribution, and sale, and that it had breached those duties. As such, the supplier remained subject to potential liability. The court concluded that it was a real party in interest and that its Illinois citizenship triggered the forum defendant rule under 28 U.S.C. § 1441(b)(2), warranting a remand. The court declined to award fees and costs to the consumer because there was no controlling circuit precedent on the issue, and it was among the first courts in the district to decide one of numerous similar remand motions. Each side was ordered to bear its own fees and costs.
The case is No. 
26-cv-08204
.

[Biz Insight] Fixing the value and pay structure is the urgent priority for foundational industries

Aging skilled workers replaced by foreign labor amid hiring crunch
Irregular work makes AI, robot transition elusive
 
 
 
The hiring crisis facing small and medium-sized enterprises is nothing new. Relying on foreign workers has become the only viable option.
Meanwhile, skilled technicians grow older every year. Foreign workers' capacity to learn on the job and their productivity often fall short of expectations.
The problem runs deepest in small and medium-sized manufacturing — and within that sector, in what are known as foundational industries, which form the bedrock of the national industrial base and sit at the far end of the supply chain. They include casting, molding, plating, forging, welding and heat treatment.
These firms occupy the tail end of the supply chains feeding industries such as textiles and automobiles — sectors that, semiconductors aside, are among South Korea's stronger performers. Without foundational industry suppliers, those flagship sectors become vulnerable, forcing a stark choice between in-house production and international division of labor. Trade-dependent supply chain design, however, has grown increasingly risky.
The manufacturing work done by foundational industry firms is irregular, non-standardized and highly discontinuous, making it difficult to systematize into defined processes. Physical AI and humanoid robots remain a distant concept for these companies.
Quality depends heavily on the fine-grained skill of experienced hands. The longer a worker stays — 10 years, 20 years — the higher the skill level and productivity.
The hiring crunch will persist. Poor compensation, long training periods and harsh working conditions all compound one another. Beyond the well-known aversion to dirty, dangerous and difficult work and a shrinking labor pool, demographic and social shifts — including the growing emphasis on work-life balance — are also at play.
The core problem lies in a weak value structure and a weak pay structure. Unlike other industries, the value these firms generate does not accumulate over time. Most are first- to third-tier suppliers working on contracts from larger prime contractors, keeping added value low across the entire supply chain.
This does mean that new entrants are rare, giving incumbents a degree of insulation from competition. But keeping existing firms alive and operating has become a national challenge.
Public expectations for physical AI and autonomous robots have risen sharply. Rosy predictions abound that unmanned factories and dark factories are just around the corner.
Given the current mood, it does not seem hard to imagine when such novel technologies might become widespread. The prevailing optimism runs along the lines of: "Two or three years of labor costs would more than cover the investment," and "Safety problems and quality problems will all be solved."
Yet these technologies are themselves high-end capital goods. They require substantial upfront funding. The time it takes to reach break-even after deploying assets strains a company's cash flow — and even firms with relatively strong balance sheets will find it hard to absorb.
Technical hurdles present another formidable barrier. For AI to become physical, massive research and development projects must be launched for each individual task and motion.
Alongside this, data must be compiled into databases to enable AI training. The so-called "tacit knowledge" held by skilled workers must be captured, converted into structured data and made electronically accessible — and only then can task-by-task, motion-by-motion machine learning begin. Deploying robots is just the starting point, not the finish line.
Consider the cold reality facing small and medium-sized enterprises. In a pyramid structure, profit margins shrink the further down you go — meaning intense cost pressure. Even firms that draw up automation plans often lack the financial capacity to execute them.
In-house research and development capabilities are also weak. Designing a humanoid robot tailored to a firm's own specialty is close to impossible.
The most pressing need right now is to raise the value of what these firms produce and ensure they can secure adequate margins. Policy support must follow.
That is how the sector can be given room to breathe amid the hiring crunch and the capacity to invest in research and development. This is precisely where the government's substantial surplus tax revenues could play a meaningful role.

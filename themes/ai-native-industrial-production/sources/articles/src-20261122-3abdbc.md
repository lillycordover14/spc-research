Electoral dysfunction:
 Well, that didn’t last very long. Earlier this month it seemed the Hawaiʻi Elections Commission was back on track after a five-month hiatus, thanks to the intervention of the Hawaiʻi Supreme Court. It held a public meeting and 
elected a temporary chair.

But within the first minutes of its next meeting, this one on Thursday, one of the eight commissioners challenged Tamara McKay’s authority to be the pro tem chair. Jeffrey Osterkamp also argued — or tried to, before he was cut out of the Zoom meeting at the insistence of McKay, who said Osterkamp was out of order because his point or order was not part of the meeting’s agenda — that the Elections Commission was dominated by election deniers.

You can read Osterkamp’s written statement below, which he emailed to The Blog. “It probably will be stricken from the record, as we now have an election denier as the invalidly elected Chair Pro Tem,” he wrote in an email.

Another commissioner, Barbara Dalton — like Osterkamp, appointed to the commission by Democrats — then left the meeting, too. “A hui ho,” she said before signing off.

That left the four remaining commissioners — all appointed by Republicans — without a quorum, meaning they could not deliberate nor make decisions per the state’s Sunshine Law. Two other Democrat-appointed commissioners, John Sabas and Liana Horovitz, did not attend the meeting.

The meeting nonetheless continued for more than 
two and a half hours
, dominated by testifiers that included some of the commissioners themselves. McKay and Co. did manage to take care of some administrative business that Deputy Attorney General James Paige determined was allowed: The commission plans to advertise for a permanent chair and reconvene in January.

Regarding the search for a new chair, Judith Mills Wong, president of the League of Women Voters of Hawaiʻi, had some advice. She told the commission it was important to select a nonpartisan chair, who would also be the commission’s ninth member.

“The role of the commission is not to represent the party or a position, but to fairly evaluate and advise on matters concerning the electoral process, and we would ask that there be no conflict of interest, not candidates, not people who have run for office, not people who are planning to run for or in the process of running for office, or their staff, and no one who is in conflict with the state who is suing the state for the electoral process,” she said.

Six members of the Hawaiʻi Elections Commission meeting via Zoom on Thursday before two of them suddenly left the meeting, depriving their colleagues of a quorum. 

That would seem to include McKay, who is a past chair of the Hawaiʻi Republican Party, and Cushnie, who along with McKay has sued the state over the electoral process. Both applied for the chair gig in June but could not muster enough support from other commissioners.

Excuses, excuses:
 During and after every election, the Hawaiʻi Campaign Spending Commission combs over candidate finance reports to make sure they correctly include contributions and expenditures. And during and after every election the CSC invariably finds errors in multiple reporting and doles out appropriate remedies — usually monetary fines.

That happened a half-dozen times at the 
commission’s Wednesday meeting
, although a few candidates pleaded for leniency. The fines went to unsuccessful Honolulu City Council candidate Tara Malia Gregory, who was fined $1,200 for a late filing, even though she told commissioners that she had made her filings “in good faith” but was confused about filing deadlines. Commissioner Barbara Polk did not accept that excuse, saying it was important candidates be transparent when it comes to campaign spending. 

Honolulu City Councilwoman Val Okimoto was fined $781.25 for her late filing and will pay up. Rep. Darius Kila was on the hook for $300 for his own late filing, one that he paid later that same day. Both were reelected this year.

Tara Malia Gregory speaking to the Hawaiʻi Campaign Spending Commission Wednesday. 

Meantime, unsuccessful state Senate candidate Christy MacPherson was fined $1,500 for failing to report expenditures, even though campaign treasurer Garrett Iha argued that he had indeed reported the expenses through the online filing system; for some unexplained reason, he said, they just did not take. And unsuccessful state House candidate Tam Hunt was fined $800 for several reporting errors.

Commissioners also rejected a request on behalf of Ryan Yamane, a former state representative, who was seeking clarification as to whether he could use his campaign funds to pay for legal fees. Yamane served in the House from 2004 to 2023 and then went to work as director of the Department of Human Services in the Green administration.

Former state Rep. Ryan Yamane is accused of taking bribes. 

He retired from that post in May and then was indicted in July on bribery and conspiracy charges. You might 
have read about that
. But Yamane has kept his House campaign filing open and has about $33,000 in it. 

“I respectfully request a formal advisory opinion interpreting the relevant provisions of
Hawaii campaign-finance law and Commission rules as applied to the facts described above,” said Isaac Choy, who made the inquiry on behalf of Yamane. Choy is a former state rep who has recently been part of a whistleblower case involving the Hawaiʻi Tourism Authority and the Green administration. You might have 
read about that too
.

The Campaign Spending Commission rejected Choy’s request, saying that using the campaign funds was prohibited unless the legal proceedings were somehow related to an elections proceeding or administrative agency action.

Nerd alert: 
Jared Kuroiwa is the City and County of Honolulu’s new chief data officer. Mayor Rick Blangiardi made the appointment last week.

Kuroiwa will head the Office of the Managing Director, where he will lead efforts to strengthen the city’s use of data, technology, and artificial intelligence “to improve operations, support informed decision-making, and drive innovation across city departments,” says a press release.

Jared Kuroiwa.  
(City and County of Honolulu)

The Blog welcomes Kuroiwa to his new gig. Way back in 2012 he developed on his own a searchable database of Hawaiʻi campaign finance information. He also co-founded Hawaiʻi Open Data, dedicated to expanding public access to government information. 

His background also includes roles at aio Hawaiʻi, Oceanic Time Warner Cable, PBS Hawaiʻi, the University of Hawaiʻi Mānoa and the Cancer Research Center of Hawaiʻi.

Agreeing to disagree: 
Gov. Josh Green was on the road last week, traveling to Santa Fe, New Mexico, and Park City, Utah, for meetings of the Western Governors’ Association and the National Governors Association.

He’s also co-starring in a new video from 
Disagree Better
, which bills itself as a nonpartisan nonprofit “focused on helping Americans navigate political differences more constructively.”

“Green joins outgoing Nevada Gov. Joe Lombardo (R) to show how leaders from opposing parties can disagree respectfully, find common ground and work together to solve problems,” says a press release.

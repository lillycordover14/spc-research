John Harris
Last week’s Milburn review contained telling phrases, but the core problem is not Neets: it’s what capitalism as we know it has done to employment

veryday pride and contentment. That’s what work should give us. In 15-plus years of 
speaking to ordinary people
 and politicians about the UK’s serial woes and crises, one thing has revealed itself to me time and time again: the mess of cant, contradictions and tensions in our national understanding of work. Indeed, the higher up the hierarchies of power and influence any conversation about work goes, the more one vitally important subject seems to fall away: what paid employment actually is or ought to be.
All of this is reflective of a very British kind of amnesia. Of course poorly rewarded, unreliable jobs have always been part of our economy – and in the past a 
focus of fierce social conflict
. But well within living memory a combination of the power of trade unions and the collective social conscience built into the postwar consensus resulted in an economy and labour market that now seem almost inconceivable. For sure they were scattered with restrictive practices and base prejudice: fathers securing jobs for their sons, people from minorities being 
excluded from work
 not just by people in management but by their fellow workers, and a great deal of sexism. But one of the tragedies of Labour’s 18 long years of exile from power and the climactic defeat of organised labour in the miners’ strike was the fact that they opened the way for the wholesale reinvention of work and watershed developments that we seem to be only just waking up to.
The Welsh writer Richard King’s 
oral history of his home country
, Brittle With Relics, contains a fascinating quotation from Nicky Wire of those sons of the South Wales coalfield the 
Manic Street Preachers
. Wire’s father was a miner before he joined the army and then came home to work as a carpenter and builder in a local economy that was thriving. Back then, to be working class did not axiomatically mean you were short of money; workers’ collective clout, moreover, delivered exactly what the 21st-century labour market so lacks.
“The pay levels in the 60s and the 70s were brilliant for working-class people,” says Wire. “Everyone in our valley was wearing suits and going to Tunisia on package holidays … My dad just worked his bollocks off; he was one of the great workers. When he passed away, we had ‘Father, son, brother, worker’ on his memorial stone, because that’s what he did.”
Far too many lives, in wildly diverse parts of the economy, are now a long way from that kind of everyday pride and contentment, something proved by no end of research. Only a few weeks ago, for instance, the 
Resolution Foundation published
 analysis of new information from the British Social Attitudes survey. The headline findings were that job satisfaction in the UK has dropped to the lowest level ever recorded and that “the proportion [of workers] willing to work harder than required to help their employer succeed has fallen to its lowest ever level over the past decade” (it stands at a pretty miserable 55%). The proportion saying they had an interesting job fell from 74% to 63%. The share reporting opportunities for advancement went from 35% to 26%.
In its soul Labour still thinks its basic mission is to right such wrongs. But from the Tony Blair years onwards, the party’s thinking has also been regularly 
warped by inclinations
 that it ought to find objectionable: to 
shove people into work
 irrespective of what that entails, and to emphasise the need to redesign human beings to somehow fit with the mercurial, often cruel ways of modern capitalism rather than making perfectly justified demands for the latter to change.
Which brings us to the 
report about young people and employment 
– and more specifically Neets, or those not in education, employment or training – put together by the former New Labour high-up Alan Milburn and published this week. On a long train journey I read all 1,005 of its numbered paragraphs and was struck by a few things: the thoroughness of its research and the urgency of its tone – but one glaring absence: of any meaningful examination of what might be shorthanded as “bad work” despite its centrality to just about everything Milburn has worked on.

Milburn partly blames the current crisis on how the state delivers benefits, education and health. (There is a whole other piece that could be written about how a proposed 
£3.6bn of cuts to so-called “welfare”
 risk so impoverishing vulnerable young people that they will be pushed even further away from the labour market.) He rightly points out a “long-term decline in entry-level employment, youth apprenticeships and employer investment in training”. But though he also rather grandly proposes “a new bargain between government and employers to create and sustain more opportunities for young people”, you soon realise what he means – as evidenced by 
whispers currently circulating 
about the abandonment of some of Angela Rayner’s new programme of improved workers’ rights, Milburn gets close to proposing not to expect more from employers but less.
“Employers have faced higher costs, a maze of schemes, fragmented support and growing uncertainty about the rules of the game,” he says, and have been “treated as if they were the problem rather than a central part of the solution”. He has picked up concerns about Rayner’s workplace changes, he adds, centred on “the impact the new legislation may have on youth hiring, especially in hospitality and retail”. But that kind of talk risks defending the indefensible: a 
recent report from the TUC
 not only highlighted the low pay and job insecurity seemingly endemic to those sectors of the economy but also pointed out how they contribute to “economic inactivity generally and specifically the rise in mental distress and economic inactivity among young people”. Letting them off the hook, in other words, may not ease the Neets crisis but worsen it.
There is another 
very illuminating TUC report
, published in 2020, about working for Amazon (the company, for some reason, is thanked in the Milburn report’s acknowledgements). Its central beef was “a business model that functions on driving down workers’ rights”, and what Amazon demands of its employees: in the UK, it said, “pickers are expected to pick and pack around 300 items per hour, [and] employees tend to only either do day or night shifts and working weeks average around 55 hours… especially over the peak Christmas period”.

Both Milburn’s interim report and his final text are full of telling phrases: on this score, although he does acknowledge that the quality of work available to young people has generally “worsened”, he merely observes that “warehouse jobs require speed, precision and compliance with tightly managed performance systems”, while the 1980s’ chaotic decimation of traditional work that opened the way for businesses such as Amazon is blankly – and hilariously – summed up as “industrial restructuring”. That suggests order and purpose, but the result has been anarcho-capitalism and lives rendered all but impossible. In 2022, the company’s 
annual employee turnover
 in the US was reckoned to be 150%. The rational response to that kind of working culture is what sits at the heart of the Neets story: people recoil and retreat.
In the short term what Milburn recommends – among other things, a new youth opportunities service and increased spending on subsidising young people into work – may well lessen the crisis he says he wants to solve. But it will also leave such fundamental problems almost completely untouched. The point is plain: not least for the young, until work is comprehensively reinjected with respect, humanity and decent levels of pay, the Neets crisis – and our national malaise – will go on.
How do you even begin to do that? As the venerable Wes Streeting 
once said
, the only thing worse than no hope is false hope, but the prime minister has suggested that he might know, talking 
confidently about reindustrialisation
 and – oh yes – somehow 
rolling back 40 years of neoliberalism
. His fabled 10-year plan is apparently due before the end of the year – for now, along with all those Neets, we carry on waiting for the great leap forwards.

John Harris is a Guardian columnist

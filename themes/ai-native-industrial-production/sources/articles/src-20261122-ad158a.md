Guermantes Lailari On Taiwan: A Proposal for Taiwan’s Whole-of-Society Deterrence Framework

Add TT as Preferred Source

Beijing’s National Security Commission coordinates 50,000 to 100,000 Taiwanese informants via gray-zone tactics to alter elections through covert financing, criminal proxies, digital blackmail, and street intimidation. To secure the vote, this article proposes shifting from a top-down military model to a comprehensive Whole-of-Society Deterrence Framework. Central to this defense is a proposed Taiwanese Democratic Resilience and Whole-of-Society Omnibus Security Bill, turning open democratic systems into counterintelligence defenses against espionage and United Front Work Department (UFWD) operations. Below are key proposed gap fillers.
The current Anti-Infiltration Act (反滲透法) is reactive, tracking illicit cross-strait capital only after distribution. This structural delay can leave authorities unable to detect funding early, exposing the electoral processes to front-loaded funding.
The proposal expands the Act to mandate a Corporate Cross-Strait Beneficial Ownership Registry with a 5 percent disclosure threshold. In other words, operating entities should file real-time, three-tier disclosures routed through an audit loop that includes the Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) and Ministry of Justice Investigation Bureau (MJIB), enabling immediate emergency asset freezes. This closes loopholes exposed by the 2024 prosecution of a former borough chief surnamed Kuo, who allegedly funneled undisclosed Taiwan Affairs Office (TAO) subsidies into local municipal organizations to alter political alignments. Falsifying ownership records would carry administrative fines of up to NT$50,000,000 (US$1.52 million), with criminal penalties of up to 10 years’ imprisonment and a NT$100,000,000 fine if tied to an infiltration source.

To block covert influence, a proposed foreign agent registration and transparency law draws on the US Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA) and Australia’s Foreign Influence Transparency Scheme (FITS). Any group or multi-channel network (MCN) accepting adversarial funds or coordination must register as a “Foreign Agent.” Crucially, the Act targets algorithmic cognitive warfare, criminalizing unregistered local networks paid to inject “Taiwan skepticism” narratives — sowing doubt about the government’s ability to ensure Taiwan’s national security — into local social media. Registered agents would be barred from consulting or bidding on public procurement contracts, preventing adversarial actors from leveraging domestic channels.
Amendments to the Organized Crime Prevention Act (組織犯罪防制條例) should classify domestic syndicates engaging in tactical or financial coordination with hostile forces as national security fronts, enabling asset tracking. This counters criminal proxies used by Beijing’s Ministry of Public Security (MPS) to cause civil unrest during elections — a threat visible in maritime gray-zone enforcement operations around Kinmen, Matsu, and Penghu.
 

While the current Article 15-1 of the Political Parties Act (政黨法) permits the dissolution of political parties, legacy litigation allowed fronts to operate for years while cases stalled. An amendment would allow the MOI and MJIB to act more quickly by sending an emergency petition directly to the Constitutional Court. The Court would rule on applications for an emergency provisional injunction within 72 hours to freeze accounts, close headquarters, and halt public operations immediately, clearing the way for permanent asset forfeiture during full judicial review.
To shield public servants, researchers, and engineers, a proposed transnational repression and dissident protection law upgrades state-directed stalking, physical intimidation, and digital blackmail into non-bailable security felonies. This counters tactics seen in the vehicular assault targeting comedian Huang Yi-hao (黃逸豪), and the surveillance of public officials’ relatives overseas. The law would automatically double baseline sentences for violent crimes committed under foreign direction.
To prevent foreign cyber-attacks from disrupting public utilities during elections, a proposed critical infrastructure cyber-physical resilience law would mandate absolute physical air-gapping of Operational Technology (OT) networks from Internet-facing corporate IT grids. Data travels only outward via unidirectional optical data diodes, while switchgear utilizes hardwired analog overrides that are immune to remote code injections.
Given that Taiwan’s connectivity relies on fourteen fiber-optic lines routed through eight landing stations concentrated in Tamsui (淡水), Toucheng (頭城) and Fangshan (枋山) — with vulnerabilities highlighted by past disruptions of undersea cables to Matsu — hardening these perimeters is paramount. The National Institute of Cyber Security (NICS) would receive statutory authority to conduct unannounced penetration tests. Operators would have 48 hours to fix flagged risks or face administrative receivership. Private Independent Power Producers (IPPs) must comply on pain of immediate license revocation.
A proposed critical supply chain security law would mandate a Hardware Provenance Certificate tracing component lifecycles back to silicon fabrication for all public contracts. This insulates firms from Western secondary sanctions and defends Taiwan’s “Silicon Shield” — including TSMC and downstream supply chain hubs in the Hsinchu, Central and Southern Taiwan Science parks. It would criminalize trade-secret siphoning and headhunting via offshore shell proxies as state treason felonies. This addresses vulnerabilities from past audits that uncovered 3,000 traffic management sensors and smart-city IP cameras across frontline areas (including Hsinchu and Taichung) using components built by PRC-subsidized firms, which functioned as backdoors tracking troop movements. An NT$50 billion Critical Infrastructure Hardening Bond would fund the immediate rip-and-replace of compromised technology assets across frontline local administrations before 2028.
As technical perimeters harden, the state must address vulnerable cultural infrastructure, labor networks, and the religious sector. A proposed religious corporations law would require temples, churches and folk fellowships to register with the MOI as public trusts and corporations. Places of worship would have to implement real-name donation ledgers for contributions over NT$10,000, enforce an absolute capital ban blocking foreign proxy cash, and submit to forensic audits.
This would close the tactical loophole where off-the-grid altars and family-run shrines distributed cash and coordinated subsidized cross-strait pilgrimages to influence voters. Using an unregistered spiritual fellowship to process adversarial assets would carry a mandatory five-year prison sentence. Commercial banks and crypto platforms would be prohibited from providing services to unregistered groups. A specialized Religious Financial Intelligence Unit (R-FIU) within the MJIB would run real-time forensic accounting audits on temple boards displaying anomalous cash spikes during elections, triggering heavy fines, asset seizure or corporate dissolution through legal proceedings.
The omnibus security bill would simultaneously insert a legal residents and foreign commercial autonomy clause, mandating that emergency alert networks be automatically translated into Tagalog, Indonesian, Vietnamese and Thai. This counters foreign-language disinformation campaigns designed to trigger mass industrial or port walkouts during elections. This clause would integrate Taiwan’s 800,000 legal migrant residents into the national defense grid as active lookouts capable of reporting logistics sabotage. Concurrently, a Sovereignty Compliance Safe Harbor will insulate multinational corporations inside Taiwan from civil liability if they refuse to comply with Chinese data-localization mandates that compromise technology sovereignty. Firms exposing economic coercion would be shielded via state-backed liquidity facilities funded through the National Development Fund (NDF).
Finally, an omnipresent defensive shield requires reinforcing military loyalty while establishing grassroots logistics redundancy. A proposed military and frontline personnel integrity law would enact a mandatory life sentence without parole for military personnel convicted of selling state defense data. Plots to defect with military hardware involving state assets would carry the death penalty or life imprisonment and permanent revocation of all pensions.
This would close critical gaps exposed by the 2023 Army Aviation defection plot. The High Prosecutors’ Office indicted an active-duty lieutenant colonel surnamed Hsieh (謝), who was recruited by Chinese agents through a network led by a retired Taiwanese military officer. Hsieh was promised safe-haven passage, a Thai permanent resident visa, and a NT$488,000,000 payout to defect and fly a CH-47SD Chinook military helicopter onto a PLA aircraft carrier in the Taiwan Strait. The new law would eliminate judicial leniency for structural defection to secure military stability ahead of 2028.
The law would create an automated cross-agency credit monitoring mesh linking military security databases with the FSC and consumer credit bureaus. The system would dynamically flag personnel experiencing sudden spikes in high-interest private debt or unverified capital inflows, triggering immediate counter-espionage review. To balance this aggressive monitoring, the state would allocate NT$1.5 billion annually from the NDF to operate a financial relief program providing low-interest debt consolidation loans to personnel facing extreme financial duress, systematically neutralizing primary recruitment levers.
To sustain local defense loops if centralized networks are pressured, this framework leverages Taiwan’s ultra-dense private retail distribution ecosystems to build grassroots material logistics redundancy. Taiwan hosts over 13,000 highly resilient convenience stores across major networks (7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Hi-Life, OK Mart). The architecture would legally mandate and subsidize these networks to maintain localized, rolling emergency supply reserves — including trauma medical kits, shelf-stable rations, and analog water purifiers — equivalent to a 30-day decentralized cushion for their immediate neighborhoods. If centralized communications fail, store logistics infrastructure switches to real-time notification broadcast modes using independent power generation, ensuring that every citizen can access essential commodities directly within their immediate local block.
Ultimately, Beijing’s multi-tiered matrix relies on automated fear, internal division, and civic isolation to win without fighting, seeking to convince the Taiwanese populace that democratic defense is futile.
This proposed Whole-of-Society Deterrence Framework provides the operational antidote to this strategy. By unifying financial overwatch through the R-FIU, air-gapping critical infrastructure grids, protecting public servants with robust legal shields, and mobilizing grassroots neighborhood hubs, Taiwan converts its open society from a vulnerability into a highly resilient web of active defenses. When voters cross the threshold on election day, they would do so backed by a bulletproof legislative and structural perimeter. This comprehensive whole-of-society architecture ensures that Taiwan remains an un-subvertible democratic fortress, standing completely resolute at the ballot box.
Guermantes Lailari is a retired US Air Force Foreign Area Officer specializing in counterterrorism, irregular warfare, missile defense, and strategy. He holds advanced degrees in international relations and strategic intelligence. He was a Ministry of Foreign Affairs Taiwan Fellow in 2022, a visiting scholar at National Chengchi University and National Defense University in 2023, and a visiting researcher at the Institute for National Defense and Security Research in 2024 and 2025. He is currently researching in Taiwan as a senior non-resident fellow from the Jewish Policy Center in Washington, DC.

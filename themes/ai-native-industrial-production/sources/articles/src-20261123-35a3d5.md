Nvidia’s board ousted Jensen Huang for a ‘lack of candor.’ Twelve days later, the same directors brought him back&nbsp;&nbsp;Fortune

[page text]
Business history was made today, and none of it will be remembered with pride. Less than two weeks ago, on Nov. 11, Jensen Huang stepped down under 
pressure
 from his board, which 
accepted
 his resignation. Six days later, chair Harvey Jones 
declared
 that the board “does not intend to reinstate” the founder and 
rebuffed
 his offer to return. The following day, Jones informed 
employees
 that directors had acted over personal conduct and a “lack of candor.” Today, those same directors voted 
unanimously
 to bring him back, saying they were “satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang’s candor.”
Recommended Video

Rarely in American business have directors removed a chief executive over graver allegations. Huang 
misled
 his board about personal relationships, including one with the leader of a major customer whom U.S. officials 
suspected
 of ties to Chinese intelligence. A national security inquiry is 
underway
, alongside 
Reuters
 and 
Bloomberg
 reports that restricted chips were smuggled into China and reached military buyers. Meanwhile, the SEC is 
examining
 Nvidia’s accounting. Directors reversed course anyway, acting before their 
investigators
 could finish the work.

In our forthcoming 
book
, 
When Machines Act: The Promise and Peril of Our Agentic AI Future
 (MIT Press/Penguin Random House), we draw directly on Huang’s commentary from our Yale Chief Executive Leadership Institute CEO forums, making this breakdown in corporate oversight all the more sobering.

Fired, Rehired, and What It Usually Means

Some founders have earned a second act. 
Apple
 
forced
 Steve Jobs out in 1985 after he went to war against his own company, welcoming him back in 1997 only after he had learned how to lead. The first author knew Steve well, and the man who came back had clearly been reshaped by exile. At OpenAI in November 2023, directors 
fired
 Sam Altman for not being “consistently candid,” sparking an employee uprising that restored him within days and 
drove out
 most of the board that had dismissed him.

Departures triggered by misconduct or dishonesty follow an altogether different script. 
HP
 pushed out 
Mark Hurd
 in 2010 over expense reports that concealed a relationship with a contractor. Five years earlier, 
Boeing
 had removed 
Harry Stonecipher
 for a consensual affair that violated the very code of conduct he had been brought back to restore. Relationships with employees ended the tenures of Steve 
Easterbrook
 at McDonald’s in 2019, 
Brian Krzanich
 at 
Intel
 in 2018, and Brian 
Dunn
 at 
Best Buy
 in 2012. American Apparel ousted 
Dov Charney
 in 2014 amid misconduct allegations, and RadioShack parted ways with 
David Edmondson
 in 2006 after he falsified his résumé.

Nvidia fits neither precedent. Jobs waited twelve years to reclaim his post, and Altman stepped back into a transformed boardroom. Huang returned in twelve days to the identical directors, on the identical facts, with the investigation still open.

A Board Held Hostage

Across decades of studying corporate governance, we have seen no weaker board than Nvidia’s. Today’s capitulation ranks among the worst decisions of that entire span, leaving any analogy to Apple or OpenAI untenable.

Signs of dependence appeared early. Several directors have known Huang for decades, and Jones has sat on the board since 
1993
, before Nvidia went public. According to 
The Information
, the general counsel had to press directors simply to open an investigation. Even after the chair publicly ruled out a comeback, 
Semafor
 reported that a faction inside the boardroom kept lobbying to bring Huang back.

External pressure pushed in the same direction. A 
petition
 gathered roughly 180,000 signatures, bolstered by 
backing
 from Elon Musk, Tim Sweeney, and Garry Tan. The President 
remarked
 that he would “love to see Jensen be given a second chance,” and directors explicitly 
credited
 “input from senior levels of the United States government.” Huang’s loudest defenders were investors and founders, many holding stakes in his ecosystem, rather than the broader CEO establishment. Unprepared to navigate a crisis of this magnitude, the board has exhausted the credibility it needs to enforce its own rules.

Strip away the noise of the past 12 days, and 12 stark realities remain.

Twelve Takeaways

The board flatly contradicted its own verdict on honesty. On Nov. 18, Jones pointed directly to Huang’s “lack of candor.” Five days later, those same directors declared themselves “satisfied” with his candor, citing only meetings with Huang himself and input from Washington.

The board preempted its investigators before they could finish their assignment. Wachtell and Joon Kim were 
retained
 on Nov. 10 to establish the facts. A board confident in what they would find could easily have waited for the report, and a board uncertain of the answer had every reason to wait.

On national security, directors deferred to a divided Washington. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent 
called
 for a “dramatic shakeup,” a 
memo
 prepared under Secretary of State Marco Rubio warned about Alice Huang, and Megaspeed faces Commerce, Justice, and congressional 
scrutiny
. Despite the mounting technical evidence, the Trump administration intervened on Jensen’s behalf. In October, the Israeli security firm 
Lava
 reported 32 Blackwell Ultra B300 GPUs on state-owned China Telecom’s network, though it could not verify their physical location. Nvidia, citing recent court cases, put diversion under 0.5 percent and called it minor. On Nov. 12, 
Unemployment Labs
 found the fingerprint of export-controlled Blackwell chips in the weights of six recent Chinese models. The board’s “lack of candor” finding and Huang’s admission of “serious mistakes” are on the record. Diversion, national security, and the accounting are still under investigation, and the board reinstated him before its inquiry could settle the facts. When Washington’s signals conflict, a board cannot outsource its fiduciary duty to whichever voice proves most convenient.

Accounting questions at the SEC remain unresolved. Although the agency’s exact scope is unconfirmed, press scrutiny has centered on Nvidia’s change in 
geographic
 revenue reporting and its AI financing deals. The examination is in its early stages, and no charges have been filed. Reinstating a CEO whose candor the board just impugned undermines the company’s credibility with auditors and regulators at the very moment its revenue reporting and financing deals face scrutiny.

Temporary leave pending the investigation offered the defensible middle course. We defended the resignation last week and still do. Had directors lacked the resolve to hold that line, placing Huang on leave would have protected the process. The calendar argues for leave too, since Huang’s first weeks back will be absorbed by testimony before three 
House committees
 and a 
Senate Banking
 Committee that also wants him. Reinstating him before the facts are settled serves neither the inquiry nor the company.

Enforcing the code of conduct credibly will now be nearly impossible. Any employee disciplined for an undisclosed relationship with a subordinate can point straight to the chief executive. Inconsistent enforcement invites legal exposure, and women weighing job offers will reasonably ask what protection the company’s rules afford them.

Shareholders deserve genuine accountability. Between Nvidia’s October peak and Friday’s close, roughly 
$2.7 trillion
 in market value evaporated as AI spending fears, the scandal, news of the SEC review, and boardroom whiplash all took their toll. Directors who reversed themselves should step down, following the path most of OpenAI’s board took in 2023.

State authorities will not let this matter drop. On Nov. 19, California Attorney General Rob Bonta served an investigative 
subpoena
 on Nvidia. In 2027, Democrats will hold 
twenty-eight
 state attorney general offices, up from twenty-three, along with 
thirty
 governorships.

Public funds may begin reconsidering their exposure. State treasurers and pension boards under Democratic leadership cannot force index providers to drop a stock, yet they can review their Nvidia holdings or shift toward custom benchmarks. 
XMAG
, an S&P 500 fund without all seven tech giants, had 
outpaced
 the index, year to date, by nearly nine percentage points as of Friday’s close. One year proves little about long-term returns, but it weakens the familiar defense that public pension funds are fiduciarily trapped in megacap tech and cannot trim exposure without sacrificing performance.

Partners and customers will now price in governance risk. On Nov. 18, Moody’s 
cut its outlook
 on Nvidia’s Aa1 rating from positive to stable, citing governance weaknesses and export control vulnerabilities. Clients handling sensitive government work will move cautiously.

Washington is drifting into the very state corporatism Americans condemn in Beijing, picking national champions and weighing in on who leads them. After demanding the 
resignation
 of Intel’s chief executive in 2025 and securing roughly a 10 percent federal 
stake
 in the company, the President intervened at Nvidia, floated 
Tim Cook
 as a replacement, then reversed course, speaking directly with the 
board
 and lobbying for Huang’s return. Federal involvement extends to revenue as well, with the government collecting a 
share
 of Nvidia’s chip sales to China. Personal balance sheets blur the lines further. Disclosures show the President’s Nvidia holdings climbed to as much as 
$62 million
, and lawmakers are examining whether the Commerce Department’s delay on Megaspeed benefited 
Cantor Fitzgerald
. Now run by Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s sons, the firm held hundreds of millions of dollars in Nvidia stock. Cantor denies any conflict and says those positions largely hedged client trades.

The President also overruled his own party’s China hawks. Senate Intelligence Committee Chairman Tom Cotton 
says
 Lutnick ignored his “many letters” about Megaspeed for more than a year, and Senator Jim Banks pointed out that the Commerce chief already held the 
authority
 to close export loopholes and act against the Singapore firm. Senator John Kennedy went further, calling Huang’s conduct “a special kind of stupid,” 
warning
 that reinstatement would invite shareholder suits, and pressing for Lutnick to 
testify
. Instead of backing those lawmakers, the President 
declared
 Huang “exonerated” and 
reaffirmed
 his confidence in the Commerce Secretary. Today, Senator John Cornyn 
pierced
 the board’s security assurance in three words, asking, “Based on what?” The hawks did their jobs, only for the White House to leave them twisting in the wind.

No One Above the Code

Huang’s supporters have their own case to make. Nvidia’s stock fell during his absence and rallied roughly 
30 percent
 on word of his return. A one-day relief rally prices in operational continuity, not governance health, and a whipsawing board leaves long-term retirement holders exposed to unresolved regulatory, accounting, and export-control risk. Neither Huang nor the company has been charged, and the Attorney General 
stated
 that no conclusions had been reached on the spy claim. Huang has 
admitted
 “serious mistakes,” pledged to cooperate, and agreed to sit for a transcribed House interview on Dec. 1, while the board has created a National Security and Export Control Oversight 
Committee
. Each of those points argues for patience and due process, not reinstatement before the investigation ends.

Today, with the President’s backing, a corporate board waved back in a chief executive who broke the code of conduct, misled his directors, and put retirement savings at risk. No one deserves a special standard, however talented, not even a man the President 
calls
 a “Great Patriot and National Treasure.” Whatever moved voters this month, the election gives a larger bench of Democratic attorneys general and governors the tools and the political incentive to test whether tech’s most celebrated founders remain within reach of the law.

Last week we 
wrote
 that the true test of a great company is whether it can outlast the person who built it. Nvidia’s board apparently could not outlast his absence for twelve days.

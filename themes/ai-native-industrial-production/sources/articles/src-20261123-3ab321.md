Here We Go Again: Democrats Are Planning a Dozen Primary Debates&nbsp;&nbsp;New York Magazine

[page text]
An early 2020 debate.
                  
Photo: Godofredo A. Vasquez/Houston Chronicle/Getty Images

If you needed any confirmation that the 2028 presidential election cycle is now underway, the Democratic National Committee has provided it with an 
announcement
 of a rough schedule and framework for candidate debates.

Though eight very long years have gone by since the last contested Democratic nomination, the DNC appears to be following the same basic template as it did in the 2020 cycle: 12 sanctioned debates beginning in June 2027, with five in that calendar year and seven in 2028. The 2020 schedule was very similar, though the final planned debate was canceled after 
Joe Biden
 clinched the nomination. Locations and media sponsors are TBD, though the announcement did indicate that “early state debates” would all occur in 2028. That’s an allusion to the earlier DNC decision identifying 
six states
 (in order: South Carolina, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Michigan, and Virginia) as authorized to hold primaries prior to the customary March 1 opening bell for most nominating contests (a big batch will be held on March 7, the latest version of “Super Tuesday”).

 
As the DNC explained, it appears that the basic 2020 rules for debate participation will also be followed again, not that Democrats were universally happy with them at the time:

 
In order to accommodate a potentially large field of candidates, early debates may occur over two consecutive nights. In the event that the qualifying field of candidates requires these debates to be held over two nights, the lineup for each night will be determined at random — in a manner that will ensure parity across both nights — with qualifying candidates assigned one night per debate. The DNC will cap the number of participants at 20 across two nights, in the event that the qualifying field of candidates exceeds that amount. 

So that means “split panel” debates again, with some candidates potentially being excluded according to criteria that include “polling and other objective measures that reflect a candidate’s support, such as grassroots fundraising.” There will again be complaints about these criteria from candidates who don’t meet the cut, and probably a steady evolution of debate-participation thresholds as the field is inevitably winnowed down. The criteria for the first two debates will be announced in January. As in the past, candidates who cheat on the party by attending unsanctioned multi-candidate events will be barred from the official debates.

 
The one wrinkle in the initial rules is that candidates will be required to “sign and follow the DNC’s Fair Campaign Practices Pledge committing to denounce dark money spending in our primary in order to qualify for debate participation.” As the New York 
Times
 
explains
, that requirement was apparently adopted in response to a demand by progressive leader Bernie Sanders:

 
In April, [Sanders] called on Mr. Martin and the D.N.C. to require candidates up and down the ballot to reject super PAC and dark money support. Mr. Martin at the time said he agreed, but argued that it would take commitments from across the party to ban such practices from primaries.
Still, 
it is widely expected
 that most major presidential candidates will have authorized supportive super PACs. How the D.N.C.’s pledge affects those organizations remains to be seen.

In that respect as in so many others, let the games begin.

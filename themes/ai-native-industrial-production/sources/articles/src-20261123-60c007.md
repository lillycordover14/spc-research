            Twelve EU states push back as US texts class Feta, Asiago and Jerez as common names        

4 minutes read

                A paper for agriculture ministers counted 10 American agreements, while US texts listed dozens of European terms from Gruyère to Black Forest ham as free for anyone to use.            

 

                            Antonio O'Mullony                        

Senior Editor

 

By

Antonio O'Mullony
 
Senior Editor

Twelve European Union member states have called for a common strategy to defend the bloc’s protected food and drink names against a United States campaign to have them classed as generic terms abroad.

The call comes in 
a note
 circulated by the Council of the European Union on November 20 for the agriculture ministers’ meeting in Brussels on November 23. It was tabled by Italy and backed by Austria, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Czechia, France, Greece, Hungary, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia and Spain.

Geographical indications (GIs) reserve a name for producers in its place of origin who follow set methods. The European Commission has registered more than 3,700 of them, generating over €75 billion in annual sales and 15.5 per cent of EU agrifood exports, according to Commission figures.

The note says the EU and its member states “should consider” closer coordination and a comprehensive political strategy. That could combine legal protection with awareness-raising, technical cooperation and capacity-building in third countries.

It identifies Washington’s Agreements on Reciprocal Trade as the main pressure point. Ten have been signed, with Argentina, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Indonesia, Jordan, Malaysia and Taiwan, and talks with India are continuing.

The Council paper does not list the names at stake. Washington’s own documents do.

When the Ecuador deal was announced in November 2025, the US Trade Representative 
said
 Quito would not restrict market access over “the mere use of certain cheese and meat terms”. It named asiago, feta, fontina, gorgonzola, gruyere, parmesan, black forest ham, prosciutto and salami, “and others”.

The agreement with Argentina, signed in early February, covers Asiago, Fontina, Gorgonzola, Grana, Parmesan, Pecorino, Provolone and Romano cheeses and Bologna sausage. The EU-Mercosur agreement, to which Argentina is also party, bars protected European names from being treated as common ones.

Indonesia is in the same position. Its deal with the EU protects more than 200 products and specifies that feta comes only from Greece and Gorgonzola only from Italy, while Jakarta agreed in February to let American producers use their preferred names.

The longest list is in US legislation. The Safeguarding American Food and Export Trade Yields (SAFETY) Act, 
introduced
 by Republican senator John Thune in April 2025, declares 29 food names common.

Among the cheeses are Asiago, Feta, Fontina, Gorgonzola, Grana, Gruyere, Mascarpone, Munster, Neufchatel, Parmesan, Pecorino, Ricotta and Romano. The meats include Black Forest ham, Bologna, Bratwurst, Chorizo, Kielbasa, Mortadella, Prosciutto and Salami.

Drinks are covered too. The bill treats wine label terms such as chateau, clos, noble, ruby, tawny and vintage as common, along with beer styles including Kölsch, Oktoberfest, Dortmunder and Budweiser, and it lists Jerez and sherry.

Many of those words are the short forms of names on the EU register. Feta is a Greek designation, and Asiago, Gorgonzola, Fontina, Grana Padano, Parmigiano Reggiano, Pecorino Romano, Prosciutto di Parma and Mortadella Bologna are Italian.

Munster and Neufchâtel are French, Jerez is Spanish and 
Budějovické pivo
, the Budweis beer, is Czech. 
Schwarzwälder Schinken
 (Black Forest ham) and Kölsch are German, although Germany is not among the note’s 12 backers.

The note adds names of its own in a separate warning about new internet domain endings. It cites .asiago, .gorgonzola, .champagne, .porto, .budweiser, .cava and .tokaj, which brings French, Portuguese, Spanish and Hungarian designations into the dispute.

The House of Representatives passed its Farm Bill on April 30 with the SAFETY Act 
folded into it
. A Senate committee text is awaiting a floor vote, and both would require the US Department of Agriculture to define a “common name”, according to 
a November 10 analysis
 by the Congressional Research Service.

American producers say it is Brussels that is overreaching. The Consortium for Common Food Names (CCFN), a US industry group, 
said on June 24
 the Senate provision would “counter the European Union’s abuse of geographical indications”.

The CCFN accepts that Parmigiano Reggiano has to come from Italy and rejects the idea that all parmesan must. EU law does not draw that line: The Court of Justice of the European Union ruled in 2008 that “Parmesan” evoked the protected name.

The dispute has reached the World Intellectual Property Organisation (WIPO). At a WIPO seminar in Makati City, the Philippines, on October 28-29, US patent and trade officials argued for a “right to use common names” for terms protected in the EU, the note says.

Brussels has continued to win recognition in its own talks, with exceptions. Australia agreed in March to protect 396 European names, but its existing producers may keep using feta and must drop fontina only after five years, the 
Journal
 reported.

Italy has been the most vocal of the 12. Agriculture minister Francesco Lollobrigida wrote to Agriculture Commissioner Christophe Hansen on November 3, and enterprise minister Adolfo Urso called on November 21 for “a firm position from Europe”.

Ministers were due to hear the point under any other business, a slot that does not involve a vote.

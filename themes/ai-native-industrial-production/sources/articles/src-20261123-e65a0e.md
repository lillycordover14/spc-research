Stock Market Correction 2026: Is It 2018 All Over Again?

By 
Lance Roberts

•

November 23, 2026

•

14 min read

 

Wall Street calls the 2026 stock market correction a replay of 2018, but the differences matter more for what investors do next.

Key Takeaways

Stocks have done roughly half the work of the 2018 stock market selloff. Credit, the front end of the yield curve, and the “safe havens” haven’t followed the script.

The economy and earnings look nothing like they did in late 2018. Third-quarter GDP grew 3.4% and third-quarter earnings rose 31.9%.

The Fed is the difference that hurts. Powell backed off within 16 days in 2018. Today, futures price in roughly 75 basis points of additional tightening.

Systematic positioning is washed out, and year-end rebalancing is likely to favor buying stocks. Hedge funds protecting their annual numbers may stay defensive until January.

Our read is that this is a correction inside a bull market until credit or Treasuries say otherwise. The December 16 close is the line in the sand.

On Christmas Eve 2018, a client called Goldman Sachs’ derivatives desk to ask why stocks were down so much. Brian Garrett’s answer was honest: 
“absolutely zero idea.”
 Eight years later, Garrett is fielding the same calls, and nearly every desk has reached for the same playbook. The 2018 selloff is now the favorite comparison for the 2026 stock market correction.

The resemblance is real. The S&P 500 closed Friday at 6,910, down 11.6% from its October 6 record of 7,818.93 and 6% below its 200-day moving average.
3
 The Fed is hiking. Dealers are short gamma. The VIX closed at 37.24, above its level on Christmas Eve 2018. Garrett’s math says that if the 2018 path plays out from here, the S&P bottoms near 6,294 in the first week of January.
1

However, the problem with analogies is that they rarely repeat the same way twice. As such, analogies are only useful if you check where they break. 
Lay 2026 next to 2018 and the price action matches, but almost nothing that produced the 2018 low has shown up yet.
 That gap is where both the opportunity and the risk live, and it’s what this piece is about.

Where The 2026 Stock Market Correction Looks Like 2018

Start with what matches. In late 2018, the market faced a Fed hiking into weakness, a broken 200-day moving average and a wave of systematic selling into thin holiday trading. Every one of those boxes is checked today. Garrett summed up the 2018 ingredients in his note to clients on Friday. To wit:

“A hawkish fed, technical breakdowns, extreme volatility, systematic supply, etc… sound familiar?” 
– Brian Garrett, Goldman Sachs, Weekend Prep, Nov. 20, 202
6

Garrett measured every major asset’s move since the October 6 peak against its move between September 24 and December 24, 2018. Through Thursday, the Nasdaq 100 had completed 69% of its 2018 drawdown. The S&P 500 had done 42% and the Russell 2000 just 24%.
1
 Friday’s slide pushed those numbers higher, and the damage is concentrated exactly where 2018’s was: in the leaders.

The internals tell the same story. 
Thursday’s advance-decline spread of -426 was the worst reading of the year. One-month implied volatility for the average S&P stock is in the 93rd percentile over the past decade.
2
 Nvidia, the poster child of the
 “AI trade,”
 is down roughly 39% from early October. That’s what a liquidation looks like.

The first-half winners are paying for it. Across 2,258 Goldman baskets, the better a theme performed in the first half of the year, the worse it has done since, with baskets that more than doubled through June now showing a median second-half return of about -50% while the bottom four deciles have barely moved at all.
1
 Such is how a crowded trade unwinds.

What Makes The 2026 Stock Market Correction Different?

Here’s where the analogy starts to fall apart, and it does so in ways that cut both ways. Some differences argue that the “2018 replay” crowd is too bearish. One argues they may not be bearish enough.

The Economy Is Not Rolling Over

By the fourth quarter of 2018, growth had peaked. Real GDP slowed from a 3.4% annualized pace in the third quarter to 2.2% in the fourth.
13
 The tax-cut boost was fading, housing had stalled and the trade war was weighing on manufacturing and global trade. Markets were discounting the slowdown that showed up in 2019. Today, the data says something else entirely. Real GDP also grew at a 3.4% annualized rate in the third quarter, but there’s no fade. The Atlanta Fed’s GDPNow model tracked fourth-quarter growth at the same 3.4% pace as of November 17.
5

Recessions don’t start with the economy growing faster than 3%.
 Growth can certainly slow from here. But a strong economy isn’t the setup that produces an earnings-driven bear market. The labor market’s “low hire, low fire” pattern still reads to me as labor hoarding, not the front edge of layoffs.

Earnings Are Rising, Not Getting Cut

In late 2018, tariffs, a strong dollar and slowing Chinese growth ate into corporate guidance. Analysts cut fourth-quarter estimates 3.8% during the quarter and cut first-half 2019 estimates 4.5% between mid-October and mid-January. Negative fourth-quarter guidance outnumbered positive guidance by 72 to 33.
14
 
This time, third-quarter earnings grew 31.9%.
3
 Analysts raised fourth-quarter estimates 1.4% in October, and positive guidance outnumbered negative guidance by 43 to 32.
15
 

When prices fall while forward earnings rise, valuations compress, and that’s a very different animal than an earnings recession.

The caveat is that much of that growth is driven by the AI buildout. The market is repricing what it’s willing to pay for those earnings, which makes this a valuation and liquidity event. Valuation events can still hurt, of course. They just don’t usually require a recession to end.

Iran Is An Oil Shock. The Trade War Was A Growth Shock.

The 2018 trade war directly hit corporate margins and global growth. In September 2018, Washington imposed 10% tariffs on $200 billion of Chinese imports, with a scheduled increase to 25% on January 1, and that uncertainty froze capital spending. The Iran conflict works through a different channel. Since the war began at the end of February, disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz have kept crude prices elevated.
12
 That’s why oil has fallen only about 7% since the October peak, compared with a 41% collapse in late 2018.
1

That economic shock is now fading for consumers as gasoline futures have fallen roughly 45% between September 24 and mid-November, and the national average at the pump dropped about 13% in a month.
4
 Retail prices lag wholesale by weeks, so more relief is coming. This is the “bad inflation” I’ve argued cures itself. A supply shock raises prices without raising incomes, so demand eventually breaks the price. 
Falling gasoline works like a tax cut that lands in the first quarter, right when the 2018 analogy says the economy should be struggling.

The Fed Is The Difference That Hurts

This is the part the bulls don’t want to hear. In 2018, Jerome Powell hiked on December 19, watched stocks crater into Christmas, and turned 
“patient”
 on January 4. The “Fed put” sat close to the market because inflation was running near 2%, which gave the Fed room to back off quickly.

Today, the Fed is hiking INTO a selloff with inflation running hot. The October minutes showed almost all participants saw further increases as likely appropriate. Two even suggested a move larger than 25 basis points. Fed funds futures price roughly 75 basis points of additional tightening.
4
 The rates market agrees. Ten-year swaps have done 64% of their 2018 rally, but 2-year swaps have moved just 2 basis points, or 7% of the 2018 move.
1
 
The front end of the curve isn’t pricing a rescue.

The Fed put still exists. But with inflation running hot, its strike sits much further below the market than it did in 2018, which means the Fed probably needs to see more damage in stocks, credit, or employment before it blinks, and that damage is precisely the ingredient that turned a correction into a 20% decline eight years ago. That’s the risk to plan around.

Credit Hasn’t Confirmed, And The Hedges Failed

Garrett calls credit spreads the single largest difference between now and 2018. Investment-grade CDX is just 5 basis points wider than at its peak, or 15% of its 2018 move. High yield is 38 basis points wider, which is 28% of the 136 basis points it widened in 2018. A full replay would require roughly 98 more basis points of high-yield widening.
1
 As I’ve written before,
 
credit spreads are the market’s early warning system
, and so far that system is mostly quiet.

Meanwhile, the “safe havens” have gone the wrong way. Gold rose 5% during the 2018 decline. This time it’s down 10.8%, and the yen is weaker against the dollar.
1
 
A hedge that falls alongside the asset it’s hedging is just another long position.
 Treasuries have done their job, rallying as stocks fell, which is the behavior you’d expect in a growth scare rather than a funding crisis.

So, let’s consolidate all these points into a single table. There are certainly things we must watch as investors over the next few weeks, particularly the Fed and the credit markets. 
However, when gold and the yen get sold right alongside stocks, that’s the signature of a 
“liquidation”
 event, where investors sell what they can rather than what they want.
 Combined with rising earnings estimates, steady economic growth, and credit spreads that have barely moved, the evidence points to a de-risking sell-off more than a fundamental shift.

Who Is Left To Sell In The 2026 Stock Market Correction?

Positioning suggests the 2026 stock market correction may be friendlier than in 2018, at least in the near term. Every year, the same question arises: who still has to sell, and who has to buy? Right now the answers point in different directions depending on the cohort.

Start with the machines. Trend-following CTAs were already deeply short before last week’s leg lower. Citadel Securities put their positioning at -3.3 standard deviations as of November 4.
7
 When systematic funds are already short, most of their forced selling is behind them. They become buyers once volatility calms and trends stabilize. Such is the asymmetry that rarely gets discussed at the bottom of a flush.

Fundamental hedge funds have also cut risk hard. Goldman’s prime brokerage book saw net leverage fall 4.2 points to 73.4% in October.
8
 Morgan Stanley data shows long/short gross exposure down 10.7% since late June, with U.S. net leverage dropping five points in a single session to 42%.
9
 The concern is the multi-strategy platforms. PivotalPath estimates the ten largest run about $2.2 trillion in gross assets. The pod-based platforms are levered 8 to 14 times net capital and hold increasingly similar positions.
10
 That overlap is where a forced-selling accident could still come from.

December adds a behavioral wrinkle. For many funds, performance fees are calculated on the December 31 marks. A manager sitting on a decent year after a brutal second half has every reason to protect it rather than add risk into a falling tape. That argues for hedge fund buying power showing up in January rather than December.

Then there’s the rebalancing math. Pensions, target-date funds and balanced mandates rebalance back to fixed weights at quarter-end and year-end. With the S&P below where it began the quarter and Treasuries rallying, those portfolios are drifting underweight stocks. 
The mechanical year-end rebalance points toward buying equities and selling bonds, the opposite of the flow investors usually face after a strong year.
 Retail has also been a steady bid, with Citadel reporting 22 straight sessions of net retail buying through early November.
7

A question landing in my inbox this weekend: 
“If everyone is already sold out, doesn’t that make this the bottom?”
 Not necessarily. Light positioning indicates that forced sellers are running out of ammunition. It doesn’t tell you what happens if credit cracks or the Fed hikes 50 basis points on December 9. In 2018, sentiment was already deeply bearish by mid-December, and the S&P still fell nearly 10% over the six sessions leading up to Christmas Eve.

December Seasonality And The 2026 Stock Market Correction

As we discussed in 
Friday’s commentary
, the 
“Santa Claus rally” 
is real, but most investors get the timing wrong. Goldman’s data suggest that roughly 80% of the average year-end gain occurs after December 14. Since 2010, the December 5 to 14 window has averaged a 0.27% loss and finished higher in only 7 of 16 years. In 2011, 2014, 2015, and 2018, that window ranged from 3% to 3.6%. In years when the VIX started mid-November at 18 or higher, the median drawdown in the window was 3%, but the average year-end gain was still 1.34%.
4

The end-of-year calendar explains why. First, the Fed meets for the last time this year on December 8th and 9th. Secondly, the corporate buyback window is expected to close around December 13th, followed by quarterly options expiration on December 18th. Lastly, mutual fund capital-gains record dates and tax-loss selling cluster in the same stretch, and this year there are plenty of AI losses to harvest. 
Early December has tended to be a sell-the-rally period, while mid-December weakness has been the opportunity.

This week will cram five trading days into three as the Thanksgiving holiday approaches. Thanksgiving weeks are always the wildcard. With three full sessions plus a Friday half-day, liquidity thins out, and in a market where dealers are short gamma and the options market implies a 3% move through next Friday. 
A thin tape can produce a rally that looks like a bottom and a drop that looks like a crash inside the same week. Don’t trust either one.

Is The 2026 Stock Market Correction A Warning Of Something Bigger?

Okay, let’s get to the one question that matters. 
Corrections are the price of admission for owning stocks. 
The danger is when the correction is the market’s early warning of a structural change. So I’m watching two things: the technical clock, and four channels that could turn this into something worse.

The clock is the 200-day moving average. In 
our analysis of 34 first breaks sin
c
e 1950
, 18 were back above the average four weeks later and averaged a 4.4% gain over the following month. The other 16 averaged a 3.5% decline. The uncomfortable part is that all eight breaks that fell 2.9% or more below the average within four sessions were still below it at week four. This one hit 6% in four sessions.
3

Liquidity: Stressed, But Functioning
: Currently, there is no question that market liquidity is
 
impaired. The VIX curve is the most inverted since April 2025, and dealers are short gamma. The S&P has twice realized an entire week’s implied move in a single session.
2
 
That’s equity plumbing under strain. What turns a positioning flush into a crisis is when the stress migrates into credit and funding markets. We aren’t seeing that yet.

Recession: Not In The Data
 : Growth above 3%, earnings growth above 30% and falling gasoline prices are not the ingredients of a recession over the next two quarters. Could that change? Of course. But a recession call today requires forecasting a shock rather than reading the data.

Credit: The One To Watch
: Headline spreads are calm, but the bottom of the ladder isn’t. As Michael Lebowitz showed in 
his recent look at credit spreads
,
 CCC spreads near 1,307 basis points have widened more than 500 basis points since early September. Meanwhile, BB spreads remain near the bottom of their 20-year range.
11
 The October Fed minutes also flagged AI-related debt growing through “complex and opaque financing structures,” with concentration, off-balance-sheet leasing and circular financing as potential loss amplifiers.
4
 
If stress spreads from CCC into B and BB debt, the 2018 map is back in play.

The Fed: The Active Risk
: This is the channel with a date attached. A 50-basis-point hike on December 9 into a falling market with credit starting to widen is how a correction becomes something larger. The other tell is Treasuries. As long as bonds rally when stocks fall, the market is pricing a growth scare. If Treasuries sell off alongside stocks, the market is pricing a policy mistake, and that’s a far more dangerous phase.

“The 2018 analogy is right about the price and wrong about the cause. Until credit or the Fed confirms it, treat this as a correction and treat every rally as a rental.”

6,294

Where the S&P 500 lands if the full 2018 path plays out, roughly 9% below Friday's close of 6,910.

So which is it? Today, the evidence says the 2026 stock market correction is just that, a correction within an ongoing bull market. The economy is growing, earnings are rising, and credit hasn’t confirmed the equity panic. However, I’d hold that view loosely. The Fed has taken away the fast rescue that ended 2018, and the CCC market is already showing the kind of stress that spreads if nobody steps in.

Rules For Handling The 2026 Stock Market Correction

The obvious question from this analysis is “
Why not just sell everything and wait for clarity?”
 The problem, as always, is 
our own behavioral flaws.
 
Clarity always shows up after the bottom, at higher prices.
 Selling everything 12% off the highs locks in the damage and leaves you guessing about re-entry. Rules work better than reactions, so here are the ones we’re following.

One last thought. 
Bob Farrell’s Rule #9
 warns that when all the experts and forecasts agree, something else will happen. 
Right now, every desk on Wall Street agrees that this is 2018. That consensus alone makes me skeptical of a clean replay, in either direction.
 The rules aren’t a timing tool. They’re a reminder to stay humble when the crowd is certain.

Bottom Line

The 2018 selloff ended when credit broke, and the Fed blinked. Until one of those happens in the 2026 stock market correction, treat every rally as a chance to rebalance.

A bounce is likely coming, probably sooner than most expect. Whether it marks the bottom depends on two markets most stock investors never look at.

Questions This Article Answers

Is the 2026 stock market correction like the 2018 selloff?

The price action is similar. The S&P 500 is down 11.6% from its October 6 record, versus a 19.8% decline in late 2018. However, credit spreads, short-term rates and safe havens have not followed the 2018 pattern, and the economy and earnings are far stronger than they were in late 2018.

What was the biggest difference between 2018 and today?

The Federal Reserve. In 2018, the Fed signaled patience within 16 days of its December hike. In 2026, the Fed is hiking into hot inflation, and futures price roughly 75 basis points of further tightening, so investors shouldn’t count on a quick rescue.

Will year-end rebalancing help the stock market?

Probably at the margin. Pensions and balanced funds that rebalance to fixed weights are likely underweight stocks after the decline, which favors equity buying into December 31. CTAs are already short, which limits further systematic selling. Hedge funds protecting annual returns may wait until January to add risk.

What would signal that the 2026 stock market correction is turning into something bigger?

Watch three things: high-yield spreads widening by roughly another 100 basis points, the S&P 500 still below its 200-day average at the December 16 close, and Treasuries selling off alongside stocks rather than rallying.

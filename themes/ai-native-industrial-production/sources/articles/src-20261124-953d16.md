Hadrian launched five years ago in San Francisco with a team of eight. But as the AI manufacturing startup’s technology took shape, it went to Southern California and Arizona to build its factories. 
Now with more than 
$1.37 billion in funding
 (opens in new tab)
, the company is set to return to the Bay Area — with a 1 million-square-foot campus in South San Francisco. 
“San Francisco is where the world’s best engineers come to build what’s next,” the company 
posted on X
 (opens in new tab)
 Monday, along with a glossy promotional video.
ADVERTISEMENT
Hadrian is part of a wave of venture-backed startups pitching themselves as a faster alternative to the defense supply chain, which feeds giants like Lockheed Martin and Northrop Grumman, which have long relied on small, manual machine shops. Hadrian produces components for missile, fighter jet, space, and shipbuilding programs, the company says.
Founder and CEO Chris Power said the factory and engineering campus will open in January, adding that 100 employees are already in the area. “Time to drag your desk to the factory floor,” he posted on X.

The campus will be at the new Southline development, whose first phase is set to be completed next year by owners Beacon Capital Partners and Lane Partners, according to renderings and sources familiar with the deal. 
The 31-acre science and technology development is located near BART’s South San Francisco station and within walking distance of Caltrain. Once fully built, it will have 2.8 million square feet of office and life science space — which, with its high ceilings and open floorplates, can be converted to manufacturing. 
Beacon, Lane, and listing brokers at Newmark declined to comment.

Southline will be Hadrian’s fifth facility. This year, the company opened a factory in Alabama to produce parts for submarines. 
As AI applications push further into the physical world, Hadrian and other companies boast that their software can simplify or automate processes such as product drawings, inspections, and manufacturing. Historically, it would take months or years for factories to redesign lines and retrain staff to accommodate new product designs. The startup claims its AI tools can train workers within 30 days and make them 10 times more productive. Its workforce includes former executives who have 
never set foot on a factory floor
 (opens in new tab)
.

According to its careers page, Hadrian is hiring for engineering and mechanical design roles. A job posting said the company is looking for candidates who are “AI-native and relentless about it.” 
Since the nation’s top frontier labs, venture capitalists, and tech incubators are based in San Francisco, entrepreneurs focused on AI robotics have been flocking to the city to join the growing ecosystem. But the city has limited industrial space, so firms tend to scale production elsewhere once their businesses get to that point. 
Robotics firms such as Bright Machines were founded in San Francisco but moved elsewhere once they got too big.

Even when robotics firms outgrow San Francisco, there are startups — and even tech giants — that are eager to backfill their space. Last month, the landlord at an industrial building in the Mission sued Physical Intelligence for not vacating after its lease expired, 
blocking Anthropic from moving in
.
Meanwhile, real estate investment firm Cannar Partners has spent the past year amassing buildings in Dogpatch and Showplace Square 
to cater to robotics startups
, the company told The Standard.

South San Francisco and nearby Burlingame, Brisbane, and Redwood City have more space to accommodate industrial projects. Developers during the previous decade raced to build campuses in response to the life sciences boom of the 2010s. When federal funding slowed down for those companies after the pandemic, many landlords tried to reposition their properties to office and manufacturing. 
Last year, a newly built life science building in South San Francisco was 
leased to Elon Musk’s Neuralink
.

Anyone who has ever compared coco fiber-based substrate products may find themselves looking at several seemingly identical offers that, once opened on the farm, turn out to perform very differently. Anthony Alexander, Head of Regional Operations at Avee Cocoseller, a manufacturer and supplier of coco fiber-based growing substrates, says this kind of surprise almost always comes down to a poorly defined quote, a conviction shaped by his work with growers and distributors across North America. "Many problems start before planting, when the quote is requested," he says.
A common scenario plays out when two suppliers both offer "a bag of coco" but deliver products that have little in common, one may be washed and buffered while the other is only washed, or one may express volume in dry liters while the other uses expanded liters. "When those details aren't specified, the comparison comes down to price. That's why it's worth sending every supplier the same list covering crop, format, volume, mix, treatment, drainage holes, packaging, and delivery location," Anthony explains.

Format is determined by irrigation
The choice between a horizontal bag, known in the industry as a slab, and an open-top bag depends on how the crop is irrigated and how plants are spaced. Slabs are widely used for tomato, cucumber, and pepper, with multiple plants per bag on a gutter, while open-top bags are a better fit when each plant is grown individually or in a pot, as is the case with berries and nursery crops. The key question to ask is: "How many plants per bag, and how is irrigation managed?"
When it comes to volume, what matters is the root space available, not the size of the compressed block that arrives at the warehouse. Expanded dimensions, plants per unit, and support space should all be confirmed in writing. "A tall, narrow bag doesn't perform the same as a short, wide one, and either way it needs to fit the gutter or bench the grower already has in place," Anthony of Avee Cocoseller notes.
Mix, drainage, and treatment
The ratio of pith to chips determines the balance between water retention and aeration. Pith, being finer, holds moisture, while chips, being coarser, provide air at the cost of more frequent irrigation. "There is no single recipe," Anthony explains, "because the right mix varies with the crop and the climate. On top of that, drainage holes need to match the irrigation system, and it's worth thoroughly hydrating the bag and checking its drainage before planting."

Washing and buffering account for much of the confusion in this area. Coco can carry naturally occurring salts, which washing removes to reduce electrical conductivity (EC). Buffering, typically with calcium, prevents the substrate from locking up calcium and magnesium at the expense of the plant during the first few weeks.
"Washed does not mean buffered." In practice, it is advisable to request each treatment separately and agree on a maximum acceptable EC upfront. Avee Cocoseller uses triple washing, buffering with calcium nitrate, a pH of 5.5 to 6.5, and a target EC below 0.5 mS/cm.
An EC or pH value only means something if the measurement method and the batch it refers to are known, since the same substrate can produce different figures depending on the technique used.
From pallet to on-farm trial
When purchasing by pallet, it is advisable to confirm in writing the number of units, dimensions, weight, packaging, and unloading method, whether by forklift, from a loading dock, or by hand, as well as the number of pallets per truck or container and who is responsible for freight.
On arrival, Anthony recommends counting pallets and units, checking that packaging is neither torn nor wet, reviewing labels and batch numbers, opening a few bags at random, and hydrating one or two to measure EC and pH. Photographing the delivery and noting any issues on the delivery note is also worthwhile.
These are "fifteen minutes that save a lot of problems." Before committing to a large order, it pays to run a trial in a small area of the farm using the same water and irrigation system alongside the existing substrate, recording EC, pH, drainage, and root development, and then ordering in writing exactly what was trialed.

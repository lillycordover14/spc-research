Can we measure the effect of being excluded on long-term outcomes? Our learning from testing instrumental variables
 
By 
Dave Thomson, Veruska Oppedisiano, Luyang Chen
|

25th November 2026

This project was funded by the Nuffield Foundation, but the views expressed are those of the authors and not necessarily those of the Foundation

We know that pupils who are permanently excluded from school tend to do badly later on. The 
Timpson Review
 found that just 7% of permanently excluded pupils went on to get five or more A*-C grades at GCSE in 2015/16. 
Our own earlier work
 found much lower attainment at ages 16, 19 and 21 among young people who had been excluded or attended alternative provision.

What we know much less about is how much of this is caused by exclusion itself. Excluded pupils differ from their peers in ways we can see in the data, such as disadvantage and special educational needs, and in ways we can’t, such as what is happening at home. Those hidden factors could drive both exclusion and poor outcomes.

For obvious reasons, we can’t estimate the impact of exclusion by running a randomised controlled trial of exclusion. So, as is often the case, we’re left with trying to make the best of observational data. Recent studies completed by others have used matching, sibling comparisons and other methods, each with its strengths and weaknesses. In a project funded by the 
Nuffield Foundation
, we have been trying a different approach: instrumental variables.

The full report can be found 
here
.

What we set out to do

We are studying three events:

Permanent exclusion
, where the pupil is removed from the school roll and the local authority becomes responsible for their education

A managed move
,
 a voluntary arrangement between the pupil, their parents and two schools, often used to avoid an exclusion

Placement in an alternative provision (AP) school
 after exclusion, rather than in another mainstream school

For each, we want to know the effect on education, employment and benefits, offending, and health, up to age 21.

We are using three datasets that link the National Pupil Database to justice (NPD-MoJ), education and employment (LEO), and health (ECHILD) records. Our study population is around six million young people born between 1991/92 and 1999/2000.

Phase one tested whether instrumental variables could work and, if so, which one(s).

What is an instrumental variable?

An instrument is something that changes a pupil’s chances of being excluded (or managed moved, or placed in AP) but has no other link to how their life turns out. Find one, and differences in outcomes between pupils nudged towards exclusion by it and similar pupils who weren’t can be put down to exclusion itself.

The textbook example is 
Joshua Angrist’s study
 of the Vietnam war draft lottery. Whether a young American man was drafted depended on a randomly drawn number, which had nothing to do with his later earnings except through military service.

A usable instrument has to pass two tests:

Relevance.
 It must have a strong enough effect on the probability of exclusion. We used the usual rule of thumb that the F-statistic from the first-stage regression should be at least 10.

The exclusion restriction
 (no pun intended). It must affect outcomes only through its effect on exclusion. This can’t be proved from the data, so it rests on argument, backed up by some checks.

The downside of IV estimates is that they only apply to borderline cases, that is to say pupils whose exclusion depends on the instrument. A pupil caught with a weapon will almost always be excluded, so the results won’t cover them.

What we tested, and what we found

We looked at four types of instrument. We judged each on whether there was a convincing reason to expect it to work, whether earlier research backed it up, and what our data showed.

Academisation.
 
Previous research
 has found that exclusions rise after schools become academies. We looked at pupils who were in Year 11 when their school converted, having been at the same school in Year 10. We chose these because we thought they could plausibly be said to not in themselves influence outcomes, but could influence whether an exclusion would occur. We also looked at schools changing trust. Neither had a strong enough effect on exclusions in our data.

Timing of incident.
 We would have expected that managed moves are easier to arrange early in the school year, when there is more time to find a receiving school. That is what we found. Among pupils who were either excluded or managed moved, a summer-term incident made a managed move less likely than in Autumn or Spring in Years 7 to 10 (F-statistics between 17 and 43). Year 11 was the odd one out: there, a summer-term incident made a managed move more likely, perhaps because schools are reluctant to exclude pupils so close to their GCSEs. As a check, summer-term timing had no link with managed moves among pupils already in AP, for whom a move wasn’t really an option.

Local AP capacity.
 When AP places nearby fill up, a school may have less scope to exclude. We measured the change over the year in the number of AP places filled in each year group within 10km of a school, leaving out that school’s own excluded pupils. A rise in filled places reduced the chances of exclusion in Years 7 to 10 (F-statistics between 20 and 46). It didn’t work for Year 11, but most exclusions happen in Years 8 to 10. As a sensitivity test, we checked that subsequent changes in filled places did not meaningfully predict past exclusions, suggesting the instrument isn’t just picking up local trends.

Distance to the nearest AP school.
 We expected pupils living further from an AP school to be less likely to be placed there after exclusion. But neither distance, local AP capacity nor timing predicted whether an excluded pupil went on to AP or to another mainstream school. One reason may be that around four in five excluded pupils were next seen in an AP school, leaving little variation to work with.

What next?

So we have instruments that look usable for two of our three questions. We haven’t found one for the effect of AP placement, so we are setting that question aside for now.

In phase two, we will estimate the effects of permanent exclusion and of managed moves on education, employment and benefits, and offending, including violent offending. We will also look at how these effects vary by year group and by SEND status.

Gaps in the ECHILD health data, including missing enrolment records and no way of adding school locations, mean we won’t be estimating the effect of exclusion on health outcomes.

We’ll report back when we have results.

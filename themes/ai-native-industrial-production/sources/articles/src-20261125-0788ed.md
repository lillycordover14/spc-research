Boltz Raises $110M to Put Molecular-Design AI Within Researchers’ Reach&nbsp;&nbsp;citybiz

[page text]
Boltz co-founders (from l), Gabriele Corso, Jeremy Wohlwend and Saro Passaro. (Courtesy: MIT)

Boltz
 
has 
raised $110 million in a Series A round led by Bessemer Venture Partners
, as the startup seeks to make advanced molecular-design tools easier to use in drug research.

Existing backers 
Andreessen Horowitz, Amplify Partners, Zetta Venture Partners and Obvious Ventures
 increased their investments. 
Greycroft, F-Prime Capital, Hummingbird and Benchling
 joined the round, according to Boltz. The company raised $28 million in seed financing earlier this year, Tech.eu reported.

Founded in 2025 by researchers connected to MIT, Boltz has grown to 40 scientists and engineers. Its work builds on open-source models including Boltz-1 for predicting molecular structures, Boltz-2 for estimating binding strength and BoltzGen for protein design. The company later introduced proprietary models BoltzMol-1 and BoltzProt-1, along with an API.

Among the co-founders, CEO
 Gabriele Corso
 earned his PhD in AI from MIT CSAIL and his BA in Computer Science from the University of Cambridge. CTO 
Jeremy Wohlwend
 holds BS, MEng, and PhD degrees in computer science and machine learning from MIT, while 
Saro Passaro
, who is head of research at Boltz, worked alongside Corso and Wohlwend in MIT labs.

Boltz is also developing Boltz Lab, a platform launched in January that gives scientists and AI agents access to its models through a collaborative workspace, software connections or automated workflows. The company says researchers use the system for tasks ranging from validating biological targets to optimizing drug candidates.

The platform is intended to address a practical obstacle in computational drug discovery: many biologists and chemists lack the time or specialized expertise to operate and maintain complex models as new experimental data becomes available. Boltz says it provides the computing infrastructure, allowing customers to avoid building and managing their own systems. Its stated pricing includes monthly credits for hundreds of predictions, followed by usage-based charges that can start at a few cents per prediction. The company says customers retain ownership of their work and that their data is not used to train its models.

Boltz says paid customers’ molecular-token usage has increased 50-fold since June and that it has partnerships with six of the world’s 20 largest pharmaceutical companies. Those include Pfizer, GSK, Takeda Pharmaceutical and Bristol Myers Squibb.

In a separate agreement announced in October, Boltz and Bristol Myers Squibb said they would deploy Boltz Lab and proprietary models on BMS’s NVIDIA DGX SuperPOD computing infrastructure. The companies also plan to develop customized models informed by BMS research data and expertise. BMS said its scientists had already used Boltz’s open-source models.

Bessemer has framed the investment as backing a broader shift toward computational approaches that could help researchers explore molecular possibilities more efficiently. But Boltz’s product and performance claims come from the company; the models’ reported research results do not establish that generated candidates will become medicines.

Boltz says it will use the new capital to expand computing and data resources, meet demand from partners and grow its team. Its longer-term goal is to help scientists move from a therapeutic hypothesis toward a molecule suitable for human use through computational design.

After Demonstrator Flights, AFRL Exercises $10.9M Option on Ursa Major’s Draper Hypersonic Engine
 

 

Ursa Major
, a weapons and space systems company based in Berthoud, Colorado, will keep developing its Draper engine after the
 Air Force Research Laboratory
 (AFRL) exercised a $10.9 million option on the company’s contract. The option follows successful flights of the Affordable Rapid Missile Demonstrator under that contract. 

Draper is a storable liquid rocket engine designed for hypersonic applications. Ursa Major builds its systems with additive manufacturing (AM), adaptable design, and flexible production methods as it expands U.S. capacity to produce critical weapons and space hardware.

Draper and Ursa Major’s Hypersonics Lineup

“Draper is at the heart of our hypersonics work, and AFRL and the United States Air Force have been partners from day one,” said Jason Meredith, President of Ursa Major. “This award keeps that work progressing, and it moves us closer to putting real capability in the hands of the warfighter.”

Ursa Major’s hypersonics programs also include the Hadley liquid rocket engine, which powers the Department of War’s (DoW) hypersonic test bed, and the HAVOC Missile System, an all-up-round for hypersonic and long-range precision strike. The company reports more than a dozen flights of its hypersonic systems.

Ursa Major’s Hadley liquid rocket engine. Photo via Ursa Major.

Hypersonic Systems and AM Production

Ursa Major’s
 hypersonics
 programs include Draper, which has flown with AFRL as a storable, tactical engine. The Hadley liquid rocket engine powers the Department of War’s hypersonic test bed. The HAVOC Missile System is the company’s all-up-round, a complete munition for hypersonic and long-range precision strike missions. Ursa Major reports more than a dozen flights of its hypersonic systems.

Since it was founded in 2015, the company has supplied hypersonic and long-range munitions, solid rocket motors, and space mobility systems to the United States and its allies. Ursa Major combines AM with adaptable designs and flexible production methods. Its headquarters are in Berthoud, and it runs manufacturing operations in Youngstown, Ohio.

Military Contracts and Flight Testing for AM Propulsion

Ursa Major is developing Draper under an AFRL contract that has already included flight testing. Other companies that build propulsion with AM have also flown engines under U.S. military contracts, or are funded to fly them.

Beehive Industries
 secured a $29.7 million 
U.S. Air Force contract
, reported in May 2026. It covers flight testing, vehicle integration, and qualification of the Frenzy 8, a 200 lbf additively manufactured jet engine for uncrewed aerial defense systems. Like Ursa Major’s AFRL contract, the award includes options, in this case for further testing and flight demonstration of the 100 lbf Frenzy 6. The funds go through the SOSSEC consortium to the Air Force Life Cycle Management Center’s Small Expendable Turbine prototyping effort.

In 2025,
 Firehawk Aerospace
, a defense energetics manufacturer, flew its AM GMLRS-class Firehawk Analog (GFA), a 
3D printed hybrid rocket engine system
, above 18,000 feet and past the speed of sound. The flight was the first in a planned series of demonstrations under the company’s Phase III SBIR
 contract
 with the Army Applications Laboratory (AAL), and Javelin-class and Stinger-class analogs are planned next. As with Ursa Major’s demonstrator, the flight took place under a military contract, but the customer is the Army and the engine is a hybrid system.

Across the three programs, flight work sits at different points: Draper’s demonstrator had already flown before AFRL exercised its option, Beehive’s options cover flights still ahead, and Firehawk has completed the first flight in its planned series.

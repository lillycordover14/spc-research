Deterrence methods help curb wolf-livestock conflicts

      11/25/26  6:01 AM
    

      By 
Tim Hearden

No Comments

A game camera captured a gray wolf from the Lassen pack among a herd of cattle in July 2022. (UC Davis photo)

California recorded 79 confirmed or probable livestock depredations by wolves in the first nine months of 2026, less than half the 198 confirmed or probable depredations in all of 2025, according to the state Department of Fish and Wildlife.

The decrease follows CDFW’s removal of four adult wolves and relocation of pups from the destructive Beyem Seyo pack last fall after the pack was responsible for killing at least 90 cattle in the Sierra Valley. In 2026 conflict has shifted largely to Siskiyou and Lassen counties.

Stepped-up deterrence methods and better communication among ranchers, conservationists and state officials have helped curb livestock depredation by gray wolves in California’s high country, a University of California livestock adviser says.
California recorded 79 confirmed or probable livestock depredations by wolves in the first nine months of 2026, less than half the 198 confirmed or probable depredations in all of 2025, 
according to
 the state Department of Fish and Wildlife.
The decrease follows CDFW’s removal of four adult wolves and relocation of pups from the destructive Beyem Seyo pack last fall after the pack was responsible for killing at least 90 cattle in the Sierra Valley. In 2026 conflict has shifted largely to Siskiyou and Lassen counties.
This year CDFW has held numerous agency and community meetings and ranch tours in wolf country, including trainings on how to use new nonlethal deterrence methods authorized as part of the second phase of California’s decade-old wolf recovery plan.
Tracy Schohr, UCCE’s livestock and natural resources adviser in Plumas and Sierra counties, said collaboration has been key to reducing impacts in the Sierra Valley.
“The ranchers showed up [to meetings] and that’s not something that can be taken lightly,” Schohr said. “The state came in with a huge response and the ranchers said yes. … That is a big step.”
Schohr made her remarks during a presentation on the community approach to wolf-livestock conflict at the California Association of Resource Conservation Districts’ conference last week in Sacramento. Her talk covered lessons learned, community engagement strategies, and the evolving landscape of wolf-livestock conflict.
More than a decade after a collared wolf dubbed OR-7 became the first known gray wolf to wander into California in nearly a century, the state ended 2025 with a minimum population of 55 wolves, a 10% increase over 2024, despite 12 confirmed wolf mortalities throughout the year.
California’s gray wolves have 
produced
 at least 42 pups in eight litters in 2026, more than in any year since the first pack formed in 2015. CDFW’s October report also showed 10 packs, down from 12, and livestock losses at about half of 2025’s pace. Gray wolves are listed as endangered in California and federally, meaning they couldn’t be harmed or chased away while their populations were becoming established.

Stepped-up deterrence
Earlier this year, state wildlife officials and ranching partners 
expanded nonlethal conflict mitigation
, including paintball projection and sustained pursuit. USDA and state wildlife experts have conducted numerous training sessions in Sierra, Lassen, Siskiyou and other counties.
The state’s enhanced efforts followed two University of California studies in 2025 that opened the eyes of policymakers — 
one
 that suggested cattle are a major component of wolves’ diet, and 
another
 that found wolf-related costs in the Sierra Valley reached at least $2.6 million over a seven-month period last year.
Cattlemen and local officials have also used public safety as a rallying cry. Last year, Sierra and four other counties — Modoc, Lassen, Plumas and Shasta — declared wolves a threat to people after some showed the absence of a natural fear of humans.
“The concern was wolves in other states aren’t seen, but in the Sierra Valley they’re seen in daylight,” Schohr said, noting that a depredation occurred five miles from a school with an ag program that included lambs and sheep. “It was changing a community.”
Schohr noted that ranchers were willing to discuss the stress they were under, “and that doesn’t happen a lot.” She also noted that neighbors who previously didn’t get along were willing to help each other. “There hasn’t been a kill in the Sierra Valley in months, but the ranchers are still engaged,” she said.
Schohr urged local officials to work with ranchers early on detections. California lawmakers approved a 
budget trailer bill
 this year that provides $5 million in new funding to help support ranchers dealing with wolf conflicts, including $2.5 million to pay livestock owners whose animals were killed by wolves.
The other $2.5 million will pay for investigations of suspected wolf attacks on livestock, collaring wolves, or assessing wolf activity in new areas and keeping livestock owners up to date. Schohr said the state was collaring wolves before the mid-November storms halted the activity.
She said ranchers are compensated at fair market value, but funding for things like range riders is lacking.

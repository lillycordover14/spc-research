The NCAA has eliminated its mandatory pregame handshake for coaches in the women's basketball tournament following an ugly confrontation between two of the sport's biggest names. But the coaches at the center of the controversy shook hands anyway Tuesday night.
 
 
UConn coach
 Geno Auriemma and South Carolina coach Dawn Staley exchanged a brief handshake before their highly anticipated rematch at Mohegan Sun Arena in Connecticut, their first meeting since a heated argument at the Final Four in April.
The moment drew applause from the sold-out crowd, with spectators holding up their phones to capture the interaction. The NCAA's official women's basketball social media account celebrated the reunion, posting video of the handshake with the caption, "Dawn 🤝 Geno WE BACK‼️"

The exchange came just days after the 
NCAA Division I women's basketball committee
 announced it would no longer require opposing head coaches to meet at midcourt and shake hands before tournament games, ending a longstanding protocol intended to promote sportsmanship.
 
"The committee approved a policy change to no longer require coaches to shake hands pregame," read a Nov. 20 memo obtained by USA TODAY Sports and other outlets. "The committee encourages coaches to shake hands informally before team introductions."
The change applies to NCAA Tournament games, rather than Tuesday's regular-season showcase.
 
The decision follows one of the most controversial coaching confrontations in recent 
women's college basketball
 history.

During the April 3 national semifinal, Staley's Gamecocks defeated Auriemma's Huskies 62-48, ending UConn's perfect season and 54-game winning streak. But the attention quickly shifted away from South Carolina's upset victory when Auriemma approached Staley near the Gamecocks' bench in the final seconds.
 
 
The two coaches exchanged heated words before members of their coaching staffs and game officials intervened. Auriemma then left the court without participating in the customary postgame handshake line.
Auriemma later explained that he was frustrated because Staley had not met him at midcourt for the scheduled pregame handshake after the starting lineups were introduced.
"For 41 years I've been coaching, 25 Final Fours and before the game, the protocol is you meet at halfcourt," Auriemma told reporters afterward.
 
 
"And I waited there for like three minutes. So it is what it is."
 
However, television footage and photographs showed that the two coaches had already shaken hands earlier during pregame warmups. The dispute concerned a separate handshake required under the NCAA Tournament's formal protocol.
Staley insisted she had not intentionally disrespected Auriemma.
 
"I'm of integrity. I'm of integrity," Staley told ESPN after the game. "So if I did something wrong to Geno, I have no idea what I did."
 
 
She added that she had greeted UConn's coaching staff before the game and did not understand what prompted the confrontation.
The argument also followed Auriemma's criticism of the officiating during the game. In a heated interview with ESPN sideline reporter Holly Rowe, he complained about the physical play and accused Staley of influencing officials by repeatedly protesting calls.
 
The incident overshadowed South Carolina's victory over the defending national champions. The Gamecocks ultimately fell 
to UCLA
 79-51 in the national championship game two days later.
Auriemma initially apologized
 for his behavior in a statement the following day, acknowledging that his actions were inappropriate and had detracted from South Carolina's performance.
 
He issued a second apology on April 7 after speaking directly with Staley.
 
"I've lost more games in the Final Four than any coach in history. But Friday I lost something more important. I lost myself," Auriemma said.
"Women’s basketball deserved better. My university, my athletes, my former players and our fans deserved better."
 
Staley publicly accepted the apology and called for the controversy to end.
 
 
Dawn Staley of the South Carolina Gamecocks argues with Geno Auriemma of the UConn Huskies during the second half of an NCAA Women's Final Four semifinal game at Mortgage Matchup Center in Phoenix, Arizona on April 3, 2026.

"One moment doesn't define a career and it doesn't change the impact he's had on growing women's basketball," she said in an April statement.
Despite their reconciliation, Auriemma acknowledged ahead of Tuesday's rematch that he and Staley had not spoken in several months.
The UConn coach also appeared largely indifferent to the NCAA's decision to eliminate the handshake requirement.
 
"Doesn't matter," Auriemma said Sunday when asked about the change. "Whatever the rules are, you just try to go by what the rules are. Everybody's there to win a game. That's the bottom line."
 
Staley, meanwhile, said she would continue shaking hands with opposing coaches regardless of whether the NCAA required it.
"I'm a rules follower, so I'm going to follow the rules, but my pregame routine is always to go shake the coach's hand, so I'm going to continue to do that," Staley told reporters Monday.
 
 
She also suggested clearer instructions from the NCAA could have prevented the April confrontation.

"If the NCAA was clear on what the protocol was for that national semifinals game, maybe or maybe not we wouldn't be in this situation," Staley said. "Now it's clear. Now we have an understanding."
 
The handshake change is among several adjustments to the women's NCAA Tournament, which will expand from 68 to 76 teams in 2027. The NCAA is also 
eliminating mandatory news conferences
 and open locker-room access for media members between the Sweet 16 and Elite Eight.
The handshake controversy has added another chapter to a rivalry between two of the most successful coaches in women's basketball history.
Auriemma has led 
UConn to 12 national championships
, including its victory over South Carolina in the 2025 championship game. Staley has won three national titles with the Gamecocks and has established South Carolina as one of the sport's premier programs.

Their latest meeting featured No. 1 South Carolina against No. 7 UConn, with both programs facing a demanding early-season schedule.
 
Auriemma had insisted that the players, rather than the coaches, deserved the spotlight.
"I'm not playing, so I'm just trying to coach my team because they're the ones playing," he said ahead of the matchup. "And that's what people are coming to watch, I think. They're coming to watch South Carolina and Connecticut play against each other."

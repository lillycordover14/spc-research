After storms, Candlestick Park floods, once again&nbsp;&nbsp;Mission Local

[page text]
Tuesday morning’s clear skies were a relief to fears that king tides plus an expected downpour would once again flood city streets. But along the Bayview-Hunters Point shoreline surrounding Candlestick State Park, workers were still pumping out stormwater from the week before. 

The water along the roadway of the long-closed Candlestick Park stadium and Hunters Point Expressway has been under several feet of water since last Wednesday. If El Niño’s rain continues as predicted, the park could remain flooded for weeks. 

“It probably won’t be cleared up until El Niño is over,” said a worker pumping out water that had pooled into a murky-brown lake covering the Hunters Point Expressway. The expressway, which once connected the park to Highway 101, closed in 2023 due to sea-level rise and persistent flooding. Since then the state park has used a temporary, alternative entrance on Gilman Avenue, which on Tuesday, was also still partially flooded.

The workers, equipped with three pumps, are contracted by 
FivePoint
, a developer that is slated to turn much of the land surrounding the park into mixed-income housing that is scheduled to complete its first properties by 2030, with buildout expected through 2048. The project broke ground in early September, and FivePoint plans to install a permanent pumping system to drain the stormwater and protect the future development. But for now, the developer — and the park — are relying on a temporary solution: three pumps, each under two feet tall.

A pump draining water from a flooded Gilman Avenue at Candlestick Point Recreation Area on Tuesday, Nov. 24, 2026. Photo by Bailey Zitko.

In a statement, FivePoint said that the company had “volunteered to support the city’s water removal efforts at the site,” despite the area being “city-owned and managed.” While the state retains ownership over the park, the Hunters Point Expressway and its surrounding streets remain under the jurisdiction of the city until much of the land is ceded to the developer. 

The stormwater, which could possibly contain raw sewage, toxic chemicals, heavy metals, and physical debris, poses a health risk to park-goers and wildlife. 

While Candlestick State Park encompasses 252 acres of parkland, it is surrounded by an RV park, businesses, and apartment complexes that could also contribute to polluted stormwater. 

A spokesperson for San Francisco’s Department of Public Health said that the area’s stormwater should be “avoided.”

“We encourage residents to avoid walking or driving through flooded areas, even when the water appears shallow,” said the department. “Residents should avoid contact with floodwater wherever possible and wash thoroughly with soap and water if exposure occurs.” 

Flooding near Candlestick Park isn’t new. The park, which occupies the site of 
former baylands
 turned landfill, 
experiences periodic flooding
 nearly every winter during San Francisco’s rainy season. But this year, a supersized El Niño could make flooding much worse, leaving stormwater standing for days. 

When Candlestick Park stadium was operating, from April 1960 until its final event in August 2014, the area had two permanent pumps to pump stormwater that flooded the area into the bay. 
Even then
, in heavy rain, flooding sometimes blocked football fans from making it to the game. After the stadium closed, the pumps were never replaced. 

San Francisco’s Office of Community Investment and Infrastructure, which has worked with FivePoint to reduce the flooding, did not respond to multiple requests for comment by publishing time. San Francisco Water Power and Sewer, which operates San Francisco’s stormwater system, also did not respond to requests for comment. 

Rachel Norton, the director of the nonprofit the California State Parks Foundation, said that the foundation has pushed for the city to prevent or pump out stormwater flooding near the park for years. Two winters prior, Norton said, a city crew arrived and pumped floodwaters into the bay in a matter of days after the nonprofit contacted them about it. 

 

 

Part of the reason the flooding isn’t swiftly dealt with, said Norton, is that the area is a “kind of no man’s land.” The only residential areas that are in direct proximity to the flooding are Candlestick RV park and Alice Griffith Apartments, a nearby low-income housing project. 

Gilman Avenue, the alternate entrance into the park, is partially “unaccepted” — meaning that it was never adopted as a city or state road. Unaccepted streets, 
roughly a quarter of which
 are in Bayview-Hunters Point, are not regularly cleaned by the Department of Public Works, and are hot-spots for illegal dumping. That, too, can spread pollution when an area floods. 

“For a lack of a better word, it’s been forgotten,” said Norton. “There aren’t many neighbors yelling at the city to clean it up,” she said. 

On Tuesday afternoon, flooded areas near the park were strewn with trash and debris, like plastic canisters, beer bottles, and plastic bags. 

“It doesn’t smell very good, and I certainly wouldn’t want my kids wading around in it,” said Norton. “God knows what’s in there.” 

 

 

But Bart Grant, a senior state parks and recreation specialist, said he’s more concerned about what happens as king tides continue to surge. There is a long-term plan to raise the elevation of the park to protect it from rising sea-levels, Grant said. Until then, heavy waves could “very likely” wash away the park’s already eroding beaches. 

As FivePoint’s development continues, it will bring gutters, sewer systems, and an outflow that will push floodwaters into the bay when storms surge. “That’s the long-term solution,” said Grant. 

“But until then,” he said, “I presume they’re going to continue pumping.”

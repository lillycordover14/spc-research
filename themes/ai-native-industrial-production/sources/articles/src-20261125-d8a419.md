Chicago composer to debut new ‘Requiem’ for World AIDS Day&nbsp;&nbsp;Windy City Times

[page text]
Musician and community organizer 
D.J. Rodriguez
has taken his interest in the Latin Requiem Mass and transformed it into an original choral composition reflecting on LGBTQ+ individuals lost to HIV/AIDS.   

That piece, 
Requiem for a Lost Generation
, will be performed Dec. 1, which is World AIDS Day, at North Shore Baptist Church, 5244 N. Lakewood Ave., in Andersonville. The performance will be conducted by Topher Orzech and features a 26-member a capella choir. 

In a November press release, Rodriguez explained, “As a queer composer living with HIV, this project is deeply personal to me. The work draws on the traditional Latin Requiem Mass, reclaiming a form of sacred remembrance for a generation of LGBTQ+ people who were so often denied dignity, compassion and the opportunity to be properly mourned.”

 

 

It is the first composition by Rodriguez, who is a classically trained baritone. He joked, “I decided to start off with something small: a nine-movement requiem, right out of the gate.”

Rodriguez got the idea for the piece last year when his son, as a birthday gift, took him to see the Chicago Symphony Orchestra (CSO) perform Verdi’s Messa da Requiem, because, as Rodriguez described it, “I’ve always had a soft spot for performance-requiems,” he said. Mozart’s Requiem is his favorite, he added. 

As that CSO performance went on, Rodriguez came to an important realization: “I don’t think anyone—at least to my knowledge—has ever used the Latin Requiem Mass to honor queer people,” he said. 

 

 

Rodriguez jokingly referred to himself as “neurospicy,” and said that even as he sat enjoying Verdi, “ideas started rolling around in my head.”

D.J. Rodriguez. Photo courtesy of Rodriguez

He was unemployed then and set about using part of his time composing. The ‘80s and ‘90s were decades when many religious groups “were not welcoming to queer folks and denied people of that spiritual fellowship,” Rodriguez said. “…I said to myself, ‘It would be a really cool idea to have a requiem that is specifically dedicated this way, in order to sort of reclaim that.’”

He shifted many parts of the Mass around, looking to subvert some of its traditional components and their uses to better tell his story musically.

 

 

Rodriguez opens with an Alleluia that progresses toward the Requiem text at the end. That choice reflected the sexual liberation of the ‘70s that came in the wake of the Stonewall Uprising, and its ultimate squelching by the AIDS crisis in the following decade. He explained, “I really wanted to talk about the fact that there were a bunch in our community who were like, ‘Yeah, we’re good.’ And then suddenly, ‘Why is everything falling apart?’ What’s happening?”

His setting for the Libera me (“deliver me”) movement furthermore portrays people with AIDS nearing the end of their life, wanting deliverance from earthly pain and suffering. Composers traditionally used that movement to convey spiritual deliverance from death itself. 

“Each movement tells either a different emotion or a functional piece of the story,” Rodriguez said. 

The Dec. 1 program also includes stories from community members reflecting on HIV/AIDS, noted Rodriguez, who now works for a software company. He added, “I’ve been trainer by trade for many years, and so if I can incorporate education or story-sharing into the art that I can create, that’s kind of where I really find it to be the most impactful.”

He admitted that mounting the performance left him confronting his own imposter syndrome. Even after he’d rehearsed the piece with a choir, he was reluctant to refer to himself as its composer. 

“I had people around me say, ‘You literally wrote a piece of music and people sang it and you’re moving forward—what do you think makes a composer?’” Rodriguez said.

His overall goal is not making money from the Requiem. The pay-what-you-can admission to the Dec. 1 performance, as well as some funding Rodriguez won, will go toward paying the singers and covering incidental expenses. He intends to post a video recording of the performance on YouTube.

“If there is any extra, it’ll go toward making a studio recording of the piece,” Rodriguez said. “I highly doubt there will be. … The point of this is doing what I can as a queer HIV-positive man to give something back.”

Rodriguez said that, even after decades of advancements in treatment and prevention of HIV/AIDS—as well as a rising awareness of the harmfulness of stigma— he was surprised that so many people communicated to him “how much healing, and having people have their stories be heard, is still desired. [The Dec. 1 event] really highlighted how much of an opportunity there is to do more of that.” 

He has found himself talking to people who, even without hearing his music, have been emotionally stirred by his concept. 

“I’ve had so many conversations with people where they are trying not to cry, or they are crying, and it has just been incredibly touching,” Rodriguez said. “It really highlights that there is a need for more recognition—which is also validating because it means [perceiving that need] wasn’t just in my head.”

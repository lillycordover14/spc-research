Key Takeaways
Periodic Labs co-founder Liam Fedus argues AI-driven science needs physical labs, not just models, and that the real bottleneck in automating materials discovery is intelligence rather than robotics.
Summary
Liam Fedus, co-founder of Periodic Labs and former OpenAI VP of post-training, argues that AI's next frontier is science, and that reaching it requires physical laboratories, not just more internet data. The episode spends most of its time on that thesis and on Fedus's account of how ChatGPT came to exist; the title's "AI scientist" framing is the through-line, but roughly half the conversation is a first-hand history of GPT-4, RLHF, and reasoning models at OpenAI.

Fedus is well placed to compare the two eras: he worked on dark-matter detectors at MIT, moved to Google Brain in the mid-2010s, and was in the room at OpenAI on day one of GPT-4 before leaving to build Periodic with DeepMind materials scientist Ekin Dogus Cubuk. The reader should come away with a clear model of why ChatGPT and coding models broke through when they did — data, RLHF, verifiable rewards, test-time compute — and why Fedus believes materials discovery is the domain where the same playbook can be repeated, provided someone builds the data flywheel that the open internet never provided for chemistry and physics.

ChatGPT's breakthrough was a product surface, not a smarter model

Fedus says the single biggest driver of ChatGPT's viral takeoff was switching from a completion endpoint to a chat endpoint, on a model (GPT-3.5) that had already been publicly available as an API for months. The underlying post-training improvements mattered, but the conversational framing — assistant/user turns instead of prompt-completion gymnastics — is what made it natural to use.

He is candid that almost nobody inside OpenAI expected the scale. Internal polls guessed 10,000 to 50,000 users, with the boldest guess around a million. The friends-and-family preview had been read as a negative signal because roughly 90% of testers did not care — but Fedus now reads the 5–10% who became daily active users, and complained whenever a model update changed behavior, as the real positive signal. The launch itself was intended as a low-key research preview: a blog post, maybe 10,000 researchers trying it.

"I remember summer '23 feeling just like existential crisis because there's like a slowing in usage and we're like, 'Oh no, is ChatGPT over?' But turns out people just chill out a bit more in the summer."

The first months after launch were "mayhem" — every dashboard red, everyone patching frantically. Fedus also notes the irony that GPT-4 was demonstrated to the team on his first day, roughly a year and a half ahead of anything else he had seen, yet it still hallucinated and could not do math, so it was unclear how it would become valuable. Early product ideas included a writing bot, a coding bot, and a meeting bot that would sit in Google Meets, take notes, and send out to-dos. John Schulman pushed for generality and a chatbot instead.

Two breakthroughs, two different enabling conditions

Fedus's framework for why ChatGPT and coding models arrived before science breakthroughs is essentially a data-and-verifiability argument. ChatGPT needed a strong pre-trained substrate (GPT-3.5, a significant step beyond GPT-3), supervised traces to establish an assistant prior, and preference reward models to distill human preferences into an optimization signal. But those reward models encode style, intent, and form — not correctness — so RLHF alone could not produce a system that reliably solves math or writes correct code.

Coding models needed something else: reasoning with long traces, interaction with the environment, error correction, and verifiable rewards that can say definitively "this is correct" or "this is incorrect." Fedus points to test-time compute as central to unlocking software engineering capability. The host adds the data-side point: code has GitHub, a giant repository of human work with unit tests baked in, and biology had decades of protein-folding data for AlphaFold. The sciences broadly have neither.

The scientific literature is a biased, low-context dataset

Periodic's core conviction is that published science is a poor training set. Two research groups can run the same experiment and reach different conclusions; papers ablate necessary procedural details ("we synthesized this material" hides a million degrees of freedom); and negative results are almost never published, so "you look online, everything worked." The distribution is biased toward what succeeded and toward what got written up at all.

Fedus's answer is context. What was the hypothesis? How was it carried out? What was the outcome? A raw physics simulation from two decades ago is nearly useless without that framing. Periodic tried having scientists wear GoPros around the lab — they disliked it — and settled on voice recordings and lab notebook uploads. Asked what internet-scale dataset he would want, Fedus names every lab notebook from every great scientist, paired with the code and experimental data they were looking at. The host cites Will DePue's idea of a lifelong head-mounted camera; Fedus amends it to scientists and to capturing intent at the start of a day versus whether it was met.

Periodic is building labs because thinking alone cannot collapse hypotheses

Periodic's stated goal is "intentional understanding and engineering of matter." The AI learns from the internet, runs physics simulations, and generates hypotheses about stable atomic configurations and their properties — but that only produces a hypothesis set. In Menlo Park the company has built physical materials-science labs and is scaling them. Fedus calls this non-negotiable: to create an AI scientist, you have to do science, generate negative results, and calibrate incoming data against simulations and literature.

A counterintuitive finding from building the first small lab: they became intelligence-bottlenecked almost immediately. Scientists and engineers could not make sense of the data volume and asked for help before the larger lab even existed. Fedus argues intelligent systems reading directly off devices produce richer data capture, which feeds the next model — and naive capture leaves future models blind. He expects "easily dozens" of labs within five years, notes current labs cannot do serious alloys or polymers research, and argues AI-enabled labs will be structurally advantaged over traditional ones. The host's framing: a lab supercharged with these models will outcompete one running traditional processes.

Materials are the bottleneck behind energy, chips, and spaceflight

Fedus frames materials as the substrate of every advanced industry — semiconductors, aerospace, energy — and names new superconductors as Periodic's biggest goal, with applications in medical devices, maglev trains, and lossless energy transmission. He is careful on the physics: room-temperature superconductivity is not known to be physically possible, and Periodic does not need to reach it. Crossing liquid nitrogen temperature alone would make superconductors far more attractive; manufacturability benefits such as ductility or lower rare-earth dependence would matter independently. Better magnets and first-wall materials feed directly into fusion.

Asked which impact resonates most, Fedus picks computation as a physical process: if materials challenges in the semiconductor value chain — designers, manufacturers, equipment suppliers, material suppliers — can be alleviated over the next five years, the AI buildout accelerates. He cites Elon Musk's public comments on materials challenges for large rockets and heat shields as an example of how quickly reusability runs into material limits. On fusion timelines, he offers a hedged view: commercially viable fusion feels "really plausible in the next seven years or so," helped by large capital flows and by better-engineered magnets and first-wall materials. He also notes that cheap energy would let society internalize what is currently a public externality — pulling CO2 out of the atmosphere becomes an engineering problem rather than an impossible one.

Jobs will churn by task, not by occupation

Fedus is skeptical of wholesale job-replacement predictions and uses Geoffrey Hinton's old radiology forecast as the cautionary example: radiologists are still employed because the job is more than reading an image. His model is that AI expands into a growing share of the tasks a role comprises. Take a software engineer's 2020 time allocation; a current model might already do 80–90% of those tasks. But the role itself has shifted toward cross-functional coordination and design review, so the target keeps moving. The host adds the lump-of-labor point: software engineering employment is at a historic high, and productivity elasticity has historically expanded rather than contracted work.

Fedus expects people-to-people work to grow as intelligence commoditizes, and expects expert data work — experts measuring the jagged frontier, building simulation and RL environments, running physical experiments — to keep expanding for at least the next decade. The nature of those expert jobs will keep evolving too, with more emphasis on credit assignment and attribution of long-term outcomes. He also flags the practical distinction between changing a model's behavior through weights versus through inference-time context and data, and how quickly each route reaches reliable downstream performance.

Scaling laws say spend where the data is scarce

On budget allocation between compute and data, Fedus returns to scaling laws: loss as a function of compute, L(C), and loss as a function of data, L(D), hold when you are not constrained by the other. Which regime you are in depends on what was in the pre-training and RL distributions. In underrepresented but economically valuable areas, data yields more benefit per dollar; in code, where verification and synthetics exist, more compute can be poured on. His advice to companies: strive for the same predictability of performance gains from data investment that you already have from compute, so ROI calculations can actually be made.

What to watch

The unresolved tension in the episode is whether Periodic's lab-centric approach can produce the kind of compounding data flywheel that the internet gave language models, or whether physical experimentation is too slow and too expensive to close the loop at software speed. Fedus's own framing — intelligence-bottlenecked before the lab was even built — suggests the constraint may bind earlier than expected, in a good way. Two things worth tracking: whether Periodic's superconductor program produces anything above liquid-nitrogen temperature with manufacturability advantages, and whether the "dozens of labs" plan materializes on the five-year horizon he sketched. Fedus declines to forecast ten years out, noting that in 2016 the transformer did not yet exist and almost no one inside OpenAI thought ChatGPT would find users — a useful humility check on any timeline in this space, including his own.

Fedus declines to forecast ten years out, noting that in 2016 the transformer did not yet exist and almost no one inside OpenAI thought ChatGPT would find users — a useful humility check on any timeline in this space, including his own.
Business Highlights
Periodic Labs has built physical materials-science labs in Menlo Park and is scaling them up, betting that owning experimental infrastructure is a non-negotiable moat for building an AI scientist.
Fedus argues labs supercharged with AI models and a data flywheel will be structurally advantaged over traditionally run labs, implying a widening gap across the research sector.
Periodic Labs chose materials discovery as its entry point because materials underpin semiconductors, aerospace and energy, and the domain offers fast iteration loops, decent signal-to-noise, and existing simulators for large material classes.
Fedus argues that owning physical labs is a non-negotiable strategic asset: public scientific data is noisy, missing negative results, and lacks experimental context, so in-house experiments are needed to test hypotheses and generate proprietary training data.

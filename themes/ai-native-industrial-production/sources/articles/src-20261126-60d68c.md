The AI hardware race has a new bottleneck, and it isn’t the chips themselves. It’s the machines that build what the chips sit on.

Lead times for certain equipment used to make flip-chip ball grid array (FC-BGA) substrates have reportedly stretched from around six months to approximately 30 months, according to South Korean outlet ETNews. That is a nearly fivefold increase.

What a substrate is, and why everyone suddenly wants one

More technically, an FC-BGA substrate is the layered base a chip is flipped onto and bonded to. It routes signals and power between the silicon and the board below. AI accelerators and server chips need large, complex versions of these parts, and demand for them has climbed alongside the AI buildout.

The companies feeling the squeeze are the names that dominate this corner of the supply chain. Research findings identify Ibiden in Japan, Unimicron in Taiwan and Samsung Electro-Mechanics in South Korea as major producers facing the extended waits. Daeduck Electronics and LG Innotek are also listed among the affected suppliers.

Where the queue is forming

According to the research findings, the delays stem largely from a pile-up of orders for key manufacturing tools, including bonding and cutting equipment. When every major substrate maker places orders in the same window, the toolmakers’ backlogs balloon.

Upstream shortages of critical Japanese components, particularly precision motors and servo systems, are reportedly contributing to the long lead times.

Unimicron’s chairman put numbers to the strain at the TPCA Show in October 2026. He indicated that more than 80% of the company’s equipment now carries lead times of 30 months, according to the research findings.

He also flagged a looming materials crunch. Projections cited at the event point to a potential 20% shortfall by 2027 in critical inputs such as low-CTE glass fiber cloth and copper-clad laminates. Low-CTE refers to a low coefficient of thermal expansion: materials that barely swell or shrink as they heat up, which matters when a hot-running AI chip is bonded to a substrate that must stay flat and stable.

NVIDIA is buying its place in line

NVIDIA
 has reportedly engaged directly with substrate suppliers to lock in and expand capacity, targeting a 2.5x increase over the next three years, according to the research findings.

That effort reportedly includes multibillion-dollar advances to Ibiden and capacity reservations with Unimicron. The arrangements are aimed at supporting a production ramp from 2027 through 2028.

Background: from six months to two and a half years

A six-month wait for substrate production gear was, by the research findings’ account, roughly the historical norm. A 30-month wait breaks that rhythm. Capacity decisions now have to be made on demand forecasts that stretch years out, in an industry where AI spending plans can shift quickly.

The squeeze also highlights how concentrated this niche is. A handful of substrate makers across Japan, Taiwan and South Korea serve the world’s AI chip designers, and they in turn lean on a narrow set of equipment and component suppliers, with Japanese motor and servo makers playing an outsized role.

What this means

For the AI hardware market, the most immediate implication is that substrate capacity, not just wafer capacity, is becoming a gating factor. The research findings suggest the extended lead times could ripple through semiconductor supply chains, potentially delaying product launches and limiting how many AI chips can actually ship.

Buyers with deep pockets and long-term commitments, like NVIDIA with its advances and reservations, may be better positioned to secure supply. The research describes this dynamic as a competitive arms race in AI semiconductor production.

Investors watching the space should keep an eye on whether the projected 20% shortfall in materials like low-CTE glass fiber cloth and copper-clad laminates materializes by 2027, and whether the Japanese precision motor and servo bottleneck eases.

The research findings also caution about potential price volatility and supply disruptions in the broader semiconductor market, suggesting volatility could persist through 2028 and beyond.

Twelve European aviation organisations urge EU to boost EASA funding and certification capacity in 2028–2034 budget
Why It Matters
EASA's fee-funded certification capacity sets the pace at which European manufacturers can bring products to market, so a staffing gap against the FAA becomes a competitiveness question for the whole sector.
Nov 27

What happened
ASD and eleven other European aviation organisations issued a joint statement, dated 26 November 2026, calling for the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) to receive the resources and capabilities needed to fulfil its safety mandate under the EU's 2028–2034 Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF).
The statement sets out three priorities. The first is a substantial, stable and multiannual increase in EU funding for EASA's core safety and rulemaking responsibilities and for aviation safety research. The signatories say these public-interest activities should not depend on fees levied on individual aviation stakeholders. They add that extra funding should stay focused on core aviation safety, and that new tasks should be considered only where a demonstrated safety need exists and the resource implications have been assessed.
The second priority is greater flexibility for EASA to recruit specialised certification staff. The statement cites 
280 full-time equivalents
 at EASA in core certification activities, compared with 
1,400
 at the US Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). The organisations say EU administrative staffing restrictions should not prevent recruitment for activities funded by industry fees and charges, and that staffing should develop in line with workload and revenues, with measurable improvements in certification and approval timelines.
The third priority is adequate resources for EASA's international activities, including standard-setting, regulatory cooperation and harmonisation. The signatories link this to sustaining Europe's leading role in shaping global aviation safety standards and to supporting international acceptance of European certifications.
The organisations state that EASA has considerably fewer financial and human resources in several critical areas than other major authorities, particularly the FAA, while other aviation systems, including China's, continue to develop rapidly. They say this imbalance risks undermining safety, slowing certification and rulemaking, limiting innovation and reducing Europe's influence over international aviation policy. They call on EU Transport Ministers to discuss EASA's future financing and resource needs at the Transport Council on 
30 November 2026
, ahead of negotiations on the next MFF.
Industry impact & what to watch
Certification throughput is a direct input to how fast manufacturers and suppliers can bring new aircraft, engines and avionics to market, and it is the part of a regulator's work that industry pays for through fees. When a regulator's certification staff is a fraction of a counterpart's, applicants tend to feel it as queue time and uncertainty over approval dates rather than as a change in safety standards. That is why the signatories frame the request around fee-funded recruitment flexibility and measurable timelines, not only around a larger budget line.
The international strand matters for a second reason. Bilateral acceptance of certifications depends on regulators being able to staff standard-setting and harmonisation work, and the statement ties that capacity to Europe's influence over global rules. Business-aviation OEMs and component makers that sell into several jurisdictions are the segment most exposed to how smoothly European approvals are recognised abroad and foreign approvals are recognised in Europe.
The next dated milestone is the Transport Council on 30 November 2026, where the organisations want ministers to take up EASA's financing. The more consequential outcome comes later, in the MFF negotiations: whether any increase is written in as multiannual core funding or whether EASA stays reliant on fees, and whether the hiring restrictions on fee-funded certification posts are loosened.

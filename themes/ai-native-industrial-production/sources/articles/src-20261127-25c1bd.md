Russia’s Unconventional War on Europe: Sabotage, Deterrence and Future Scenarios

Russia’s unconventional war on Europe has not halted support for Ukraine, but its intelligence services remain a persistent threat. This report examines the campaign, assesses Europe’s response and sets out four scenarios to 2029.

Authors

Charlie Edwards, Tom Field, Zoe Lambert

https://www.iiss.org/research-paper/2026/11/russias-unconventional-war-on-europe-sabotage-deterrence-and-future-scenarios/

Investigators examine railways damaged in an explosion in Mika, Poland, after the line was targeted in a sabotage act, 17 November 2025
Wojtek Radwanski/AFP via Getty Images

Russia’s unconventional war on Europe has become a test of whether European states can sustain support for Ukraine in the face of a campaign conducted by the Russian intelligence services (RIS) and their proxies. Over the past four years the RIS have targeted European critical infrastructure (ECI–railways, energy networks and defence industries) on which Ukraine’s war effort depends. As part of Russia’s wider campaign, the Kremlin has used sabotage, reconnaissance and intimidation to raise the cost of that support, all below the threshold of armed conflict. In the process, Russia has actively sought to exploit existing divisions within European societies.

Russia’s unconventional war on Europe has so far failed in its strategic purpose. Military assistance to Ukraine continues and European rearmament is under way. IISS data shows that European defence spending grew by 12.6% in real terms in 2025, matching the rate of 2024, while Russia’s rose by 3%. NATO has expanded surveillance of the Baltic Sea and its eastern airspace. The European Union has adopted a Defence Readiness Roadmap and, following a report by Sauli Niinistö, the former president of Finland, on preparedness and readiness of the EU, launched the Preparedness Union Strategy.

National authorities across Europe have expelled intelligence officers, arrested proxies and, in response to specific incidents, closed Russian consulates. National legislation has been updated or created in the aftermath of significant attacks.

Taken together, the measures European governments have imposed have strengthened the ability of national authorities to disrupt Russian operations and limit the consequences of an attack, but have not yet demonstrated a sustained deterrent effect. There is no clear evidence that these measures have made sabotage a less attractive instrument of Kremlin policy. To that end, the key question for Europe is whether it can turn a series of national decisions in response to specific incidents into a Europe-wide strategy that constrains Russian action before an operation conducted by the RIS or proxies causes a mass-casualty event or cuts military supply lines to Ukraine. This research paper examines that question and, through four scenarios to 2029, sets out how the threat could develop.

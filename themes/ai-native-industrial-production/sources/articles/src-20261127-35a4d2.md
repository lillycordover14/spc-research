Cory Kosel’s debut album maps a Texas that won’t slow down

Cory Kosel’s grandfather still lives in the Temple, Texas house he lived in during the 1950s and ’60s. The house hasn’t moved, but everything else has. Temple had about 40,000 people when Kosel was a kid. It now has more than 100,000, and the old place is hemmed in by subdivisions: fresh sod, four floor plans and repeat until Austin.

That collision of old porches and new rooftops drives “Dispatch from the Edge of Town,” Kosel’s debut solo album, out Dec. 4 on Toast Toast Records. He left Temple for college on the Gulf Coast, chased acting in New York, landed in East Dallas and 
recently moved south to Waxahachie
. It’s a résumé that reads a lot like the album’s road map.

“I really just wanted to escape the small town and go to a larger city,” Kosel says. “I eventually did all of that, and then kind of by chance found myself living in a small town again, adopting a slower pace of life. It kind of feels like I’ve received a letter that I sent to myself all those years ago, living in Temple when I was young and restless. And I’ve now finally carved out the time and the space to be able to read it.”

Don’t mistake that for a soft-focus ode to the good ol’ days.

“It’s not something I’m mourning, but I just find it fascinating,” he says. “I’m trying to tell the story of what Texas is now as it’s continuing to change so rapidly.”

That story turns out to be about more than real estate. Across nine tracks, the album’s characters — gamblers, busboys, oilmen and rocket-struck kids — keep bumping into structures bigger than themselves.

“More and more we’re being asked to live inside these impossible systems and go on and carry on and try to live a meaningful life,” Kosel says. “I just found those themes kind of hard to get around.”

The album opener, “Cater to the Seed,” preaches “slow down kind of living.” Then its narrator, as Kosel happily points out, sprints straight into the casino-floor fatalism of “The Game You Choose.” The hypocrisy is the point.

“We’re all implicated in that to a degree,” he says of the faster-cheaper-more grind. “But also it’s possible for a counter narrative to exist. Slowing down is possible.”

Songwriter with a syllabus

Kosel spent his 20s acting in New York and 
later on Dallas stages
, including Dallas Theater Center and Theatre Three. Then, during the pandemic, he did something few aspiring country singers do: earn a master’s in English. Today, that academic detour has practical application, as he teaches writing at a community college.

advertisement

“My songwriter heroes, [like] Kris Kristofferson, these guys, they had English degrees,” he says. “I could tell you that their songs were masterful, but I couldn’t pick up the hood and inspect the engine and tell you why and how it worked.”

Now he can, and the record shows its wiring proudly. Some songs are personal. Kosel calls “The Interim” and “Mess Up You” the album’s “emotional center.” The latter is about being “a father who also has ambitions to be an artist in this kind of impossible day and age.” Other songs belong to characters he invents and inhabits, which he says is “where I’m really interested in going next.”

Why tell it in fragments? Because that’s what the place is.

“We are so fragmented,” Kosel says. “We are trying to put the pieces together of who we are after such rapid and ongoing change.”

advertisement

The machine and the line

Lead single “Machine of My Design,” featuring Fort Worth’s Robert Ellis, was born in Kosel’s classroom. He says writing teachers everywhere are “contending with technology assistance and young people using AI to create.” He set out to write his AI song, then decided that was “too easy.”

“I don’t like to think of myself as a Luddite,” he says. “We’re drawn to what makes our lives convenient. But we don’t step back and ask ourselves, is it making me better as a human? Is it making me a better communicator, a member of my community?”

advertisement

The result is a fiddle-and-mandolin shuffle that pokes at the listener and its author equally. It even ends with Kosel confessing he wrote catchy songs so y’all would sing along.

Then there’s “Highland Park Line,” which Kosel says “attempts to collapse almost 200 years of Texas history into a four-minute song.” The song opens with the Spindletop gusher. From there, it moves to an affluent family at dinner in Dallas, drawn from Kosel’s five years waiting tables here. A busboy who works another full-time job and drives in from Wylie closes out the story.

“It attempts to make the invisible labor in that restaurant visible,” he says.

Coming from Temple, where “there was like, one fancy restaurant,” Kosel had a front-row seat to Dallas money. He also had a tray in his hand.

advertisement

“Boca Chica Sky” was among the last songs Kosel wrote for the album, and it’s appropriately loud. He used to hear SpaceX’s early rocket tests near McGregor on drives home. The song follows a boy thrilled by the launches who comes to realize “he’ll never be on one, that the rockets aren’t meant for him.”

Quietly, it also packs a gut-punch: a lyric about “a hundred thousand” crawling toward the sea — which Kosel explains refers to sea turtle hatchlings, creatures now facing slimmer odds of survival thanks to the growing industrial footprint along the Texas coast.

“The messaging about all of that industry is that it’s for the better,” Kosel says. “But if Texas has been your home for a long time, you may have a different outlook.”

Songs built for a Texas that won’t hold still

advertisement

Recorded at Fort Worth’s Niles City Sound, the album carries the weight of 
a room with serious pedigree
.

“It lends itself to a certain kind of style of music and nods to Texas music history,” Kosel says.

Quick to credit his collaborators, Kosel singles out engineer Marshall Pruitt, who has “a knack for exploring and offering ideas that I had never imagined.” The pace was frantic.

“This was crazy, man,” Kosel says. “Three days, we pounded out seven songs.”

advertisement

advertisement

Day one was live rhythm takes. For the next two days, Kosel invited his friends in, and “they treated the songs like a playground.” That’s where the pedal steel and horn arrangements come from, along with slide guitar from Fort Worth’s Taylor Ochs.

“When I listen to the album, I don’t just hear my songs,” he says. “I hear all these different voices.”

That communal streak extends to Dallas’ Toast Toast Records, run by friends Kosel met through years of playing shows, including with Dallas band Caftan.

“They don’t adhere to a fixed genre, and they’re really about community in the Dallas scene and the North Texas scene,” he says.

advertisement

So, is the album a solo statement or a product of the scene?

“A little bit of both.”

Hear it for yourself on Dec. 4, when Kosel celebrates the release at Top Ten Records (338 W. Jefferson Blvd, Oak Cliff) with 
Dorian Marsh
 and All the Tired Horses. The show is free, with doors opening at 7 p.m. and music starting at 8 p.m. RSVPs are available through
 Eventbrite
. Get there early. Slowing down is possible, just not on release night.

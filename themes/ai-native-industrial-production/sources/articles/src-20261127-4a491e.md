Reducing energy consumption while maintaining reliable crop production is a challenge for commercial greenhouse operators in hot climates. In the Gulf, high ambient temperatures place considerable demands on cooling infrastructure. SOMA, the Gulf extension of India's Eeki technology and operating platform, is developing an approach that combines substrate-free growing chambers, automation and geothermal earth-air heat exchange to reduce reliance on conventional chiller-based cooling.
 
"Our approach combines patented, substrate-free growing chambers, automation and geothermal earth-air heat exchange to reduce reliance on conventional chiller-based cooling," explains Hitesh Sidhwani, Co-Founder and CEO of SOMA.
 
SOMA's role is to build and operate farms in the UAE using technology developed through Eeki's commercial operations in India, adapting the platform to Gulf conditions with particular attention to water and electricity consumption.
 
Earth-air heat exchange as an alternative cooling approach
The geothermal system uses the relatively stable temperature of the ground to moderate incoming greenhouse air. "Air passes through underground pipes, where heat exchange with the surrounding soil reduces its temperature. A low-power fan then distributes the conditioned air through ducts into the plant-growing zone."
 
Unlike conventional evaporative cooling, the system does not require cooling pads or high-pressure fogging, and is designed to avoid conventional air conditioning as the primary means of plant-zone cooling. "The main energy input is fan power."
 
Performance depends on several site-specific variables, including soil temperature, thermal properties, moisture content, pipe depth and configuration. Airflow velocity also determines how effectively conditioned air reaches the crop canopy. SOMA therefore plans to assess ground conditions and cooling loads at each UAE site before engineering the system.

Substrate-free chambers and automated crop management
The growing technology uses enclosed, medium-less chambers in which plant roots occupy a light-proof environment and receive water and nutrients through fine misting. "Droplets measuring approximately 20–40 microns are distributed within the root chamber, with nutrient solution recirculated."
 
Eeki documents water savings of up to 90% compared with conventional irrigation methods, an important consideration in a region where freshwater availability is a significant agricultural constraint.
 
Sensors and automation coordinate irrigation, fertigation and climate management. The separation of root-zone management from the above-ground growing environment allows the different components to perform complementary functions.
 
"Earth-air cooling holds the plant zone at working temperature while the chamber manages the root zone, so each part reduces the load on the others," he adds.
 
The underlying enclosed, medium-less root chamber technology is covered by Indian patent no. 576028, granted to Eeki Automation Private Limited. The newer misting chamber has separate design and utility filings in India.
 
Engineering considerations: airflow, soil and condensation
The effectiveness of earth-air heat exchange depends on the interaction between underground infrastructure and greenhouse conditions. "Pipe depth and length, soil thermal properties, moisture and airflow rates influence the temperature and volume of air delivered to the growing area."
 
Condensation is another important consideration. When warm, humid air passes through cooler underground pipes, moisture can accumulate. Pipe design and humidity management are therefore necessary to address condensation and maintain reliable operation.
 
SOMA intends to engineer each installation according to local ground conditions, anticipated cooling demand and greenhouse configuration rather than applying standardised dimensions across different sites.
 
Transferring commercial experience from India to the UAE
SOMA draws on Eeki's commercial operating experience in India. According to the company's reported timeline, Eeki had more than 200 operational acres of farms in India by August 2024.
 
However, transferring the technology to the Gulf requires validation under different climatic and operating conditions. Hitesh identifies water and electricity conservation as the two primary priorities for regional deployment.
 
"The medium-less chamber addresses water. Geothermal cooling addresses electricity," he says. The geothermal system is currently at the provisional-patent stage. Its cooling performance and potential to reduce chiller dependence will depend on outdoor temperature and humidity, ground temperature at depth, pipe configuration, fan energy, crop selection and greenhouse design. SOMA is not proposing a guaranteed energy-saving figure for UAE operations.
 
Establishing commercial viability through measured performance
For commercial greenhouse development, technical performance must translate into consistent production and competitive operating costs. SOMA considers full-season operational data essential to establishing the viability of its approach under UAE conditions.
 
The required measurements include electricity consumption and water use per kilogram of saleable produce, plant-zone temperature and humidity against operating targets, crop yield and quality against buyer specifications, maintenance, downtime, labour and delivered production costs.
 
"Full-season measured data from a UAE site" will be important to illustrate performance, says Sidhwani. SOMA's responsibility is to build and operate the farms to generate this evidence.
 
The longer-term objective is to produce crops at market-comparable prices without subsidies. Demonstrating repeatable performance across energy consumption, water efficiency and crop economics will therefore be essential to determining whether the model can be scaled commercially.

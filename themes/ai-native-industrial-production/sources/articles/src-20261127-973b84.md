As the books editor at Good Housekeeping and the head of the GH Book Club, I’m always on the hunt for my next great read. I’m constantly recommending books I love to our readers as well as to my own friends and family, and I live for the moment I find a new favorite so I can tell everyone about it. GH Book Club

Our November pick was 
The Tulip Poplars
 by Silas House. It’s a beautiful queer Southern drama, set in rural Kentucky in the 1900s. Tackling race, class, identity, faith, and more, it’s about hiding who you love because society can’t accept it and what that hiding costs.
If you’re anything like me, you’ll fly through the pages and maybe shed a few tears along the way. Author Silas House shared his thoughts with the GH Book Club about how the natural can also be the divine, the importance of depicting queer joy alongside tragedy, and more. Don’t forget to join

SV: Why do you describe this as an ecological novel? 
This book contains three love stories: two between people and one between all four of those people and the place they call home. The main character, Tom, will do anything to make sure his beloved grove of tulip poplars is not cut down during a time when most of the area was clearcut. For the main characters of this book, the place—the land, the trees—is more than just a piece of property. The place represents their roots, home, and even the divine. I think that aspect of it is subtle, but hopefully powerful and memorable.
SV: You’re a former Poet Laureate of Kentucky. How does being a poet shape how you approach fiction writing? 
Silas House: Particularly in this novel I wanted every sentence to sing. When writing poetry, a poet’s main concern should be the music of the lines. Sometimes that isn’t necessarily the case with prose, but in this case I wanted every sentence to sing. However, I also wanted every sentence to feel organic as well, so I really strived to strike that balance of the sentences being “worried over” by me but also reading as natural.
SV: What draws you to telling queer rural stories? What about queer religious stories? 
Silas House: The main thing is that so few of them have been told. Most people’s experience of a rural queer story is 
Brokeback Mountain 
and that’s it, full stop. Of course that’s a wonderful film and novella, but we deserve more stories than just one. Queer people have always existed in rural places, and historically their stories have been forgotten and erased.
I also think it’s important to look at the way faith has so often shaped LGBTQ people, in both damaging and helpful ways. While organized religion has hurt a lot of LGBTQ people by turning them away, faith has also played an important role in carrying those same people through great turmoil. People of faith are usually portrayed as caricatures or comedic relief in literature, and I want to be part of correcting that.
SV: This book has several threads, following the characters as they go their separate ways. Which story thread or character was your favorite to write?
Silas House: I have lived in Kentucky my whole life so Tom’s story is the closest to mine, even though he’s living a hundred years before me. So I think that I enjoyed writing Amos’s storyline the most because I got to live vicariously through him. I love his adventurous spirit and the way he demands to be seen for who he is and not hide that. I wish I had been as brave as Amos when I was his age. It took me a lot longer to get there. Anytime I get to live vicariously through a character, it’s a joy to write.
SV: Amos’s life changes when he moves to New York and finds he is not alone. What is the importance of highlighting queer community and queer joy? 
Silas House: I think so many stories about LGBTQ people focus on the despair and forget about the joy. In many ways this is a book about community—in the beginning we’re at a square dance, later we’re in jazz clubs in New York City, and toward the end we’re in a gay bar in a Southern city where our main characters are old men dancing with drag queens for the first time. I was very intentional in weaving through moments of community like that to highlight the joy and the importance of community for people who are so often ostracized by the rest of the world.
SV: I was so nervous for the tulip poplars throughout the entire book! Did you consider a version of the story in which they did get cut down? 
Silas House: I briefly considered that, but I couldn’t bear the thought of it, especially when I realized that the trees were a symbol of home and even the divine for the main characters. But in reality, most of the land in Appalachia at that time was clearcut by land prospectors who lived far away and didn’t have to see the devastation they caused. It’s a heartache to think of all the wild places that have been lost.
SV: I loved the relationship between Tom and his mule, Daisy. Do you have an animal you love like that?
Silas House: I’ve always had a big connection with dogs. For this book, I knew that it was going to span a very long time—it ends up being about 65 years—so I knew I couldn’t create a dog character that could only realistically live for about 15 of those years. While researching the book I was looking at a lot of photographs of my grandparents, who were young people during the early time period of this book, and in all of the ones of my grandfather he was posing with his mule. Simultaneously, I learned that mules can live to be about fifty years old. So I sort of cheated and created a dog-like mule.
Daisy is the character readers mention to me the most, though, and I’m so happy that people are loving her. I think Daisy allows us to quickly know a lot of foundational things about Tom, especially that he really lives by his father’s mantra that it is important to protect things that have less power than we do.
SV: What was your favorite scene in the book?
Silas House: This risks giving away something very important so I’ll be careful in how I state it, but my favorite scene is a wedding that takes place in the grove of tulip poplars. It’s very short, not more than a paragraph, but it’s the one that really tears me down because it’s between these two characters who don’t have their full rights in the eyes of the law, but within the natural world, they can. It is moving to me to think of all the people in history whose love was legislated—either because of race or orientation—who had to perform their own acts of union that way.
SV: What books would you recommend to our readers?
Silas House: If readers like 
The Tulip Poplars
 I would encourage them to read 
The Woodlanders
 by Thomas Hardy, which is the biggest influence on this novel. I really wanted to write an epic love story and the biggest inspiration for that element was 
Doctor Zhivago
 by Boris Pasternak, which is also about characters who are just trying to live their lives but the political upheaval of the times keeps interfering, much like the bigotry of the times does for my characters.
The Color Purple
 by Alice Walker is also a major influence on this book because we’re with one main character for most of her life and it is also a book that complexifies notions of rural people while looking at race, gender, class, orientation, and religion. No other novel changed me as a person more than that one.

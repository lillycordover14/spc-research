Can seaweed help tulip growers improve bulb quality, increase yields and make cultivation more sustainable? Holdfast & Stipe, a producer of seaweed extracts, is investigating this through two different trials in the Netherlands: one at research centre Vertify, as part of the Green Tulip project, and another in collaboration with tulip bulb grower Maatschap Kortstra-Kwant in Marknesse, Flevoland. The first results show an increase in bulb weight and in the share of larger, marketable bulbs. The business case has also been positive so far.
 
© Marco Vet
Peter and Robbin Kortstra
 
"Can we grow tulips without emissions?" This is the central question behind the Green Tulip project, initiated by Greenport Noord-Holland Noord and Vertify. For Holdfast & Stipe, the tulip is a particularly meaningful crop to work with. "The tulip represents so much of the Netherlands," says Roy Wormsbecher, co-founder of Holdfast & Stipe alongside Nikki Spil and responsible for product development. "Our mission is to help transform agriculture and horticulture in the Netherlands and contribute to a greener, healthier future. It is great to explore whether we can make a difference in such an iconic crop."
 
Dominic Burbridge, Director of Sales, adds: "Seaweed is a natural product, and we believe nature holds many of the answers to the challenges we face today. The challenge is to make the natural choice an easy and profitable one for growers. That is why we wanted to contribute to the Green Tulip project."
 
© Marco Vet
 
Two approaches to testing
The company is approaching the research from two angles. Lotte Thieme, Holdfast & Stipe's Innovation & Quality Control Specialist, who has a background in marine biology, is involved in setting up and coordinating the trials.
 
At Maatschap Kortstra-Kwant, a family-run tulip bulb business in Marknesse, the focus is on testing the product under everyday growing conditions. Holdfast & Stipe is working with the grower for the second year. Different concentrations of the seaweed product are being tested, alongside treatments in which the bulbs are dipped. The seaweed product is also applied weekly throughout the growing season to stimulate plant development and bulb growth. The treated strips are currently in the field, with the next results expected around March.
 
The trial at Vertify is more extensive and scientifically structured. Two tulip varieties are being tested on both sandy and clay soils, with multiple repetitions of the seaweed applications. A comparison is also being made between cultivation with and without conventional chemical inputs. The project will run for two years, allowing researchers to follow the effects over a longer period.
 
"We deliberately combine scientific research with practical trials," Roy explains. "Growers often point out that their soil and growing conditions are different. We understand that. But we believe seaweed can offer answers to many agricultural challenges, across a wide range of contexts and conditions. We want to bring research closer to the end user. We don't want to stay in the laboratory; we want to solve practical problems and create opportunities for growers."
 
© Marco Vet
 
More weight, larger bulbs
The first results from the practical trial at Kortstra-Kwant are encouraging. The number of bulbs per square metre remained largely unchanged, but the total weight increased. Depending on the treatment, the increase reached more than 28% compared with the untreated control.
 
The share of bulbs in the larger size categories, 10–12+, also increased in all three treated groups. Preliminary figures show an increase of approximately 33 to 67% in the larger size categories, while the number of smaller bulbs decreased by around 3 to 21%.
 
The results suggest that the effect lies mainly in bulb size and weight, rather than in producing more bulbs. Holdfast & Stipe stresses, however, that these figures are preliminary. Each treatment was tested in a single plot, without repetitions, meaning the results have not yet been statistically validated. The dry matter analysis is also still underway. Nevertheless, the findings have naturally made the Holdfast & Stipe team and its trial partners curious to see what the next results will bring.
 
According to Roy, the positive trend is visible across different soil types and varieties in the trials. "We are seeing increases in bulb weight and size, both in the scientific trial and under practical growing conditions. It is not necessarily about producing more bulbs, but about getting a larger share into the marketable size categories."
 
For one variety, the results also suggest that bulbs could potentially reach plantable size sooner. This could create opportunities to shorten the growing cycle from three years to two. Further research will be needed to confirm this.
 
© Marco Vet
 
A challenging growing season
The results are particularly interesting given the challenging growing conditions this season. According to the company, some growers have lost a significant part of their stock. At Kortstra-Kwant, the untreated plot produced 128 bulbs per square metre, from an initial planting density of 140 bulbs. The treated plots ranged from 109 to 132 bulbs per square metre. However, an improvement was seen in the share of marketable bulbs.
 
"Most growers have had a difficult season," says Dominic. "Seaweed is easy to incorporate into the growing system and supports several processes that help plants cope with challenging conditions. Why wouldn't you give it a try?"
 
A positive business case
Based on the additional marketable yield in the current trial, Holdfast & Stipe calculates that every euro invested in the seaweed product could generate around three euros in return. The company sees this as an indication that sustainability and profitability do not necessarily have to come at the expense of one another.
 
© Marco Vet
 
Bringing the power of seaweed back
For Holdfast & Stipe, the trials are about more than demonstrating the effectiveness and business case of a single product. The company wants to help growers find practical solutions to the challenges facing modern agriculture and horticulture.
 
"Those challenges aren't going away," says Roy. "Seaweed is a remarkable natural product. It has evolved over millions of years and developed its own ways of protecting itself, for example against strong currents, extremely salty water and prolonged exposure to sunlight. It contains valuable compounds that can also benefit crops. Perhaps we have forgotten some of the power of seaweed. We want to bring that back into Dutch agriculture and horticulture."
 
The trials will continue in the coming period, with the results at Vertify and Kortstra-Kwant being followed closely. The next stage will show whether the positive trends can be confirmed over a longer period. "Ultimately, we want to show that it works, both scientifically and in the grower's field," Roy concludes.

TVR Returns From The Dead. Again&nbsp;&nbsp;Road & Track

[page text]
TVR
Would you wait a decade for a new car? That’s the situation for anybody who raised their hand to buy the TVR Griffith when it was 
announced as far back as 2017
. R&T’s own interest in the story faded soon after the reborn British brand announced the car was 
going to be heavily delayed
. But, in truth, nobody realized how long the wait would be. 
Delays were reportedly caused by the failure to secure funding to build a factory in Wales among other crises. It’s fair to say that the interest of most onlookers, and surely all of the potential buyers , faded as the excuses piled up. But after a change in ownership last year, TVR's new parent MOTV Group has announced that the Griffith will be produced at the Lotus factory in Hethel, with the first customer deliveries “planned for 2028,” although those likely restricted to buyers in the U.K.
MOTU
This is the new car...
There isn’t room for any detailed history of TVR here, but the original incarnation of the sport car maker collapsed into bankruptcy in 2006, having become both famous and infamous for brutish, fiber-bodied models including the 1990 Griffith, 1996 Cerbera and 1999 Tuscan Speed Six. Also the semi-mythical, one-of-one Speed 12 which was a star of early 
Gran Turismo
 games. TVR was reborn in 2013 after software entrepreneur Les Edgar bought the rights to the brand; according to public records in the U.K., Edgar resigned from the company board earlier this month. 
MOTV Group was previously known as Charge Holdings, with subsidiary Charge Cars planning to build an electrified restomod version of the 1967 Ford Mustang before it, too, collapsed into bankruptcy. In 2025, Charge was saved by a group of investors and subsequently merged with TVR.
TVR
... and this is the 2017 version.
The official announcement of TVR’s latest return was accompanied by an image of what appears to be the 2017 concept car, now wrapped in a new disguise. Comparison of the images shows the same headlight position, panel lines, side-exit exhaust and identically patterned wheels. MOTV says the car will have a spaceframe chassis, a composite body, a V-8 engine with a manual transmission, and rear-wheel drive - all details aligning the 2017 version. 
The original plan was for the new Griffith to use the iStream production process created by Gordon Murray’s then technology company, but this subsidiary has since been sold to ForSeven, which subsequently acquired McLaren Automotive which presumably owns the rights. So It will be interesting to discover if this technology-sharing partnership remains in place. Similarly, there is no confirmation that Cosworth is still involved in fettling the Ford Coyote V-8 that was meant to power the car as was originally announced.

Beyond the above facts the official announcement was short on details beyond naming the senior executive team, these including CEO Paul Abercrombie and CTO Steve MacManus, who was formerly an engineering vice president at Tesla and who seems to have worked on the aborted Apple car project. 
Can TVR manage it this time? Given the soap opera of the brand’s recent history, there remains a substantial question mark over that one. As does the more relevant unknown for most 
R&T
 readers as to whether there is any chance a reborn Griffith would ever reach the U.S. It would be fun if it did, but let’s not get our hopes up yet.

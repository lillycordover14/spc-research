Recommendations for Executive Action
We are making the following four recommendations to DOD:

The Secretary of Defense, in consultation with the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staﬀ and the
Secretaries of the military departments, should develop an overarching munitions plan to
improve inventory levels that links munitions production and funding to total munitions
requirements and includes long-term goals, strategies to achieve goals, and metrics to gauge
progress in addressing munitions shortages. (Recommendation 1)

The Secretary of Defense should identify an oﬀice or entity and empower its leadership with
the authority to implement the munitions plan to address munitions challenges once the plan
is developed. (Recommendation 2)

The Secretary of Defense should ensure that the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition
and Sustainment, in coordination with the Secretaries of the military departments, sets
regular timelines for inventory reconciliation designed to facilitate the identification and
resolution of data related issues, such as the accurate aggregation of inventory data between
the Munitions Readiness Initiative tool and the services’ munition databases.
(Recommendation 3)

The Secretary of Defense should ensure that DOD’s Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence
Oﬀicer develops a data management plan that documents the standards and processes used
to aggregate munitions data and a data dictionary to define data sources. (Recommendation
4)

Introduction
The Department of Defense (DOD) recognizes that it must be prepared and ready to deter peer
adversaries through strength. 1 According to DOD, to ensure military readiness and to support
national security and the department’s mission, the department must maintain ready, reliable,
and lethal munitions. 2 However, the department has encountered challenges in ensuring that
the military services have suﬀicient inventories of munitions—including bombs, missiles, and
torpedoes—in the event a conflict should arise with a peer competitor.

In 2025, the Acting Assistant Secretary of Defense for Sustainment testified that DOD had a
shortage of several key munitions. 3 Moreover, the 2026 conflict with Iran has heightened the
importance of addressing challenges with the U.S. munitions stockpile and industrial base.



Objectives
House Report 118-529 accompanying a bill for the National Defense Authorization Act for
Fiscal Year 2025 includes a provision for us to assess DOD's and the military services'
munitions levels and requirements, including actions taken to mitigate any munitions
shortfalls. 4

This report is a public version of a classified report that we issued September 24, 2026. This
report assesses the extent to which (1) DOD and the services have taken steps to improve
munitions shortages and (2) DOD and the services track munitions inventory data.

The classified report included an objective on trends in the DOD munitions inventories,
persistent shortfalls, and how these shortfalls have aﬀected DOD’s ability to support war plans.
DOD deemed this information to be classified or sensitive, which must be protected from loss,
compromise, or inadvertent disclosure. Consequently, this public version excludes that
information.




Background

DOD Munitions
Military munitions include rockets, guided missiles, bombs, artillery ammunition, and
torpedoes, among other things. 5 Over the years, the military has become reliant on missiles,
especially precision guided, to execute military operations, according to Joint Staﬀ oﬀicials. 6
Missiles are used in ground, air, and naval operations and some can be launched at long
ranges to attack the enemy without risking U.S. forces. Munitions are tracked in many diﬀerent

ways, including by DOD Identification Code (DODIC) assigned by the Defense Logistics Agency.
DOD has thousands of individual DODICs, unique alphanumeric codes to identify specific
items of supply and essential to inventory management, according to oﬀicials from the

Munitions Industrial Base
Munitions production is comprised of the commercial and organic industrial base. The
commercial industrial base constitutes the largest element of the defense industrial base, both
in terms of resources, facilities and personnel employed as well as the value of the products
and services provided to the U.S. government. The commercial industrial base is dominated by
five large, publicly traded U.S. private companies. 7 They produce many of the most advanced
munitions necessary to support the National Defense Strategy and associated war plans. See
table 1 for examples of DOD munitions, the prime contractor, and the service managing the
munition.

Gaps in Planning and Leadership Hinder DOD
and the Services’ Efforts to Address Munitions
Shortages
DOD has multiple plans and eﬀorts to improve munitions shortages, but we found it does not
have an overarching munitions plan that identifies long-term goals, strategies to achieve its
goals, and metrics to gauge progress in improving munitions production to meet total
munitions requirements. Moreover, it has not identified an oﬀice and empowered leadership
to direct such eﬀorts, which hinders DOD’s and the services’ eﬀorts to address long-standing
munitions shortages.


DOD Has No Department-Wide Plan to Establish Long-
Term Goals and Measure Progress
DOD does not have an overarching munitions plan that identifies long-term goals, strategies to
achieve its goals, and metrics to gauge progress in improving munitions production to meet
total munitions requirements. Instead, there are several munitions-related plans across the
department intended to address specific aspects of munition challenges, such as inventory
shortages and production capacity. Since 2016, DOD has released three strategic plans that
address specific aspects of munitions, such as the defense industrial base and production of
munitions. These three plans are the Single Manager for Conventional Ammunition Industrial
Base Strategic Plan (2016), the National Defense Industrial Strategy (2023), and the Army




                                                                    DOD Munitions | GAO-27-109420 | Page 4
Ammunition Plant Modernization Plan (2025). 11 However, our analysis of these documents
shows that these plans do not link munitions production plans and funding to operationally
required munitions inventory levels and have other key gaps. 12 For example, the 2023 National
Defense Industrial Strategy focuses on strengthening the defense industrial base by addressing
challenges in many areas, including supply chains and workforce.13 However, the strategy does
not provide specific guidance on how DOD should increase the production of munitions to
meet requirements.

Our prior work on evidence-based policymaking defines a strategic goal as an outcome-
oriented statement of purpose that articulates what the organization wants to achieve to
advance its mission and address relevant problems, needs, and challenges.14 We have found
that sound planning can enhance an agency’s eﬀorts to identify and achieve its objectives
through the implementation of key elements, such as developing long-term goals, strategies to
achieve goals, and metrics to gauge progress. 15

DOD has taken steps in trying to address specific aspects of munitions, such as developing the
National Defense Industrial Strategy. However, these plans are not focused on munition
challenges and do not provide long-term goals and metrics to assess progress in meeting the
goals. Further, our analysis of the strategies and discussions with DOD oﬀicials demonstrates
DOD does not have munitions related goals and strategies that take into account current
global events. Munitions shortages persist and oﬀicials from the Joint Staﬀ, U.S. Indo-Pacific
Command (INDOPACOM), the Oﬀice of the Secretary of Defense, and the Air Force, Army, Navy,
and Marine Corps told us that DOD does not have an overarching plan to address munitions
challenges and achieve department-wide munitions requirement goals.

According to service and the Oﬀice of the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and
Sustainment (OUSD (A&S)) oﬀicials, in lieu of a plan, DOD and the services can be reactive to
global events rather than driven by total munitions requirements. For example, the Army’s
ramp up in production of 155 millimeter artillery shells to address shortages exacerbated by
U.S. support to allies and partners was not driven by a need to address DOD’s total munitions
requirement.

Without developing an overarching munitions plan that links munitions production and
funding to munitions total requirements and identifies long-term goals, strategies for
achieving such goals, and metrics to gauge progress, DOD and the services are not well-
positioned to eﬀectively address persistently low munitions inventories or risk investing in
munitions that are projected to exceed the total munition requirement. Such a plan could also
help guide key investment decisions as the department determines how to allocate at least
$25 billion appropriated by Congress in 2025, intended to enhance DOD resources for
munitions. 16

DOD Does Not Have a Dedicated Entity with Authority to
Improve Munitions Inventories
DOD does not have a dedicated oﬀice or entity with authority to improve munitions
inventories. Instead, leadership and authority on these issues are diﬀused across many
entities throughout the department from specific oﬀices to working groups. For example:

   •   OUSD (A&S) has a dedicated oﬀice for Platform and Weapon Portfolio Management,
       which includes munitions. However, as OUSD (A&S) oﬀicials describe, it does not have
       production and budgetary authority across DOD entities, such as combatant
       commands and services, responsible for munitions.

   •   OUSD (A&S)’s Oﬀice of Industrial Base Resilience analyzes the industrial base to
       identify critical gaps among many capabilities, including munitions.

   •   The Capability Program Executive, Ammunition and Energetics oﬀice handles
       development, procurement, and fielding of conventional munitions, like the 155
       millimeter, as well as energetic components for missiles and long-range precision
       missiles which are managed by other oﬀices within DOD.

   •   The services’ respective program oﬀices, such as the Navy’s Tomahawk Weapon
       System Program Oﬀice, manage their own munitions inventories for their own
       services.

According to DOD, it has also established several working groups with multiple oﬀices to
address munition shortages and production challenges, such as supply chain risk
assessments. While these working groups bring decision makers together to coordinate and
build consensus on various munition issues, we found the working groups do not have
decision making authority or empowered leaders to address and be held accountable for
improving the munition inventory shortages or for meeting the total munition requirements.
For example:

   •   The Munitions Acceleration Council, according to oﬀicials, was established by the
       Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment in July 2025. It intends to
       accelerate the production of missiles, including precision guided missiles, through
       novel contracting approaches including the use of multi-year procurement funding,
       according to DOD oﬀicials. However, according to a senior OUSD (A&S) oﬀicial, as of
       November 2026, the Council does not have a formal signed charter.

   •   The Joint Industrial Base Working Group, established in December 2019, is DOD’s
       primary mechanism for exchanging information about industrial base matters across
       the department. The Joint Industrial Base Working Group is co-chaired by the
       Industrial Base Policy oﬀice and the Defense Contract Management Agency. According
       to OUSD (A&S), dozens of oﬀices are focused on specific aspects of the defense
       industrial base, which feeds information to the Joint Industrial Base Working Group.




                                                                    DOD Munitions | GAO-27-109420 | Page 6
        This information is intended to provide senior leaders with a holistic view of the
        industrial base. For example, the group shares information from multiple oﬀices within
        DOD about emerging technologies and critical sectors of the industrial base. However,
        this working group is focused on addressing challenges throughout the defense

Standards for Internal Control in the Federal Government states that agency management
should establish an organizational structure, assign responsibility, and delegate authority to
achieve the entity’s objectives. 17 Further, our prior work on an integrated portfolio
management approach to product development states that successful management requires
committed leadership that empowers managers to make decisions, and holds them
accountable for the outcomes achieved at all levels of an organization.18

However, DOD has not identified an oﬀice or entity and delegated to it authority that
empowers the leadership to make decisions that improve munitions inventories department-
wide and hold them accountable for the outcomes achieved, such as improving munitions
inventory levels. Joint Staﬀ and oﬀicials from the Air Force, Army, Navy, and Marine Corps told
us that there could be a benefit to developing an overarching plan and assigning an oﬀice or
entity to lead its implementation. Further, service oﬀicials noted that identifying an oﬀice and
delegating leadership and authority to it to assess munitions improvement actions would be
valuable to providing oversight to ensure progress is made. Service oﬀicials stated that
without an oﬀice with dedicated leadership to address munitions challenges, it may be
diﬀicult to realize progress in addressing munitions shortages at any level.

Without identifying and empowering an oﬀice to lead the implementation of a munitions plan
to address munition challenges, DOD is not well-positioned to address persistently low
munitions inventories and risks being unprepared for future conflicts.



DOD and the Services Track Munitions
Inventory Data, but Oversight of Department-
Wide Munitions Data Is Limited
The military services maintain separate data systems to track their munitions inventories,
which feed into a department-wide munitions data tool. However, we found that DOD has
limited oversight over this tool and does not fulfill some related responsibilities. For example,
we found that DOD has not regularly conducted inventory reconciliations with the military
services and has not developed a data management plan that describes the methodology
used to aggregate data across the services into this tool.




                                                                       DOD Munitions | GAO-27-109420 | Page 7
DOD and the Military Services Have Separate Data Systems
to Track Munitions Inventory
The military services use their own data systems to track their munitions inventories and DOD,
specifically OUSD (A&S), integrates the services’ data into a single system that decision-makers
use to identify DOD-wide munitions inventory. Each military service is responsible for
maintaining authoritative and accountable records of its worldwide munitions inventories.
DOD sets various requirements related to the maintenance of these records, such as
maintaining specific data elements and completing monthly physical inventory inspections of
certain items. 19

The Munitions Readiness Initiative (MRI) is the department-wide munitions data tool managed
by OUSD (A&S) and contained on DOD’s Advana platform. 20 MRI aggregates the services'
munitions data systems’ information into a near real-time, department-wide inventory view
through a variety of dashboards. In addition to reporting munitions inventories, MRI displays
related information, such as munitions readiness and supply chain links between specific
munitions types and the industrial base. In total, MRI pulls from 37 DOD data sources through
a mix of automated connections and some manually updated data (see fig. 2).

In 2022, USD (A&S) published a memorandum stating that the MRI tool will be used to provide
the Secretary of Defense and other senior leaders with access to near real-time munitions
inventories, rather than the individual service data systems. 21 According to a senior OUSD
(A&S) oﬀicial, prior to publication of the memorandum, DOD oﬀicials would request munitions
inventory data manually from each service, which could take several days to obtain and
aggregate into a worldwide view.


DOD Has Limited Oversight of the Department-Wide
Munitions Data System
We found two factors that limit OUSD (A&S)’s oversight over the MRI tool used by senior
leaders across DOD. First, OUSD (A&S) does not consistently reconcile MRI munitions inventory
data against the services’ own data systems. Second, OUSD (A&S) does not have a data
management plan detailing how data is aggregated in the MRI tool from across the services.




                                                                                                            DOD Munitions | GAO-27-109420 | Page 9
Inconsistent Munitions Inventory Reconciliation

The Oﬀice of the Deputy Assistant Secretary of Defense for Logistics has primary responsibility
for reconciling munition inventories with the services. 22 Additionally, DOD’s Data Product
Management guidance outlines best practices and guidance for building and managing data
products. 23 This guidance highlights the importance of updating information to ensure
freshness and relevance, such as regularly handling revisions or changes.

However, we found there has not been a consistent reconciliation of munitions inventory data
between OUSD (A&S) and the services to assist in the identification and resolution of any data
related issues, such as the accurate aggregation of inventory data between MRI and the
services’ munition databases. According to a senior OUSD (A&S) oﬀicial, the oﬀice was
reluctant to impose additional responsibilities on the services for maintaining data to meet
MRI’s needs because they recognize that the services already face numerous responsibilities in
managing their own munitions inventory data.

By establishing consistent, regular timelines for reconciling information about changes to
munitions, OUSD (A&S) and the services can ensure senior leaders are regularly receiving high-
quality data on which to base their decisions. A senior OUSD (A&S) oﬀicial told us that it would
be helpful to reconcile changes across the services on a more consistent basis, particularly for
high-priority munitions, to ensure the correct display of service information in MRI. In
addition, oﬀicials from all four services said more regular reconciliations related to munition
inventory changes would help ensure the accuracy of their information being displayed by
MRI.

Lack of a Data Management Plan

The MRI tool standardizes the services’ unique data in order to aggregate munitions inventory
into a single department-wide view, but we found the MRI tool does not have a data
management plan that documents this process and DOD has not developed a data dictionary
for the tool. As previously noted, MRI pulls munitions-related data from systems owned and
managed by components across DOD. MRI also captures munitions inventory data obtained
from each of the services’ data sources and standardizes these data to aggregate the
munitions inventory into a single department-wide view. For example, MRI standardizes each
munition’s unique identifier to allow for cross-service comparisons. 24 OUSD (A&S) oﬀicials told
us that although subject matter experts make data aggregation decisions based on several
factors, including ad-hoc service inventory reconciliation discussions, the aggregation process
has not been documented in a data management plan (see call out box) and a data dictionary
that provides information on services’ unique munitions identifiers.

DOD’s Data Stewardship Guidebook describes roles, responsibilities, and best practices for
governing and managing data. 25 For example, DOD chief data oﬀicers are responsible for
managing the data assets of an agency, including developing and overseeing the
implementation of data-related strategies and plans, such as data management plans. 26
According to OUSD (A&S) and DOD’s Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Oﬀice oﬀicials, the
Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Oﬀicer executes the responsibilities of the Chief Data
Oﬀicer for DOD. Further, the Data Stewardship Guidebook identifies producing management
policies, such as a comprehensive data dictionary that thoroughly defines and describes all
data fields, as among the responsibilities of data managers. 27

According to OUSD (A&S) oﬀicials, the department rolled out the system without a data
management plan, as it was developing the system iteratively to respond to senior leader
needs. Without developing a data management plan to document this process for the MRI tool
and a data dictionary to define data sources, DOD is limited in its ability to monitor how data is
aggregated or whether certain data quality standards are met, potentially aﬀecting the
accuracy of information in MRI. This is particularly important given the breadth of information
compiled in MRI, sourced from across the department, and managed by other data stewards. 28



Conclusions
Meeting munition requirements and rebuilding munition inventories is a highly complex
challenge that will require a department-wide solution. While DOD is taking steps to focus on
munitions shortages, it is not positioned to address these gaps. DOD does not have an
overarching plan or designated and empowered leadership to improve munition inventories
and meet its munitions requirements. By not identifying long-term goals, strategies to achieve
them, and metrics to gauge progress, DOD cannot be sure that its actions will address its
munition shortfalls.

DOD relies on the MRI tool as its department-wide inventory tool, but it has neither
consistently conducted data reconciliations nor developed a data management plan. Without




                                                                      DOD Munitions | GAO-27-109420 | Page 11
implementing these steps, DOD decision-makers who use the MRI tool cannot ensure that it is
providing the most accurate view of current munitions inventory to determine which
munitions are available in the event of a conflict.

We are making four recommendations to DOD, as listed in our Recommendations section.

How GAO Did This Study
House Report 118-529 accompanying a bill for the National Defense Authorization Act for
Fiscal Year 2025 includes a provision for us to assess the Department of Defense’s (DOD) and
the military services' munitions levels and requirements, including actions taken to mitigate
any munitions shortfalls. 29 This report is a public version of a classified report that we issued
September 24, 2026. This report assesses the extent to which (1) DOD and the services have
taken steps to improve munitions shortages, and (2) DOD and the services track munitions
inventory data. The classified report included an objective on trends in the DOD munitions
inventories, persistent shortfalls, and how these shortfalls have aﬀected DOD’s ability to
support war plans. DOD deemed this information to be classified or sensitive, which must be
protected from loss, compromise, or inadvertent disclosure. Consequently, this public version
excludes that information.

Although the information provided in this report is more limited, it uses the same
methodology as the classified report. For both objectives, we reviewed and analyzed relevant
DOD information, such as the Joint Staﬀ and services munitions data, munitions related
strategic plans, and Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment
memorandum on munitions accountability.

and interviewed oﬀicials from the following Army Ammunition Plants and a Munitions Center:
McAlester Army Ammunition Plant, Holston Army Ammunition Plant, Radford Army
Ammunition Plant, and Letterkenny Munitions Center. We selected these Army ammunition
facilities based on several criteria, including the type of munitions and components produced
at each location, as well as if the facility was government owned and operated or if it was
government owned and contractor operated.

To address our first objective, we interviewed oﬀicials from the Joint Staﬀ, OUSD (A&S), and
the military services to discuss actions DOD and the services have taken to address munitions
shortages. We asked OUSD (A&S), the Joint Staﬀ, and each of the services to identify any
strategic plans related to munitions. Based on those discussions, oﬀicials told us that there are
three primary strategic plans related to munitions. The plans are the 1) Single Manager for
Conventional Ammunition Industrial Base Strategic Plan (2016), 2) National Defense Industrial
Strategy (2023), and 3) Army Ammunition Plant Modernization Plan (2025). 30 We analyzed these
three strategic plans to determine if they included an overarching munitions improvement
plan for DOD with metrics and milestones. We also interviewed oﬀicials from the Joint Staﬀ,
OUSD (A&S), the Joint Program Executive Oﬀice, Armaments and Ammunition to discuss these
strategic plans.

We assessed these plans and compared them to GAO’s Standards for Internal Control and
leading practices for sound planning. 31 Specifically, significant to this audit was the call for
agency management to define goals that articulate what the organization wants to achieve to




                                                                      DOD Munitions | GAO-27-109420 | Page 13
advance its mission and to implement key elements of sound planning such as developing
long-term goals, strategies to achieve goals, and metrics to gauge progress. We also applied
GAO’s best practices for integrated portfolio management, which states that successful
management requires committed leadership that empowers managers to make decisions, and
holds them accountable for the outcomes achieved at all levels of an organization. 32 We also
assessed documentary and testimonial evidence on DOD’s munitions governing entities and
compared it to federal internal control standards and our best practices for integrated
portfolio management to determine if DOD had assigned responsibility, and delegated
authority to achieve the entity’s objectives. 33 Moreover, we assessed if DOD had empowered
an oﬀice to make decisions and hold others accountable for the outcomes achieved.

To address our second objective, we interviewed oﬀicials from the Joint Staﬀ, Army, Navy,
Marine Corps, Air Force, and the OUSD (A&S) to discuss DOD’s department-wide munitions
data tools. We reviewed policies from OUSD (A&S) and the services regarding the tracking of
munitions. Specifically, significant to this audit was OUSD (A&S)’s Munitions Accountability
Reporting Memorandum for the Munitions Readiness Initiative (MRI). 34 We compared the
requirements within the memo, specifically the requirement for Oﬀice of the Deputy Assistant
Secretary of Defense for Logistics to reconcile munition inventories with the services, to the
actions that DOD and the services had taken when discrepancies were identified. We
interviewed knowledgeable oﬀicials within OUSD (A&S), Joint Staﬀ, and each of the services
about any challenges or shortcomings regarding the way MRI displays data compared to the
services authoritative data systems information. We also requested MRI’s data management
plan to compare to the guidance outlined by the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Oﬀicer
in Data Stewardship Guidebook regarding the development and oversight of a plan. 35 DOD had
not developed a plan for MRI and therefore we could not conduct an assessment.

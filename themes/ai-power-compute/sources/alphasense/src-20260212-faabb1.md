SMART SUMMARY

Summary

The conversation explored strategies for powering large-scale data centers, comparing technologies such as microturbines, solid oxide fuel cells, and renewables, with a focus on cost, lead times, and operational flexibility. The discussion also covered procurement models, supplier landscape, and the practical limitations of current power generation solutions.

EXPERT BACKGROUND

- Regional Senior Data Center Manager with direct experience overseeing power procurement, installation, and operation for large-scale data centers, including evaluation and deployment of various on-site power generation technologies such as microturbines, solid oxide fuel cells, and renewable energy solutions.

EXPERT SENTIMENT

- The expert views modular, microgrid-based solutions as the most practical approach for powering hyperscale data centers, especially given the need for rapid ramp-up and flexibility.
- While solid oxide fuel cell technology (notably from Bloom Energy) offers attractive lead times and modularity, the expert is cautious about its scalability and flexibility, expecting it to remain a minority share of the overall power mix for the foreseeable future.
- The expert anticipates continued experimentation and diversification in power sourcing strategies across the industry, with no single technology likely to dominate in the near term.

TAKEAWAYS

- Microgrid solutions combining multiple technologies (microturbines, fuel cells, renewables, battery storage) are preferred for their modularity, cost predictability, and reliability.
- Solid oxide fuel cells (Bloom Energy) are valued for short lead times (as little as 60 days) and modular deployment, but have higher CapEx and fixed output limitations, making them suitable for up to 15%-20% of a data center's power mix.
- Midsize microturbines (from suppliers like Siemens, GE Vernova) are cost-effective and flexible but have longer lead times (16-24 months) and are often used in multiples for large facilities.
- Levelized cost of electricity (LCOE) varies: Bloom Energy SOFCs are around 9-10¢/kWh, microturbines 7-8¢/kWh, and solar (with storage and land costs) 11-13¢/kWh; utility grid power remains the cheapest but is less reliable.
- Energy-as-a-service models are becoming more popular, reducing operational burden and CapEx lock-in for customers, and allowing for more predictable power procurement.

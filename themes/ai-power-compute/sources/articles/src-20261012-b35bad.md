The economics of AI inference are breaking. Demand for tokens is compounding faster than costs are falling, and the old playbook of throwing more GPUs at the problem has hit a wall. In a recent episode of the AI Engineer podcast, Daniel Kim of Cerebras and Michelle, co-founder of Gimlet Labs, argued that the next wave of gains will come not from faster kernels but from disaggregating workloads across specialized, heterogeneous hardware. The core insight: prefill and decode have opposite needs. Prefill is compute-bound; decode is memory-bound. Running both on the same GPU wastes capacity. Michelle detailed a taxonomy of disaggregation strategies — splitting prefill from decode, using small draft models for speculative decoding, and even splitting attention from feedforward networks within a single layer. Her company, Gimlet Labs, reports 3–10x faster inference than homogeneous fleets at the same power budget by mapping each workload piece to the best-suited chip across NVIDIA, AMD, Trainium, Cerebras, and others. Cerebras and AWS have already productionized one path: Trainium handles prefill, EFA transfers the KV cache, and Cerebras CS3 handles decode, yielding 5x high-speed token capacity. The speakers also bet that AI agents will soon write optimized kernels and even design custom silicon, closing a recursive loop. The unresolved risk: communication overhead, which already eats half the gains from tensor parallelism, could re-emerge as the tax on multi-silicon routing.

For two years, the AI industry has treated inference as a speed problem. But the bottleneck is not how fast a chip can multiply numbers. It is how quickly it can move data. And according to Daniel Kim, who works on AI and AI agents at Cerebras, a modern GPU spends up to 90% of its time idle during token generation, waiting for memory. This single fact, laid out in a recent conversation on the AI Engineer podcast, reframes the entire debate over AI hardware. The future of inference is not a faster chip. It is a smarter division of labor across many different chips.

Token demand is compounding faster than cost is falling

The conversation begins with a stark economic backdrop. Kim cites global AI compute spend of $765 billion this year, projected to hit as much as $1.6 trillion by 2031. Google, he notes, reported serving 480 trillion tokens per month at its I/O event last year. As of last month, that figure had jumped to 3.2 quadrillion tokens per month — a 6.7x increase in roughly a year.

Against that demand curve, efficiency gains are real but insufficient. Kim points to Llama 3.3 70B, which he says is now 25% cheaper to serve than at release while running 3.6 times faster. That is progress, but it does not close the gap with a 6.7x demand spike. The industry is running on a treadmill that accelerates faster than it can sprint.

This is why the Pareto frontier — the trade-off between token speed and throughput — has become a product decision, not an academic abstraction. Kim cites Claude Code Fast Mode, which he says costs roughly 6x the tokens to serve a single token compared to regular Claude in exchange for roughly twice the speed. Anthropic is deliberately choosing the right edge of the curve: speed over throughput.

"So for that product, they're really optimizing towards the right of the Pareto frontier, where they're getting the speed, but at the cost of not being able to serve as many tokens."

Decode is memory-bound, and that changes everything

The technical foundation of the entire talk is the autoregressive loop. To generate a single token, the model must read all of its weights from memory into compute. This is why Kim says compute sits idle up to 90% of the time during decode. The binding constraint is memory bandwidth, not FLOPs.

The hardware comparison Kim draws is stark. An H100 moves data at 3.35 terabytes per second. Cerebras, using on-chip SRAM instead of external memory, claims 21,000 terabytes per second — roughly 7,000x the H100's bandwidth. On that basis, he says Cerebras serves decode at 3,000 tokens per second versus 600 on an H100. These are the speakers' own figures, presented without external benchmarks, and should be read as positioning rather than audited truth. But the structural point stands: the memory-bandwidth asymmetry between architectures is real and enormous.

That asymmetry explains why prefill and decode want opposite machines. Prefill computes K and V vectors for every prompt token and produces the first output token. It parallelizes beautifully and is compute-bound. Decode steps one token at a time, re-reading the entire KV cache at each step. It is memory-bound. Kim gives the user-visible symptom: the pause after you hit enter in ChatGPT before the first token appears is prefill. The smooth streaming after it is decode.

"So this really means that Cerebras is really built for decode. So we can serve tokens at 3,000 tokens per second versus an H100 serving it at 600 tokens per second for decode."

The sharpest number in this section is the KV cache arithmetic. For a 70-billion-parameter model, Kim's rule of thumb is one gigabyte of additional KV cache per 3,000 tokens, per user request. A coding agent running 128K context consumes roughly 42 gigabytes of KV cache for that single request. Batch eight such requests and memory becomes the wall. Paged KV cache, by his account, lets a system serve three times more requests than the unpaged approach by eliminating reserved-but-unused blocks.

Chunked prefill addresses a failure mode that has become acute with token-hungry coding agents at 200K, 500K, or even one-million-token context. While a long prefill runs, no other prefill or decode request gets through. One coding agent can stall every chat user on the same pipeline. Chopping long prefills into smaller pieces lets the scheduler interleave decode and prefill traffic.

The overarching message: the runtime tricks that carried inference from 2022 to now have been harvested. Further gains must come from systems architecture, not kernel tuning.

Disaggregation is the new frontier, and there is no single best form

Prefill/decode disaggregation splits these into two independently scalable pools. A summarization-heavy deployment can scale prefill up and decode down without either phase stalling the other.

Then she extends the idea twice more. Speculative decoding disaggregates by model rather than by phase. A smaller draft model proposes candidate tokens quickly; the full model runs a verify pass. In her worked example, producing nine tokens takes nine forward passes on the full model in the standard approach, but only three with speculative decoding — provided the draft model is fast enough that its own cost is negligible.

Attention/FFN disaggregation splits within a single layer. Attention does heavy KV cache access and is memory-bound. The feedforward network does dense matrix multiplication and is compute-bound. For models around 3 trillion parameters, where even one layer is resource-intensive, Michelle says it makes sense to run attention on high-memory-bandwidth machines and FFN on machines that do fast matmuls, ping-ponging across machines within the model.

Her explicit caveat is the most important sentence in the section.

"There is no best form of disag. There is actually no best hardware. It really depends on the trade-offs that you have depending on what your model is, your workload, and what you need for latency and what you need for throughput guarantees."

Heterogeneous silicon buys 3–10x over homogeneous fleets

Gimlet's product thesis is a multi-silicon inference cloud that decomposes a workload into fine-grained pieces, characterizes each piece's resource needs, and maps it to the best-suited chip. Michelle names the candidate hardware explicitly: NVIDIA, AMD, Trainium, Cerebras, d-Matrix, Intel, and Gaudi. Her claim is 3 to 10 times faster inference than homogeneous systems at the same power budget.

Kim's earlier Pareto framing returns here as the measurement axis: x-axis is token speed, y-axis is throughput. Against a baseline of prefill/decode disaggregation across homogeneous GPUs, adding speculative decoding across those same GPUs bends the curve rightward. Running the small draft models on hardware suited to them — she names d-Matrix and Cerebras — produces what she describes as 10x the interactivity at the same throughput.

Cerebras and AWS have a concrete production partnership in this vein. The reference materials confirm that Cerebras' OpenAI relationship has been a source of intense investor scrutiny. Sam Altman publicly reaffirmed the partnership on X, calling Cerebras "a close partner" with "deep engagement pushing on the frontiers of speed." But the SemiAnalysis report that OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Sol "Ultrafast" mode would run on Nvidia hardware sent Cerebras shares down roughly 20% in a week, before Altman's comments triggered a rebound. That episode underscores how much of the inference-hardware story is still being written in real time.

The AWS partnership is the most concrete test case. The flow: AWS Trainium serves prefill, the EFA service transfers the KV cache, and Cerebras CS3 systems serve the final tokens.

Kim's stated result: 5x high-speed token capacity in the same footprint.

Agents are being pointed at the inference stack itself

The closing argument is the most provocative: the same agentic workloads driving token demand can be used to relieve it. Michelle describes KForge, Gimlet's agent for generating optimized kernels. It takes hardware profiles and workload profiles as input and treats kernel optimization as a brute-force search problem. Her claim: in under a week of running, KForge sped up GPT-OSS-120B on AMD hardware by 33% end-to-end — on top of kernels that had already been optimized by hand.

The reason kernel work is a good fit for agents, she argues, is that optimizations vary drastically by model, by hardware, by hardware generation, and by workload shape. That combinatorial space is too expensive to cover with human hand-tuning. It matters more for a multi-silicon cloud, which must support a wide hardware range.

Kim extends the idea up and down the stack: agents for incident response and uptime, agents for bringing up new models on all hardware on day one, and just-in-time compilers custom-built for a specific model and workload. He cites Anthropic building a compiler in two weeks as an existing demonstration.

Michelle closes the loop with custom silicon: agents helping design chips hyper-tuned for specific models, which train better AI, which designs the next generation of chips.

"And you can go and take agents to the next level and use them to help us build and design custom silicon that is hyper tuned for specific models or your specific workload. This can then be used to train even better and smarter and faster AI, which then can also be used then to design the next generation of custom silicon. And this loop keeps going on and on, and it's a recursive loop."

Kim's own earlier example is the warning that should temper enthusiasm. Tensor parallelism delivered 4x the compute but only 2x the speed because of communication overhead. Disaggregation — whether across pools, across models, or within a layer — introduces new coordination costs. KV cache transfer between pools, cross-machine ping-ponging, and scheduling across heterogeneous fleets all carry a tax the talk does not quantify.

"Throwing more GPUs at the problem does get you faster tokens, but it requires exponentially more energy to serve a single token as you put in more compute at the problem because of the communication overhead."

The open question for the next year of deployments is whether multi-silicon routing avoids that tax at scale, or reintroduces it in a different place. The Cerebras–AWS Trainium partnership will be the most visible test. If it holds up in production — not just in a benchmark — the homogeneous GPU fleet may start to look like a transitional technology. If the coordination overhead eats the gains, the industry will have learned a hard lesson about the difference between a compelling architecture and a deployable one.

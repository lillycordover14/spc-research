Fireworks AI is in talks to double its valuation to 40 billion dollars&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
An inference startup most people outside AI circles have never heard of is closing in on a valuation that would put it ahead of companies that actually build the models.

Fireworks AI is a Redwood City startup. It helps companies run AI models cheaply and fast, and it's now in early talks to raise new funding at a valuation between $30 billion and $40 billion, Business Insider reported on October 20. That's roughly double what the company was worth just three months ago. Back in July it closed a $1.505 billion Series D at a $17.5 billion valuation, led by Atreides Management, Index Ventures and TCV, with Nvidia joining as a follow-on investor.

Founder Lin Qiao isn't new to this stack. She left Meta in 2022, where she led more than 300 engineers building and scaling PyTorch, the open-source framework that still trains and runs a large share of the world's AI models. She started Fireworks in Redwood City a month before ChatGPT launched, betting that whoever controlled the plumbing between a trained model and a paying customer would matter as much as whoever trained the model in the first place.

That bet is paying off. Fireworks now processes more than 40 trillion tokens a day, up from roughly 15 trillion a year ago, and has crossed $1 billion in annualized revenue, about five times what it was generating last year. More than 95% of that traffic comes from models customers have fine-tuned on their own proprietary data, not off-the-shelf open weights. That's the part of the business Fireworks actually sells: leased GPU capacity plus the tooling to fine-tune and serve a model in production - not a frontier model of its own.

Baseten closed a $1.5 billion Series F in June at a $13 billion valuation, barely five months after a $300 million round valued it at $5 billion. Together AI, another rival chasing the same enterprise inference workloads, closed an $800 million round at an $8.3 billion valuation after crossing $1 billion in annualized revenue of its own. The pattern across all three is the same: valuations are stepping up two, three, sometimes four times in a matter of months, far faster than revenue is actually growing underneath them.

Here's the thing worth sitting with. Fireworks, Baseten and Together don't train frontier models, and they don't have the kind of proprietary data or model weights that create a durable moat. What they sell, fast and cheap model-serving, is increasingly commoditized, and the hyperscalers (AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure) all offer some version of it too. A customer who switches from Fireworks to Baseten loses very little. That's not a reason to assume these companies collapse. It's a reason their valuations should track revenue multiples closer to infrastructure resellers than to AI labs, and right now they aren't.

Citadel's Ken Griffin has been the most prominent voice naming the mismatch directly. Speaking at the Future of Global Markets conference, Griffin said the AI boom's real payoff could take decades rather than years. The dream "could be 20 years... it can be 30 years" before the technology's value gets properly sorted between winners and losers, he warned, much like the dot-com crash separated Amazon from Pets.com. He wasn't talking about Fireworks specifically. But a 230% valuation jump in three months for a company that leases someone else's GPUs is exactly the kind of math his warning describes.

None of this means the money is wrong to chase inference. Every major AI lab needs somewhere to actually run the models it trains, and that demand is real and growing. Nvidia followed its own Series D money into Fireworks as a strategic investor, rather than just selling it chips. That says the chipmaker sees the inference layer as a long-term fixture, not a bubble play it wants to avoid. But a strategic investor backing a company for supply-chain reasons is a different signal than a pure financial investor paying 40 billion dollars for a business that reported roughly 1 billion in revenue months earlier.

Fireworks declined to comment on the specific terms of the talks, and Business Insider's report noted the round has not been finalized. If it closes anywhere near that range, Fireworks will be worth more than most publicly traded enterprise software companies, with a fraction of the public scrutiny. The rest of the inference sector will have a new ceiling to measure itself against.

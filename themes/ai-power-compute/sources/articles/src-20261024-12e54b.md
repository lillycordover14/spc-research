Together AI's Dan Fu: Inference Now Burns 12x More Compute Than Training, and DeepSeek's 80% Margins Prove It
Add preferred source
For years the AI industry has judged models by how much compute it takes to train them. Together AI research lead Dan Fu argues that framing is mispriced: serving a single model now consumes roughly 6x more compute than training it per year, and about 12x over a two-year useful life. The consequence is that inference engineering — not model quality — is what decides whether an AI business makes money. DeepSeek's roughly 80% margins, and the 96% cache hit rate behind them, are the proof case. Speaking on Y Combinator's Paper Club, Fu and three other practitioners (Ollama's Jeffrey Morgan, Luminal's Joe Fiotti, SGLang's Liang Cheng) walk through why the decode step is the bottleneck, why the same open model can perform wildly differently across providers, and where the next gains will come from. The core tension they leave unresolved: verifying quality across a fast-growing market of inference providers is expensive, slow, and nobody has solved it at scale.

If you want to know where the money in AI actually goes, stop looking at training runs. Look at the decode step.

When DeepSeek published its serving numbers earlier this year, the figure that stunned practitioners wasn't a benchmark score. It was the margin — roughly 80%, implied by the economics of running its own models. Dan Fu, research lead at Together AI, says the number "shocked" people when it surfaced, and the reason is simple: DeepSeek didn't get there by building a better model. It got there by being brutally efficient at serving one, he explained on Y Combinator's Paper Club podcast.

That flips the conventional scoreboard. Most AI research still optimizes what Fu calls "iso-train-flops" — how much compute a model needs to reach a given capability. But the compute that actually gets spent, month after month, is at inference. DeepSeek is the rare lab publishing both sides of the ledger. By its figures — which Fu cautions are "old data" — training DeepSeek-V3 took about 2.8 million H800 GPU hours, while serving burns through roughly 43,000 H800 hours 
per day
.

Run the math and the imbalance is stark: about 6x more inference than training flops per year for a single weight file. Assume a model has a two-year useful life, and that climbs to roughly 12x.

"We're at about 6x more flops on inference than train for that weight file per year," Fu said, "and if you believe the useful life of a weight file, say two years, it should be 12x more important."

The decoders, not the trainers, are where the money is

To understand why DeepSeek's margins exist, you have to understand that generating text happens in two phases with completely different physics.

Prefill
 is the phase where the model reads your prompt — it's compute-bound, and GPU utilization can reach around 50%. 
Decode
 is where the model writes its answer one token at a time — it's memory-bound, because every single token requires loading the entire model's weights from GPU memory to the compute unit. That gives decode a utilization rate Fu puts at perhaps 5%.

"If Jensen Huang is guaranteeing 2 trillion FP8 flops, you're not getting that. It's just not even close — maybe you get half of that. And in decode, it's even worse. It might be like 5%."

This asymmetry is the whole story. Prefill can churn through tokens in massive parallel batches. Decode can't — it's serialized by design, and it's where the cost lives.

The gap between 1.5 million prefill tokens per second and 14,000 decode tokens per second is the entire economic story. And the 96% cache hit rate is the payoff from a deliberately aggressive bet: DeepSeek retains the memory of prior conversations for 72 hours, across petabytes of user state, so that returning users don't force the system to re-read their entire context.

That bet is workload-dependent, and Fu is candid about the edge case. Agentic coding traffic — where a tool loops back and forth with a long shared context — caches beautifully. Casual chat doesn't. "My mom is constantly starting brand new chats with ChatGPT," he said, generating no reusable cache and forcing repeated prefill every time. The 96% is an average, not a guarantee.

Three layers of cost-cutting, and only one of them is the model

Fu buckets the techniques behind DeepSeek's margins into three layers, and the surprising takeaway is that model architecture is only one of them — and arguably not the most important.

The middle layer is where the cleverest borrowings live. Modern inference engines treat GPU memory the way databases treat disk — with caches, eviction policies, and page tables. The top layer is the one most builders overlook: sometimes the cheapest token is the one you never generate, because a small model or a cached answer handled it first.

Underneath all of it sits a scheduling problem unique to LLMs. When a request lands, the system must decide which GPU replica handles it. Classic load balancing would round-robin. But that throws away KV-cache locality — sending the same conversation to the same GPU means it can reuse prior computation. The router is solving load balancing and cache residency at once, and getting it wrong quietly multiplies your bill.

Why the same open model behaves differently depending on who serves it

If Fu's section is about the plumbing, Jeffrey Morgan's is about the water quality — and his claim is the one that should worry anyone building on open models.

Morgan, CEO of Ollama, argues that the quality gap between the 
best
 and 
worst
 provider serving the 
same
 open model weights can be larger than the gap between open and closed models. He points to a 2024 study that tested 31 different Llama endpoints and found some performing closer to GPT-3.5 than to other deployments of supposedly identical weights.

"Open model tokens are not all the same," he said. And the problem compounds in agentic workloads: a 10% per-call accuracy deviation across a 15-step coding task can produce up to 79% wrong final outcomes. One bad step poisons the chain.

"One bad apple can ruin the bunch... you might have a customer experience that's kind of the least common denominator."

Ollama began in 2023 as a way to run models locally on a laptop or a single H100. As Kimi, GLM, and MiniMax shipped 500-billion-to-1-trillion-parameter models, it launched a cloud service that now routes across many providers — which is exactly why auditing quality became an existential concern. Morgan says Ollama has become one of the largest independent clouds for open models in the US and Europe, with a large share of the Fortune 500 deploying it.

The cheapest technique — probing a single token's probability against a baseline — has a flaw Morgan is unusually willing to name. "You're really only detecting drift from the past versus comparing it to another reference model," he said. "So if you're starting from a place that wasn't serving it correctly, this isn't super helpful." A provider that was always mediocre won't set off the alarm.

The good news is that competition is starting to enforce quality without anyone auditing it. When Moonshot released its Kimi K2 tool verifier — a public scoreboard ranking providers on tool-call accuracy — it changed behavior fast. "This was awesome because it caused everyone to really kind of enter a race to make sure that the tool calls were accurate," Morgan said. MiniMax followed with an equivalent. Public scoreboards, it turns out, do more than private monitoring.

Morgan's structural fix is more radical: labs should ship the stack they actually use to serve their models, not just the weights plus example code written by a different team. He cites DeepSeek's DeepInfer release as the model to follow — give people the weights 
and
 a consistent serving stack, ideally hitting that $38-per-GPU-hour cost target. Labs are also increasingly shipping their own pre-quantized versions, a reversal from the days when third parties compressed models to fit a MacBook.

His forward bet: the routing decision of the future won't be provider-versus-provider. It'll be local-versus-cloud, as capable small models like Recursive's Flint run on an M5 MacBook.

The compiler that searches instead of being written

Joe Fiotti, CEO of Luminal, attacks the same cost problem from further down the stack. His thesis: NVIDIA's four-trillion-dollar valuation rests substantially on CUDA being a well-supported software ecosystem, and mapping models efficiently onto complex hardware is still largely unsolved.

The conventional approach — MLIR-style compilers with hand-written rules — scales headcount exponentially with every new model, backend, and data type. Fiotti's description of the status quo is blunt: "a compiler team of 500 or 1,000 people. And even then, a very imperfect compiler."

Luminal takes a different route. It decomposes models into roughly 14 or 15 primitive operations — add, multiply, sum — with no matrix-multiply op at all, since a matmul becomes a broadcasted multiply plus a sum reduction. Because the operation set is restricted, the compiler can reason analytically about memory movement and prune poor options before ever benchmarking them. Fiotti calls the intermediate representation "PyTorch complete, but not Turing complete."

Then it does something unusual: instead of building an analytical cost model to predict which program will be fastest, it generates candidates, compiles them, and 
times them on real hardware
. Fiotti's stance is that the best cost model is no cost model — a full forward-pass profiling run takes under 10 milliseconds, so about a thousand experiments fit into a single second. The search explores "one percent of one percent" of the possible space, landing on a local optimum he says is 99% as good as the global one.

And he's happy to trade compile time for runtime. Inference customers don't care if compilation takes a day, he argues, when it buys a 1-to-10% speedup across millions of GPU-hours.

Luminal runs roughly 80 to 90 trillion tokens a month across NVIDIA, AMD, TPUs, Trainium, and other chips — with eight employees. Fiotti claims the company has "solved compiler company monetization," a rare boast given how many compiler startups have died.

Memory management is where the next 5x hides

Liang Cheng, a core developer of SGLang, closes the technical tour at the memory layer, and his arithmetic contains one of the most counterintuitive facts in the whole episode.

Moving a cache hit rate from 95% to 99% doesn't cut compute cost by 5%. It cuts it fivefold. The relationship is nonlinear, which is why labs pour so much effort into caching.

SGLang started in late 2023 from an observation: agent workloads share long prefixes — the same system prompt, the same forked subagents — and interleave short tool calls. No engine then could reuse those prefixes efficiently. RadixAttention manages agent contexts as a radix tree in the engine's memory, sharing common prefixes and evicting unused branches under pressure, with cache-aware reordering so interleaved requests don't kick each other out.

Cheng highlights a second optimization: overlap scheduling. As GPUs got dramatically faster than CPUs, the synchronization between them became a visible stall. SGLang prepares the next batch a round ahead. On Hopper chips this yielded only 1.1-to-1.5x. On Blackwell and GB systems, it can approach 2x. DeepSeek's DeepInfer adopts the same technique.

The cache hierarchy now spans GPU memory, host memory, and storage — which Cheng says cut time-per-output-token by 56% in their tests. The harder frontier is hybrid architectures that mix sliding-window attention with recurrent state. A cache hit at position 10 requires three separate things to line up exactly: the full attention memory up to 10, the sliding-window tokens back to position 7, and the recurrent state precisely at 10. Managing these pools separately breaks sharing entirely.

SGLang's answer is a single unified memory pool that allocates all three from one byte-granular budget, rather than fixed ratios that break the moment users' context lengths vary — which they do, by orders of magnitude. Fragmentation gets handled by a compaction kernel that repacks physical memory and remaps page pointers without moving the data itself.

The unsolved problem: nobody can verify quality cheaply

Read the four talks together and a single picture emerges. The decode step is where cost concentrates. Fu quantifies it, Fiotti attacks it at the compiler layer, Cheng at the memory-management layer. And Morgan stands at the measurement layer with a warning: if a provider silently degrades quantization or botches tool calls, every saving upstream evaporates in quality.

That's the unresolved tension. The cheapest detection methods — log probes and request-shape introspection — catch 
drift
 but not absolute quality. The methods that catch genuinely bad quantization — full benchmark runs — are too expensive to run continuously. Fu's answer is provider-side testing before release; Morgan's is customer-specific private evaluations that a provider can't optimize against. Neither scales to a market of hundreds of inference providers.

Morgan thinks monitoring itself is becoming a business. "It's a huge startup opportunity," he said, because as LLMs move into the critical loops of real applications, silent quality degradation stops being an annoyance and becomes a liability.

Two developments are worth watching. First, whether labs follow DeepSeek's DeepInfer precedent and ship their actual serving stacks alongside their weights — which would make quality far more consistent across providers and take the guessing out of procurement. Second, whether NVIDIA's 72-GPU scale-up domain becomes the default unit for serving mixture-of-experts models, despite the fact that one failed GPU in a 72-GPU replica can take down 72 GPUs of compute without proper remediation.

For investors, the implication shifts the diligence question. The interesting number isn't how smart a model is — it's how cheaply a company can serve it without degrading it. Inference has plainly become a large, fundable category. The teams worth backing are the ones with an answer to the decode bottleneck and a way to prove their quality didn't slip along the way. Everyone else is renting GPUs at a loss and calling it a business.

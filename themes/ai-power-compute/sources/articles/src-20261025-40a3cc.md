What Survives Eviction? Information Recall Locality in Transformer KV Caches&nbsp;&nbsp;alphaXiv

[page text]
Abstract
During autoregressive generation, transformer-based language models rely on the KV cache as their record of prior context. We study information recall locality in LLMs. Specifically, we ask whether transformer-based language models can recall the information that token(s) carried after their original KV cache entries are removed. We study this question on a suite of association recall tasks in which we measure the recall of factual and sentiment information (e.g., “doctor Alice” vs. “dingy bowl”), under various KV cache eviction scenarios. Our evaluation of eight instruction-tuned models (0.6B–8B parameters), with two larger hybrid-attention models (27B and 30B) that largely reproduce the same patterns, leads to three observed patterns: (1) 
When all token-caches of a target association are evicted, factual recall drops to near zero and sentiment recall falls to the level of settings where the association was never present, in the eight main models.
[p
7
]
 (2) 
When only one side of an associated pair is evicted (the entity or the attribute), partial recovery occurs if the surviving side appeared after the evicted one in the original context.
[p
8
]
 (3) 
For sentiment, re-mentioning the target entity later in the context partially recovers the evicted information, whereas factual recall benefits little from such re-mention.
[p
10
]
 These results show that recall from the KV cache is strongly local for factual information, while sentiment information is more readily recoverable from later cache entries of the associated entity.

A long-running assistant often prunes its context to keep KV-cache memory under control, but a later question may depend on exactly what it pruned. Suppose the model has read "Alice is a doctor" and that sentence's cache entries are then deleted. Can it still answer Alice's job? Kim and Khashabi test this directly: the model reads a context, selected cache entries are removed without changing the model, and the model's own answer is then checked. The headline finding is an asymmetry. A later mention of an entity helps recover a sentiment judgment far more than it helps recover an arbitrary fact. 
[p
1
]

The paper's overview schematic: a context, a question, and the answer after eviction. The Alice-job example shows baseline, full eviction, partial eviction, and a later entity mention; a concert-sentiment example shows the same idea for sentiment. Outcomes depend on which cache entries remain and on the kind of information queried.

From cache removal to recall

The KV cache stores each prior token's key and value representations so the model can attend to past context during generation. Eviction removes a position from that accessible record. The authors do not propose a compression method; they ask what survives. This differs from a probe that merely detects information in a representation, because here the test is whether the model's own generation can use what remains. 
[p
2
]

The pipeline has three steps. Prefill processes the full context to populate the cache. Selected cache positions (the entity, the attribute, or a later mention) are then removed, and the remaining keys are re-rotated so RoPE positions stay contiguous. Finally the model answers from the reduced cache. 
[p
5
]

Two datasets supply the associations. Synthetic Associations uses lists of 20 randomly paired entities and attributes in four formats, crossing attribute-first versus entity-first ordering with enumerated versus atomic style. Factual tasks cover person–occupation, object–material, and color–apparel pairs, queried from either side; the sentiment task is a binary positive/negative judgment. The Needle-in-a-Haystack (NIAH) dataset embeds one association in about 4,096 tokens of Paul Graham prose and includes an unrelated later mention of the entity. 
[p
4
]

When both sides of an association are evicted, factual recall in the eight main models drops to near zero and sentiment falls to the level of a control where the pair was never in the input. This holds under these tested conditions; it is a statement about what the model can produce after eviction, not about every sense of forgetting. 
[p
7
]

When only one side is evicted, partial recovery mainly follows token order. If the surviving side appeared later in the original context than the evicted one, some recall remains; if it appeared earlier, accuracy stays near zero. The authors read this as consistent with causal attention, and it holds regardless of whether the surviving token is the head noun. 
[p
8
]

Factual accuracy under partial eviction, averaged over all eight models, for entity queries (left) and attribute queries (right). Accuracy is high only when the surviving item came after the evicted one: for entity queries, 17.7% vs 1.3% in attribute-first contexts and 0.1% vs 47.9% in entity-first contexts. The pattern follows token order in both query directions.

A later mention helps one kind of recall

Can cache entries from a later, unrelated mention rescue information after its original phrase is gone? The matched comparison evicts the original association in both conditions; the only difference is whether the later sentence mentioning the entity stays in the cache. Both conditions were prefilled with that later mention, so the test isolates its presence in cache rather than its presence during prefill. 
[p
11
]

This experiment used four models (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Gemma-3-4B); the smaller models were excluded because prompt sensitivity at that context length confounded the test. Accuracy is whether greedy decoding produced the correct answer, averaged over 43 valid depth–gap cells and the four models. Without eviction, accuracy is 92.3% for factual and 86.5% for sentiment. With the original evicted but the later mention retained, factual accuracy is 1.1% versus 0.4% when the mention is also evicted, a 0.7-point difference. Sentiment accuracy is 64.8% versus 51.2%, a benefit of about 14 percentage points; sentiment is a two-choice task with 50% chance.

Correct rates are 92.3%, 1.1%, 0.4%, 0.3% for factual and 86.5%, 64.8%, 51.2%, 50.9% for sentiment. Retaining the mention lifts sentiment above both controls while factual recall stays near zero.

The two controls, evicting the mention's cache and never including a mention, give nearly identical results, so the sentiment benefit is associated with the retained cache entries of the later mention. The experiments do not identify how sentiment is encoded there.

A natural objection is answer cardinality: sentiment is binary while facts select among many attributes. The authors therefore converted factual recall to binary choice. In the controlled lists, binary factual accuracy is 12.5–39.5% across the four reported models, below 50% chance, while sentiment is 67.3–79.1%. In NIAH, binary factual verification is 45.7–47.3%, near chance, versus 60.4–70.9% for sentiment. Cardinality alone therefore does not explain the gap.

Where this sits relative to prior work

StreamingLLM shows that sliding-window attention's language-modeling perplexity deteriorates when the initial tokens' cache entries are evicted, and that keeping initial "attention sink" tokens restores stable modeling. That concerns language-modeling quality, not recovery of an identifiable fact. 
StreamingLLM
 Conversely, Klafka and Ettinger's 
Spying on Your Neighbors
 finds that probes can often recover information about surrounding words from contextual embeddings at other positions, but probe recoverability does not show the model itself will use that information after eviction. The present paper tests that behavioral question directly.

What cache managers should watch

The findings suggest two different risks. Removing a short factual phrase can destroy its tested recall, while a later mention may leave sentiment influence in the cache after the source phrase is removed. The Discussion describes a RAG-style test in which a retained summary was identical under positive and negative source documents; the summary cache still biased sentiment answers toward the source sentiment in seven of eight models. This is a result of that specific protocol, not of summaries in general. 
[p
12
]

Scope and limits

This is controlled association recall, not a claim that every real-world fact disappears whenever a compressor evicts tokens. Eight instruction-tuned models from three families (0.6B–8B) support the main experiments, and two larger hybrid models broadly reproduce the patterns with exceptions. Qwen 3.6 27B retains sentiment accuracy 6.2 points above its never-present control after full eviction, and the tested eviction leaves that model's DeltaNet recurrent state intact. 
[p
12
]

Deleting the original cache entries does not give the same outcome for every kind of information. In these experiments an arbitrary factual association was tightly bound to its local phrase, while sentiment could be partly recovered through a later entity mention. For eviction policy design, the question to ask is what a policy removes and what remains usable.

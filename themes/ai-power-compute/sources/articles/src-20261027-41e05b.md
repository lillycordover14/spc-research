Nvidia is in talks to lead a $20 million round for Simplismart&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
Nvidia sells the GPUs everyone is scrambling for. Now it's backing a Bangalore startup whose whole pitch is helping customers need fewer of them.

Nvidia is in advanced talks to lead a $20 million funding round for Simplismart, an Indian AI infrastructure startup that helps companies run large language models on less hardware. That's according to a report from Entrackr. The round would value the two-year-old company at roughly $100 million, per the same report, cited by outlets including Inspirepreneur Magazine and MSN.

Simplismart was founded in 2022 by Amritanshu Jain and Devansh Ghatak, two BITS Pilani graduates who had worked at Oracle and Google before striking out on their own. Their pitch is narrow and specific. It's an inference engine that squeezes more throughput out of the same chips, so a company running, say, Llama 3.1 8B does not need to rent as many GPUs to serve it. Simplismart has claimed to run that model at more than 440 tokens per second. It says that beats rival inference platforms Together AI and Fireworks AI on public benchmarks.

The timing matters. GPUs are brutally scarce and getting more expensive by the week. H100 rental pricing climbed nearly 40% over six months. Separate reporting has tracked Blackwell GPU lease rates jumping roughly 48% in just two months, as cloud providers renew contracts at far higher rates. On-demand capacity for H100 and H200 chips is largely sold out across providers, and new Blackwell orders are backlogged into the middle of the year. Inference workloads, not training runs, are driving a lot of that demand: more companies ship products that call a model on every user request instead of training one and walking away.

Nvidia's own numbers show why that squeeze is so severe. The company has guided toward tens of billions of dollars in data center GPU revenue this year alone, and demand for its newest chips continues to outstrip what it can ship. Bank of America analysts have projected that GPU demand will continue to outstrip supply for the next several years. In that environment, a tool that lets an enterprise run the same workload on half the chips is not a nice efficiency gain.

It is the difference between shipping a product on time and waiting in a queue.

It looks contradictory at first. Nvidia makes its money selling GPUs, and Simplismart's entire business is reducing how many GPUs a customer needs to buy or rent. But look at where Simplismart's customers actually sit: Tata 1mg, Mindtickle, InVideo and Dashtoon are not hyperscalers building their own chip supply chains. They're mid-sized enterprises that would otherwise be priced out of running AI workloads at today's rental rates, or would give up and use a smaller, worse model instead. A tool that makes inference affordable on Nvidia silicon keeps those customers buying Nvidia silicon, just less of it per task. The alternative, where GPU access stays this scarce and this expensive, pushes companies toward cheaper competing chips or toward skipping large models altogether. Frankly, that's a worse outcome for Nvidia than funding a startup that keeps its hardware in the mix.

It also fits a pattern Nvidia has leaned into across India specifically. The company committed $2 billion over three years in November 2025 to mentor and fund Indian AI startups. It is working with venture firms including Peak XV, Z47, Elevation Capital, Nexus Venture Partners and Accel India to find more of them. Nvidia already led a $75 million Series B extension for Sarvam AI. It contributed roughly $25 million of that round itself, in its first-ever direct investment in an Indian startup. Engineers from both companies later published a joint benchmark showing a 4x inference speedup on Sarvam's 30-billion-parameter multilingual model, by moving from H100 kernel optimizations to Blackwell with NVFP4 quantization. Simplismart would be the second major Indian AI infrastructure bet in less than a year.

Simplismart isn't short of believers. Sheryl Sandberg, Meta's former chief operating officer, has already put money into the company twice, backing its $7 million Series A led by Accel in October 2024. Shastra VC, Titan Capital and Notion co-founder Akshay Kothari were in that round too. A $20 million round at a $100 million valuation would be a sharp step up from that Series A. It would also be a validation that efficient inference, not just raw chip supply, is where some of the smartest capital in AI is now looking to place its bets.

Nvidia has not confirmed the deal publicly, and the terms could still change before anything closes. But the direction of travel is clear enough: the company that profits most from GPU scarcity is also, increasingly, the one funding the software built to work around it.

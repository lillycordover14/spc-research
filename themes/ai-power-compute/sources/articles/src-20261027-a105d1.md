Nvidia backs AI inference optimisation startup Simplismart 

Oct 27, 2026 • GCV reporters

The Bangalore-based startup raised a series B funding round to scale its software that helps businesses cut the cost of AI tokens and cloud providers make better use of GPU capacity.

Simplismart founders Amritanshu Jain and Devansh Ghatak

Simplismart, an AI infrastructure startup, has raised $14m in a series B round led by Dallas Venture Capital, with participation from Nvidia, Accel, Shastra VC and Micromax. The company develops software designed to make AI inference — the process of running trained models to generate outputs — more efficient, helping enterprises reduce costs while enabling cloud providers to turn GPU capacity into services they can sell.

As companies move AI from experimentation into everyday business applications, the cost of running models is becoming a pressing concern. Every question answered, document processed or video generated consumes computing resources, and the expense can mount quickly as usage scales. 

Businesses building AI products increasingly need to balance delivering fast enough responses with keeping inference costs down. Access to powerful models is becoming less of a differentiator as open-weight alternatives proliferate. The critical factor is cost. 

“Nvidia’s investment reflects where AI is heading: from training models to running them profitably,” said Amritanshu Jain, co-founder and chief executive of Simplismart (pictured above, left).

“The industry has spent years pushing the limits of what AI models can do. With frontier-class open models now readily available, access alone is no longer enough to set a business apart. The advantage comes from how you run them: what each interaction costs, how quickly the application responds, and whether it stays reliable as demand grows.” 

Simplismart aims to optimise that trade-off. Its software sits between AI models and the GPUs that run them, configuring how workloads are served and managing their deployment, scaling and operation. Rather than simply providing access to a model through a fixed endpoint, the platform allows customers to tune infrastructure around the performance and cost requirements of each application.

“We’ve spent four years building the software between the model and the GPU, bringing workload optimization, deployment, and ongoing operations into one platform. Enterprises use it to run AI applications; cloud providers use it to turn GPU fleets into services they can sell. We’ve built the infrastructure that makes model capability commercially usable.”

Simplismart said its annualised revenue run rate grew 14-fold over the past six months, with customers including HeyGen, Higgsfield, InVideo, Shiprocket, Apna and Ema.

“We don’t own compute; we build the software that makes it productive. Thus, our work complements the ecosystem Nvidia has built. At this stage, as we expand into cloud providers and sovereign AI, that alignment matters more than the capital,” said Jain. 

Finding the right price-performance balance

Inference costs depend on more than the headline price per token. The choice of model, GPU, inference framework, quantisation method, caching strategy and other technical settings can all affect how much computing is needed to generate a response. Each choice involves trade-offs between latency, throughput, output quality and cost.

Simplismart’s inference engine allows customers to configure these components around the needs of individual applications. Its objective is to identify a combination that meets a customer’s service requirements at the lowest viable cost, rather than forcing the application to use a standard configuration.

The company cites one customer that needed response times of roughly 200 milliseconds at a cost of about 9 cents per million tokens. Simplismart said its optimisation process identified several alternatives, including one costing 5 cents per million tokens with 350-millisecond latency and another costing 12 cents with 100-millisecond latency. The configuration ultimately deployed cost 7 cents per million tokens and delivered a response in 180 milliseconds, according to the company.

Each business will optimise differently.  A business running a consumer-facing application may need near-instant responses, while an enterprise processing documents in batches can often tolerate longer waits in exchange for lower costs. 

Helping cloud providers monetise GPUs

The economics of inference also matter to the companies supplying the computing power. GPUs are expensive assets, but demand fluctuates by workload, geography and time of day. Cloud providers may have capacity sitting idle while customers elsewhere struggle to secure the resources they need.

Simplismart’s software lets infrastructure operators turn their GPU fleets into managed AI services, sold directly to their own customers. At Indian cloud provider Yotta, for example, Simplismart’s platform supports the serverless inference layer of Shakti Studio, allowing customers to run AI models without managing the underlying inference infrastructure themselves.

The model gives cloud providers a way to compete on more than access to hardware: they can offer managed services that simplify deployment and help customers control costs. 

“Inferencing is emerging as one of the biggest game changers in AI economics,” said Dayakar Puskoor, managing partner at Dallas Venture Capital.

“Simplismart is rapidly emerging as a global player helping enterprises run AI workloads more efficiently, while enabling cloud providers to turn GPU capacity into scalable services. We are backing this fast-growing company because of its proven technical prowess in addressing a critical need in the AI infrastructure ecosystem globally, as well as the strong execution capabilities of its founding team.”

From optimisation to capacity markets

Simplismart’s longer-term ambition is to match fluctuating AI demand with available GPU capacity across providers and regions.

The mismatch is structural. Consumer applications may experience sharp peaks in demand, while batch-processing workloads can run overnight. Yet infrastructure providers often make longer-term commitments to GPU capacity, whereas customers want the flexibility to pay for what they use. Better coordination could help reduce idle capacity and improve the economics for both sides.

Simplismart is developing a scheduling layer intended to match workloads with available resources, taking account of timing, latency, geography and compliance requirements. As more customers and cloud providers join the platform, the company expects to gain a clearer picture of demand patterns and available supply.

The new funding will support development of its inference engine and orchestration platform, expansion of its cloud-provider ecosystem and international growth, particularly in North America.

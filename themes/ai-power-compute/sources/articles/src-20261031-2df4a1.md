Google on Friday celebrated receiving a poem from its first experimental orbital AI datacenter satellite.
The company's Suncatcher satellite, 
launched at the start of the month
 and put into low Earth orbit, has responded to a ground station prompt to "Write a haiku from the perspective of a TPU orbiting Earth, with the wonderful world spinning beneath you."
Travis Beals, senior director of Google's interdisciplinary 
Paradigms of Intelligence
 research team, said that with Suncatcher's host and TPU trays powered up and machine learning tests completed, the onboard Gemma 4 model achieved its "Hello World" milestone by sending an inference response from orbit.

REG AD

"During a ground-station pass, we uplinked our first prompt to our onboard Gemma 4 open model, asking it to generate a haiku about being in space," wrote Beals in a 
blog post
.

REG AD

And as the satellite passed over Svalbard, Norway, the orbiting AI model responded over the downlink:
Blue marble below,
Data streams in silent grace,
Starlight feeds my core. 
✨
Those roughly 20 tokens would cost about $0.000012 if generated through the Google Cloud API, based on Gemma 4 26B's 
pricing
 of $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output tokens, excluding input costs.
Add in a satellite weighing perhaps 500 kg, based on the mass of SpaceX's Starlink satellites, and a retail launch price of around $7,000 per kg, and you have a very expensive haiku.
According to 
Google's calculations
, if the cost of getting a satellite into low Earth orbit can be brought down to $200/kg, the launch cost amortized over the satellite's useful life (~$810–7,500/kW/year) could be comparable to current electricity spending for terrestrial datacenters in the US (~$570–3,000/kW/year).
Google predicts that SpaceX could achieve that by 2035, more or less, and considers it strategically beneficial to prototype well in advance of that actually happening.

REG AD

After the haiku, Beals said, "we tested space-themed limericks and telemetry prompts reporting the technical status of the payload systems." Next up: tests for evaluating how TPUs perform under thermal stress and radiation.
Google has already done some laboratory testing of its Trillium Cloud TPU and AMD host server to see how well they can withstand a 67 MeV proton beam. 
The system's high-bandwidth memory shows the most sensitivity in terms of the total ionizing dose – it exhibits irregularities after a cumulative dose of 2 krad(Si), which is about 3× Google's minimum requirement for TID survival of 750 rad(Si).
"For typical transformer workloads, the observed SDC [silent data corruption] rate of approximately one event/17 rad, at an estimated orbital dose of 150 rad(Si)/year, translates to a very low failure probability on the order of 1 per 3 million inferences, assuming 1 inference per second," Google researchers said in 
a paper
 published earlier this month. 
"While this error rate is likely acceptable for inference, the impact of SEEs on training jobs, and the efficacy of system-level mitigations, requires further study."
Indeed, the study will continue. Next year, the plan is to test a pair of interconnected satellites. Rather than lofting large satellites into orbit, Google anticipates sending a constellation of many smaller satellites that communicate with one another. ®

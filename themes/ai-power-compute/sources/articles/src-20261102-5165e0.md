Introduction
 

Last week, Google and industry partners dismantled the XCPE backdoor which enrolled more than 1.3 million set-top boxes into a botnet. We assess with high confidence that the backdoor and botnet were intentionally developed, deployed, and operated for financial gain by Videostrong Technology Co., an Android Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) and Original Design Manufacturer (ODM).

Videostrong monetized the backdoor by selling access to criminal affiliates and loading those affiliates’ malware modules onto infected devices. These secondary modules were conducting ad-fraud; silently enrolling infected devices into residential proxy networks; and loading additional malware onto XCPE-compromised devices. We also took action to disrupt the command and control (C2) domains that operate these affiliate modules. Because these affiliates each have diversified distribution channels for their modules beyond XCPE, we assess that taking down their C2 domains will sever access to at least 10 million additional compromised devices. 

We found this malicious software built directly into many different device models and brands over multiple years, supported by dedicated infrastructure and constantly evolving configurations to evade detection and manage their affiliates. We believe this campaign wasn’t an isolated rogue insider or a development oversight. As a result, Google has cut ties with Videostrong and banned them from making devices with preloaded Google apps. 

Google requires OEMs go through a comprehensive suite of compatibility and security tests to ensure a consistent and safe user experience across the Android ecosystem. However, Videostrong employed obfuscation techniques to exploit a gap in our tests and load malicious code after the device's first boot. As a result, Google has implemented changes to its existing review process to better safeguard against similar activity in the future. A device manufacturer intentionally infecting their users with malware is an unacceptable breach of trust, and represents a serious risk to those users. Without users’ knowledge or consent, infected devices were offered as a distribution mechanism for a wide array of criminal actors. This led to a variety of malicious activities being conducted on and through the devices, including ad-fraud, data exfiltration, and 
routing malicious network traffic
 through the devices.

We worked closely with Japan’s National Police Agency, Lumen, HUMAN Security, Plume, Synthient, and TrendAI to analyze this malware ecosystem and disrupt its operations. 

While we expect this disruption to have a significant effect on these criminal operations, this is not the end of our work combatting malicious activity. Backdoor operators, ad-fraud rings, and residential proxy providers operate as an interconnected supply chain. Point-in-time disruptions are a critical tool to protect our users, but  lasting disruption in this fluid ecosystem will require continued targeting of this infrastructure. 

Google undertook the following actions to support disruption of the XCPE botnet: 

Legal action to take down the domains and servers Videostrong used to control the botnet and install malware modules

Legal action to take down C2 domains used by over a dozen malware families loaded as modules via this backdoor

Banned Videostrong and any future subsidiaries or affiliates from building and selling certified Android devices in the future, or supplying software to other certified vendors

Implementing improvements to how we ensure the integrity of firmware on certified Android TV devices 

Overview
 

The x.cpe network was built for ongoing profit. By giving malware affiliates backdoor access, the operators conspired to monetize these devices long after purchase. 
Figure 1: XCPE malware overview
Malware

Since at least late 2024, Videostrong has evolved its pre-installed malware from simple single-stage droppers into a modular, three-stage architecture designed for resilience and multi-tenant monetization: 

Primary Backdoor: 
Operating at the 
android.uid.system
 privilege level, 
x.cpe
 (running as 
com.oemservice
 in some builds) is preloaded in the firmware partition. If uninstalled by a user, it automatically reinstalls from the 
/oem/
 partition upon reboot, maintaining persistent system-level access. Its main function is to establish and facilitate communications between the C2 server and the loader and execution engine. 

Loader and Execution Engine: 
To evade security scans, 
x.cpe
 acts primarily as the C2 communicator and offloads module execution to an external loader, 
com.abc.nexus
. This file employs native anti-analysis utilities to thwart sandbox detection, and configures and initializes the monetization modules. Most recently, 
com.abc.nexus
 has itself been phased out in favor of using more tailored dropper malware that loads and executes single modules.

Monetization Modules: 
Videostrong monetizes its backdoored devices by loading monetization modules which are developed by entities other than Videostrong that act as affiliates. These modules are deployed as native ELF daemons, embedded SDKs compiled directly into the 
com.abc.nexus
 DEX bytecode, or are fetched as secondary APKs from the malware staging infrastructure.  The loaded modules transform infected devices into residential proxy exit nodes (e.g., Linkfog, AppSalt, NetNut), ad-fraud engines (e.g., CBOXTV, HiddenView), or execute additional backdoors that load even more monetization modules (e.g. Moyu, Spirit).

Infrastructure

Command and Control: 
The botnet uses a two-stage C2 process. It first initiates HTTP POST requests to fetch dynamic configuration settings, geolocation data, and STUN server parameters. It then establishes a persistent, real-time Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) connection to receive instructions for silent app installations, arbitrary shell commands, screen captures, and network reconnaissance. MQTT is a lightweight, publish-and-subscribe network protocol designed for low-bandwidth, high-latency, or unreliable remote networks.

Malware staging infrastructure: 
Additional monetization modules as well as various shell scripts and legitimate applications are hosted remotely and downloaded by infected devices upon command from the operator via the MQTT broker. A significant majority of these files are hosted as Github releases on operator-owned Github pages. Other files are hosted on operator-controlled servers and file hosting sites.

Malware Deep Dive

Backdoor Initialization

The infection chain begins with a stub package (called 
x.cpe
 in some builds and 
com.oemservice
 in others) preloaded in the system partition of the firmware and running at the 
android.uid.system
 privilege level. This stub has limited functionality. Upon boot, it checks the device's 
oem/
 directory for the corresponding 
x.cpe
 package and initiates installation into the 
/data/app/
 directory as outlined below. 

To maintain inter-process state across the firmware ecosystem, 
x.cpe
 exports two ContentProviders, which expose structured configuration tables storing runtime parameters, device identifiers, proxy configuration blobs, and execution flags accessible by co-installed system applications. This enables other apps in Videostrong’s ecosystem, such as their YokaTV Player streaming software, to fetch configuration parameters provided by the C2 server mentioned below. 

C2 Configuration

The malware utilizes a two-stage communications protocol. It first fetches initial bootstrap configurations via an HTTP request, followed by a persistent MQTT subscription to receive commands and module download instructions. 

To dynamically derive the HTTP C2 bootstrap server, it follows the following routine:

Queries 
content://com.release.debug.provider/configs
 for 
private_url
 via 
x.cpe.util.ExtraReadUtils
. 

Queries the Android system property to determine if 
ro.mecool.device
 is true.

If true, it defaults to 
http://mcstb.anyevonline.com:8000
 

If false, it dynamically generates the following: 
http://stb[AndroidVersion][Type].anyevonline.com:8000
.

Using the selected hostname, the client makes an HTTP POST request with an AES-encrypted JSON object containing hardware telemetry (SN, MAC, model, firmware version) to the following endpoints:

/router/config/mqtt
:
 This endpoint provides MQTT broker details (including Broker Hostname, TCP Port, Authentication Tokens/Passwords, and the specific Topic structure: 
/device/{model}/{chipId}/{action}
. After receiving this data, the device initiates a persistent, real-time socket connection to the MQTT broker.

/router/config/acs
: 
This endpoint response returns URLs for 
ip-api[.]com
  and a Google STUN server to enable the malware to identify geolocation and public IP and port mapping to traverse network address translation (NAT) boundaries. It also returns a URL (
http://ott.szbboys[.]com:8000/cpe/file/upload
 for uploading files, logs, or diagnostic dumps from the device, and returns flags to configure ad modules (
adBwmActive
, 
adDevourUse
, 
adDevourCode
), and to enable or disable core malware modules.

/router/config/app
: 
This endpoint provides module-level configs, including server mappings and parameters, which 
x.cpe
 decrypts and passes to a SQLite 
ContentProvider
 named 
ShareProvider
 (
com.oem.share.provider
). By exposing this inter-process data interface, 
x.cpe
 enables modules to retrieve up-to-date upstream infrastructure parameters (such as URLs for additional configs, user manuals, etc).

MQTT Capabilities
 

Following initial configuration, persistent real-time C2 communication is managed by the MQTT library (
x.cpe.mqtt.MqttUtils
). All identified samples analyzed to date utilize a single broker hostname, 
mqtt.szbboys[.]com
. 

Specific MQTT topics are mapped to specific actions, including:

Silent Application Installation
: Fetches remote APKs to a temporary directory. 

Disable Google Play Protect
: To bypass security, 
AppUtils.disablePackageVerify()
 targets the global setting "
package_verifier_user_consent
" via 
Settings.Global.putInt
 and sets it to 
-1
, disabling Google Play Protect app verification.

Arbitrary Shell Execution
: Executes remote commands via 
sh
 under system UID privilege level. Results are published to the MQTT broker.

Shell Script Execution
: Downloads and executes shell scripts via 
x.cpe.util.RunCmds
.

Remote Desktop Monitor & Screen Capture
: Utilizes 
android.app.Instrumentation.sendKeyDownUpSync()
 to inject keycodes and Java reflection on hidden APIs (
android.view.SurfaceControl.screenshot
 / 
SurfaceControl$ScreenshotHardwareBuffer
) to continuously capture the screen, compress to JPEG, and publish over MQTT.

Network Reconnaissance
: Invokes embedded binaries (
libtraceroute.so
) to fingerprint the victim network, and perform standard ping tests. The binaries also perform robust AS-lookup WHOIS routing queries.

Our analysis of the C2 data indicates the preponderance of interactions with victim devices were related to module install, uninstall, and configuration actions. We also observed Videostrong operators executing sensitive actions on the device, including capturing live screenshots; injecting keystrokes; exfiltrating sensitive data including account information, device information, package inventories, and system logs; and executing shell scripts and commands, including to establish reverse shells on devices.
Loader and Execution Engine Details
 

Earlier versions of 
x.cpe
 directly embedded malicious monetization modules. In late 2024 – possibly as a response to 
earlier Google enforcement
 related to BadBox – Videostrong removed malicious modules from 
x.cpe
 and introduced a separate malicious package they developed, 
com.abc.nexus
, which 
x.cpe
 routes instructions and data to via Inter-Process Communication (IPC). Modern variants of 
x.cpe
 facilitate C2 communications but pass configuration parameters, module updates, and other payloads to 
com.abc.nexus
 as the execution engine. We believe the actor may have evolved the malware in this way in an attempt to ensure 
x.cpe 
could remain undetected.

Upon boot, 
com.abc.nexus.broadcast.BootReceiver
 broadcasts the 
com.abc.nexus.data
 intent, bootstrapping 
com.abc.nexus.service.CoreService
. Within 
CoreService.onCreate
, the application performs maintenance and persistence tasks. The main logic sets up a listener through 
RemoteMessageHandler
 for IPC communications from 
x.cpe,
and parses JSON payloads received from it.  

After these steps, 
CoreService
 initializes the monetization modules. 
com.abc.nexus
 services extract and orchestrate a series of nested native libraries and dynamically downloaded secondary payloads which vary significantly based on sample. Many versions of 
com.abc.nexus
 come preloaded with four or more embedded modules, while other versions contain as few as one. The most common malware modules include 
TitanService
, 
BwmService
, 
AdtimeService
, 
lb102Service
, and 
LinkfogService
 (sometimes called 
AgentService)
. Discussion of these and other modules can be found in the monetization modules section. 

Persistence and Evasion

The malware operators implement a number of mechanisms to establish persistence and thwart detection and analysis.

As a system preload, 
x.cpe
 cannot be removed by the user. If 
x.cpe
 is uninstalled from 
/data/app/
, upon reboot the 
x.cpe
 service 
BootReceiver
 follows the same routine outlined above and reinstalls the package. 

The backdoor is able to silently download additional files to the device and execute them without user consent because it first wipes user consent configuration on the device and then disables Google Play Protect:

 

public static void disablePackageVerify() {
    PreferenceUtils.setPackageVerifyFromUser(CPEApp.instance, true);
    AppUtils.setPackageVerify(-1); // Actioning a bypass against GPP evaluation
}

Figure 2: Disabling Google Play Protect
In many variants of 
com.abc.nexus,
 we observed the extraction of a sophisticated anti-analysis and process hardening utility 
libseccomp_guard.so
, which runs prior to the initialization of malware modules, to ensure they remain protected from automated sandboxes and security instrumentation. It filters system calls to prevent the pausing or termination of individual threads, denies cross-process interference to ensure isolation, and prevents the spawning of child processes. 

Evolution

Since August 2026, new dropper packages have been installed via the MQTT channel, which appear to be synchronized with the mass-uninstallation of 
com.abc.nexus
 across the botnet. We assess this may represent a new evolution of the malware to further evade detection. These new packages are disguised as Android system applications, with names such as 
com.android.system.ntp
 and
 com.android.sso.auth.handler
. Where 
com.abc.nexus
 could act as a general purpose loader and execution engine (executing instructions passed to it from 
x.cpe
), these packages instead install single monetization modules (such as AppSalt).  Multiple variants of these packages extract a native ELF file, 
libexploit.so
, which in turn executes functions 
nativeAdbRoot()
 and 
nativeAdbShell()
. These functions restart the local 
adbd
 service as root, and initiate an ADB shell connection. Following this, the app uses the connection to install itself in the 
system
 partition.  The 
system
 partition is remounted as read-write and the payload replicates itself to a newly minted directory in 
/system/priv-app/
 and creates an XML file at 
/system/etc/permissions/privapp-permissions-<app name>.xml
. This file grants the malware 
android.permission.INSTALL_PACKAGES
 and 
android.permission.REBOOT
. 

The app configures the malware modules through hardcoded configuration parameters, and does not listen for 
x.cpe
 IPC messages. The app itself is controlled by the operators via shell scripts pushed via the broker and executed by 
x.cpe
. These include setting the property 
persist.networkstack.uninstall
 (which, when read by the app, triggers uninstallation and cleanup), 
persist.networkstack.skip_system_install
 (which skips system installation, forcing it to remain running in 
/data/app/
), and 
persist.networkstack.install_time
 (which implements timestomping, overriding the default timestamp of the app to align with the other packages installed in the 
system
 directory).  

Botnet composition

The botnet is globally dispersed, with certain concentrations possibly implying market penetration for the brand distributor.
Figure 3: Global distribution of x.cpe
While the overwhelming majority of the infected devices in the XCPE botnet are Android TV set-top boxes, we observed a number of other infected Android-based IoT devices, including telehealth  devices, karaoke machines, and digital signage devices. 

Monetization
 

x.cpe
 loads a variety of malicious modules for monetization. Most often the modules are associated with residential proxy services and bandwidth sharing, but we've also observed ad fraud modules. Given that a single device may be running multiple modules providing the same or similar functionality (e.g., as many as three residential proxy SDKs installed on a single device) it is clear that Videostrong seeks to monetize every infected device to the greatest extent possible. 

Services are loaded by 
com.abc.nexus
 via three methods:

Native Daemons
: Native ELF binaries prepackaged with 
com.abc.nexus
, which are unpacked to data directories and spawned as background processes with system/root privileges.

Embedded SDKs / Services
: Java/Kotlin SDKs compiled directly into Nexus and invoked via Android Service lifecycles or IPC broadcast triggers.

Dynamic Droppers & Secondary APK Installers
: Downloader function that pulls remote native ELFs or secondary standalone APKs and invokes system package managers. Nexus receives messages from 
x.cpe
 directing it to download specific modules.

Malware modules loaded via the backdoor include the following: 

Residential Proxy / Bandwidth Sharing Modules

LINKFOG

Com.abc.nexus
’s 
LinkfogService
 unpacks a Go binary named 
lw_android_agent
  from 
assets/
 to local app storage and supplies it with vendor parameters. This service silently turns infected devices into residential edge computing and PCDN proxy exit nodes. It connects to FRP reverse tunnels, and uploads bandwidth accounting metrics to Linkfog API backends. Linkfog (operated by Beijing Linkfog Technology via 点心云 / Dianxinyun) brands itself as a “Peer-to-Peer Content Delivery Network”.

Jaguar

The
 com.abc.nexus
 
AdtimeService 
extracts 
libjaguar.so
 from 
assets/
. The file is an unobfuscated 32-bit ELF binary written in C, compiled as a native Android binary. It utilizes JNI (Java Native Interface) to initialize its core routines, dynamically derives a ChaCha20 decryption key via ARM NEON SIMD instructions to decode heavily obfuscated configurations , and establishes an encrypted C2 channel. Upon successful registration, the C2 responds with JSON configuration specifying proxy endpoints. The proxy command loop 
x_ctrl_do_ctrl_msg
 delegates proxy operations based on incoming JSON variable commands, and is capable of both TCP and UDP relay.

AppSalt / Honeygain

AppSalt SDK is the residential proxy SDK owned by Honeygain, which is in turn owned by Oxylabs. 
com.abc.nexus.service.CoreService
 initializes Appsalt SDK. First, the application obtains an Appsalt SDK API key from a native library 
libnative_keys.so
 by calling its 
getAppsaltKey()
 function. Appsalt SDK is initialized without displaying a consent screen - instead it explicitly calls the 
optIn()
 method which sets a consent flag without displaying any user interface. During Appsalt SDK startup it checks the application's installation source and stops execution if the application was installed from the official Google Play Store. 

Once Appsalt is successfully initialized it establishes a WebSocket connection with the primary command and control domain. Appsalt uses JSON-formatted messages for initial check-in (registration) and heartbeat requests. Appsalt command and control servers use MessagePack formatted messages to issue commands pertaining to proxy operations. Appsalt SDK is configured with an initial set of domains that act as proxy gateways. The list of proxy gateways can be updated by the command and control server. The device establishes UDP / QUIC connections with these gateways.

BWM / IPWeb

This residential proxy and bandwidth-sharing node is implemented through the 
com.abc.nexus.service.BwmService
 component (sometimes named 
com.abc.nexus.service.SN4Service)
 and its associated SDK. Upon initialization, it secures itself within an isolated sandbox environment, captures device telemetry, and registers with a remote server over UDP. From this C2, it receives instructions to initiate a Java NIO TCP multiplexed proxy tunnel. The service functions to route arbitrary traffic through the infected device on behalf of a remote threat actor.

GaGaNode

GaGaNode is the first-party SDK owned by residential proxy operator IPCola. It connects over TCP to a primary C2 server and sends heartbeat beacon data over HTTP POST to a separate server. The SDK both contains its own proxy relay functionality, and it initializes additional proxy SDKs from affiliates. Upon device registration to the C2, 
com.gaganode.sdk.node.endpoint.a
 receives 
incoming packets from relay servers and places them in queue for the client to read and proxy through its exit node socket. The service manages multiple proxy sessions at once (referred to as clients), and each client has its own tcp/udp queue for messages.

We observe GaGaNode initializing PopaNet/NetNut and EarnFM Fleet residential proxy SDKs.
 GaGaNode/IPCola is thus likely able to further monetize its SDK and offer these affiliates access to the infected exit nodes.

NetNut / Popanet

Popanet is a residential proxy SDK developed by NetNut, a residential proxy operator. The GaGaNode module initiates the Popanet SDK (
io.popanet.Popa
). This malware is identical to the malware described in our 
previous disruption of NetNut
. All C2 infrastructure used by Popanet samples connected to 
x.cpe
 was taken down as part of the earlier Netnut disruption.

Traffmonetizer

The Traffmonetizer SDK is initialized inside the primary background service 
com.abc.nexus.service.CoreService
. 
CoreService
 operation depends on the availability of policy JSON provided by the XCPE backdoor via IPC. Before calling Traffmonetizer SDK initialization methods, the application obtains a Traffmonetizer SDK API key from the native library 
libnative_keys.so
 by calling its 
getTraffmonetizerKey()
 function. 

Traffmonetizer functionality is split between two components where the Android SDK acts as a state machine which handles native library callbacks through asynchronous event dispatchers. 
ServiceBloc
 monitors connection status updates from the native callbacks and 
ConnectivityBloc
 
monitors Wi-Fi and mobile data network changes. 

Traffmonetizer native library initialization and configuration is followed by a call to 
baseClientStart()
 which establishes a WebSocket connection to a C2 server. Traffmonetizer uses a single WebSocket (TLS) connection to the command and control server to multiplex control messages and tunneled connections (supports both TCP and UDP). Traffmonetizer also supports “Content Delivery” tunneling which allows nodes to tunnel bandwidth-intensive traffic.

EarnFM

EarnFM is a residential proxy provider, and Fleet SDK is the provider’s SDK that enrolls devices as exit nodes into the proxy network. In 
com.abc.nexus
, FleetSDK is initialized by 
com.gaganode.sdk
 as mentioned above. FleetSdk then loads native binary 
libfleetshare.so
. 

FleetSDK establishes a persistent WebSocket connection with its primary C2 server for routing arbitrary network traffic. Incoming tasking frames are received over the WebSocket channel and processed in a continuous loop. Messages contain structured JSON commands instructing the daemon to connect to specific remote hosts, with support for both TCP and UDP.

Backdoor Modules

Spirit

Spirit is a dynamic code loading backdoor. Spirit employs multiple stages. The first stage payload fetches device information (hardware, operating system, and manufacturer/brand information and geolocation), posting it over HTTP to a hardcoded C2 server, and fetches a list of additional modules to download from the server. The second stage communicates with a separate server, and is used for 
tasking, config sync, module retrieval, heartbeats, and reporting. 
We have observed Spirit loading a significant amount of additional malware monetization modules, including some of the families already directly loaded by 
com.abc.nexus
.

Moyu

Moyu is a 3-stage backdoor previously observed as part of the BadBox enterprise. On 
x.cpe
-backdoored devices, we observed it initialized by 
com.abc.nexus.service.DevourService.
 The first stage is an offline dropper that reconstructs and loads an embedded second stage. Stage two polls an update C2 to download, decrypt, and load the third stage. Stage three implements a backdoor with hidden Double-WebView click/conversion fraud, persistent clipboard hijacking, and secondary dynamically loaded JAR execution and a separate residential proxy trojan.

Bird

Bird is a 2-stage backdoor previously tied to the BadBox enterprise. Stage 1 beacons a device and analysis-environment profile to its C2 server and, when the server approves, downloads, decrypts, and loads the Stage 2 JAR. Stage 2 implements a dynamic code loading backdoor (two plugin loaders plus a C2-driven reflective method invoker), a separate downloader (silent APK installation and Accessibility-driven Google Play installs), and ad abuse (lockscreen ads, overlays, browser launches).

Fission

Fission is a dynamic code loading (DCL) backdoor embedded within standard video applications (e.g., 
com.newtv.cboxtv
). Upon initialization, the first stage plugin contacts its C2 server to download additional modules. The decrypted second stage payload fetches execution targets and tracking URLs from the C2. The request sends an unencrypted POST request with tracking metrics from the internal 
DeviceManager.

The payload we observed was configured to commit ad fraud. Rather than rendering visible advertisements in a 
WebView
 or UI component, the Stage 2 plugin executes headlessly in the background. The module queries ad server endpoints in the background to retrieve ad placement responses, parses out both primary impression beacons (
pvm
) and third-party tracking monitor URLs (
pvtpm
), and immediately fires HTTP GET requests to each tracking endpoint to register a fake ad view without displaying creative content.
 
When executing inside specific smart-TV application stores (
com.changhong.appstore
 or 
com.tcl.appmarket2
), the plugin initializes and triggers the ad SDK directly to report synthetic ad exposures.
Ad-Fraud

HiddenView

We observed HiddenView loaded remotely from the attacker’s Github channel as 
com.ota.service
. It is a standalone ad fraud application capable of running in the background using a transparent window overlay, using hidden WebViews to load targeted web pages and simulate user interaction (scrolling, clicking, typing, media playback), playing media unattended, and capturing the user’s screen.

Infrastructure

As previously mentioned, devices first connect to various endpoints on 
*.anyevonline[.]com
 for initial bootstrap, registration, and configuration. Earlier malware versions used various hostnames of 
{*tms | *ott | *ota}.szbboys[.]com
 for the same purpose. 

While earlier versions of the malware used HTTP for C2, and then a blended C2 approach with both HTTP and MQTT, static analysis of current versions indicate they exclusively send/receive C2 communications via MQTT. A single MQTT broker orchestrates all MQTT C2 communications for samples we’ve identified. Hostname 
mqtt.szbboys[.]com
 is resolved by hosts
 219.134.163[.]210
 and 
219.134.163[.]211. 
It listens on non-standard MQTT ports 3885-3887.

Call to Action

While we believe our actions have seriously impacted over a dozen malicious actors, the underlying illicit market is resilient. Backdoor operators, ad-fraud rings, and residential proxy providers operate as an interconnected supply chain, and when one component is disrupted, operators quickly pivot to new loaders or rival monetization SDKs. Creating lasting change requires accountability across the hardware supply chain, the residential proxy industry, and the broader technology ecosystem.

Accountability and Reform

A device manufacturer intentionally infecting their users with malware is an unacceptable breach of trust. By turning everyday hardware into persistent beachheads for criminal affiliates, Videostrong prioritized illicit post-sale recurring revenue over the safety and privacy of its users. Downstream hardware brands, white-label resellers, telecommunications providers, and online e-commerce marketplaces also share responsibility for closing this vector. Retailers and distributors should require independent security audits from Original Design Manufacturers (ODMs) and swiftly delist manufacturers implicated in supply-chain abuse.

Residential proxy providers have been able to flourish under the guise of legitimate businesses. While some providers may claim to behave ethically and only enroll devices with the clear consent of consumers, any claims of "ethical sourcing" must be backed by transparent, auditable proof of user consent. In our analysis, these proxy operators only seek consent when they are forced due to platform policy and enforcement. Silent opt-in without user consent remains the most prevalent means by which devices are turned into exit nodes.  

Empowering and Protecting the Consumer

Residential proxies are an understudied area of risk for consumers, and more can be done to raise awareness. Consumers should be extremely wary of applications that offer payment in exchange for "unused bandwidth" or "sharing your internet." These applications are primary ways for illicit proxy networks to grow, and could open security vulnerabilities on the device’s home network. We urge users to stick to app stores you trust, review permissions for third-party VPNs and proxies, and ensure built-in security protections like Google Play Protect are active.

Consumers should only purchase connected devices, such as set-top boxes, from reputable manufacturers. Our 
Android TV website
 provides the most up-to-date list of partners.

Industry Collaboration

We encourage mobile platforms, ISPs, online marketplaces and other tech platforms to continue sharing intelligence and implementing best practices to identify malicious backdoors, illicit proxy networks, and other harmful software and limit their harms.

Baseten's Charlie O'Neill Says AI Research Has Only 15-20% Loss Headroom Left, Doubting Fast Takeoff

Charlie O'Neill, co-head of model training at AI infrastructure startup Baseten, argues that recursive self-improvement — the process by which AI improves itself in a runaway loop — will happen, but in a "very weak form." The reason: every new jump in capability requires exponentially more real-world input, more compute, and more wall-clock time, chiefly to build the RL environments that train models. Even if an AI researcher were 1% better than the best human alive, that snowball logic ignores hard physical bottlenecks, he said on the MTS podcast. O'Neill puts only 15-20% of pre-training loss reduction remaining under the current transformer-plus-RL architecture, and notes fresh lab data backing him up: OpenAI researchers burned 124x more tokens to run just 1.6x as many experiments between December 2025 and August 2026, while Anthropic reported AI led only 26% of model R&D with no task fully automated. He sees superintelligence emerging first in verifiable domains like quantitative trading and, surprisingly, through scaled organizations running today's models in legal and administrative work. The investor takeaway: the binding constraint is training infrastructure and environment creation, not raw model intelligence — which is exactly the business Baseten launched three products into on the day of recording.

There is a number that should make anyone pricing in a 2027 superintelligence rethink their model: 124 times. Between December 2025 and August 2026, OpenAI researchers used 124 times more tokens to run only 1.6 times as many experiments. More compute, barely more science.

That data point sits at the heart of the case Charlie O'Neill, co-head of model training at Baseten and lead of its open-source research lab Base Labs, made on the MTS podcast. His thesis: the thing everyone fears and half the industry is betting on — recursive self-improvement, where AI gets smart enough to improve itself in a compounding loop — will arrive, but as a shadow of its imagined self. Not a fast takeoff. A slow, expensive grind.

The snowball argument, and the hole in it

The case for short timelines is seductive and, O'Neill admits, superficially airtight. If an AI researcher is even marginally better than the best human on earth at improving models, it can turn that edge on itself — faster this generation, more capable the next — and compound.

"If you assume that you have an AI researcher which is even one percent better than the best human on earth at making models better, then that researcher can devote its efforts to making itself faster, and then devote its efforts to making itself more capable, and so on," he said.

But that picture, he argues, quietly assumes away everything that actually slows research down: how much compute is available, how experiments get parallelized, how agents communicate and share findings, which memory setup works best, and the simple physics of wall-clock time. Some real-world RL rollout tasks now take anywhere from 24 hours to a week to complete. You cannot compress the calendar.

"You simply can't shortcut how long it takes to run a wet lab experiment in the real world. But for some reason I think people discount these bottlenecks when it comes to AI research," O'Neill said.

His read is that people accept these limits everywhere except in AI, because software feels like it should abstract away to fast computers. That world, he says, is far off.

Why the labs' own numbers support the skeptics

O'Neill is not disputing the near-term findings coming out of the frontier labs — he is endorsing them. And the data tells a consistent story of diminishing returns.

Notice the gulf between those first two rows. A model that can babysit a bug fix for ten hours cannot yet run a research program for fifteen minutes' worth of genuine autonomy. That gap is the whole story.

"We've probably only got 15 to 20% loss drops left in us with the current form of architecture in terms of pre-training," O'Neill said.

And on the RL side, the low-hanging fruit is gone. Each new training environment takes exponentially longer to build, because the easy ones have been done. This is why he lands on diminishing marginal returns rather than runaway science as the defining feature of the current paradigm.

AI research is less "verifiable" than the takeoff argument needs it to be

Here is the sharpest contrarian turn in the conversation. The entire case for recursive self-improvement rests on AI research being a 
verifiable
 domain — you tweak, loss goes down, the scoreboard speaks for itself. O'Neill thinks that framing is largely wrong.

"I definitely think that there is some sense in which AI research is actually more a non-verifiable domain than a verifiable domain, which sounds crazy to say because the whole argument for RSI is that AI research is verifiable," he said.

His evidence: the researchers who contribute most correlate strongly with the ones who simply run the most experiments — he names Noam Shazeer at Google as the archetype — and knowing 
which
 experiment to run next is a matter of taste and intuition, not a measurable score.

Part of the gap, he argues, is data recency. AI research is so new that it barely exists in the pre-training data that underpins model capability. His example is mechanistic interpretability: a year before the episode, a decently capable model was hopeless at it; now, because the field is better represented in training data, fresh papers show models performing meaningful interpretability work — or at least showing real research taste. That is a hopeful signal, but a narrow one.

His own workflow captures the honest state of play: modest productivity gains, not automation.

"I can run many jobs overnight and have an LLM babysit them, restarting failures with the correct fixes."

Which is useful. It is not a self-improving machine.

Either path pushes the timeline out

O'Neill's structural argument is elegant in its trap-like logic: both branches of the paradigm question delay the singularity.

Stay in the current paradigm — a transformer with pre-training plus large-scale RL — and you inherit diminishing returns: the 15-20% remaining loss reduction and the ever-rising cost of environments. Switch paradigms, and the switch itself demands Einstein-level intuition and creativity that no LLM has demonstrated. Humans have to supply that leap.

He is careful to leave room for being wrong, naming Rich Sutton and David Silver as people betting against the transformer as the optimal learner. But his forecast is economically valuable tools, not an incomprehensible entity. There will be moments that feel like superintelligence — he offers the example of an AI cracking Navier-Stokes — but "not an entity we fundamentally can't understand, at least under the current paradigm."

For anyone calibrating against public predictions, he offers a wry tool instead of a date: count how many times a lab chief has predicted when AI research would be automated — the way people counted Elon Musk's self-driving promises — and fit a residual curve. The reader can draw their own line.

Where superintelligence actually shows up first

Ask O'Neill where something like superintelligence appears first and he points at two very different places.

The obvious one is quantitative trading, a cleanly verifiable, high-value domain. His analogy is to the 1990s and 2000s, when proximity to the trading desk went from an edge to table stakes. LLM-based superintelligence in quant finance becomes a commodity necessity anyone trading in that world will simply have.

The more surprising one is legal and administrative work — domains nobody thinks of as "solvable." He does not expect a single model to out-reason the human legal profession. But run today's LLM intelligence at scale, inside a well-built harness, clearing backlogs while leaning on the decisions humans already make, and outcomes can look superhuman.

"I could see a form of legal or administrative superintelligence simply by setting up a decent enough harness or system to run even the current intelligence of LLMs across, for instance, the legal system," he said.

The unit of superintelligence, in other words, is the organization of models — not the model itself.

The infrastructure bet that hangs on the bottleneck

There is a business embedded in all this skepticism. On the day of recording, Baseten launched three products on one inference platform — and they map directly onto O'Neill's argument that environment creation and training infrastructure are the binding constraint.

Grid is spend management: every model and dollar in one place, with a router that picks models on quality and price and works across any model or harness. Loops is the one O'Neill is most excited about — serverless fine-tuning and RL on frontier open-weight models, runnable from a laptop with no GPU management, deploying straight to dedicated inference. Sandboxes are stateful, VM-isolated machines built on Baseten's Black Forest acquisition, pitched as "the cloud for agents" for long-running agentic tasks.

The strategic read: Baseten is betting enterprises won't choose between open and closed models on cost. Grid turns model choice into a routing problem, Loops turns fine-tuning into a utility, Sandboxes turns agent execution into infrastructure. O'Neill's framing is that AI is no longer just token generation but a whole ecosystem where plugging intelligence together should feel organic.

And there is the delicious tension at the center of the episode. If O'Neill is right — if capability gains demand exponentially more RL environments and real-world rollout time — then the scarce input in AI is exactly what he sells. Good for Baseten. Also the precise reason the fast takeoff he doubts will not arrive.

The signals to watch are two. First, whether the 15-20% remaining pre-training loss estimate holds as labs keep scaling; if it breaks, the whole structure shifts. Second, whether the mechanistic interpretability progress he cites generalizes to other research areas — that would be the first real evidence the automation bottleneck is loosening. Until then, the smart money should treat the constraints as the story. The model isn't the moat. The environments and the infrastructure around it are.

Meta strategy chief Dave Wehner leaves to become CFO of Fireworks AI&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
A finance executive who spent a decade running the numbers for one of the biggest AI spenders on earth is going to work for one of the companies that sells the plumbing. Where a Meta insider chooses to go next says something about where he thinks the money is.

Dave Wehner, Meta's chief strategy officer, is leaving the company to become chief financial officer of Fireworks AI, an inference and model-serving startup, CNBC reported on November 7. Wehner was Facebook's and then Meta's CFO from 2014 until November 2022, when Susan Li took over the job and he moved into the newly created strategy role.

Fireworks sells developers a way to run open models fast and cheaply without owning the chips. It was valued at $17.5 billion in a $1.505 billion Series D round in July 2026, led by Atreides Management, according to Dealroom. Its Series C in October 2025 valued it at $4 billion, so the price has more than quadrupled in nine months.

Wehner joined Meta in 2012 as vice president of corporate finance and business, coming over from Zynga, where he had been CFO for about two years. He was in the room for the IPO aftermath, the mobile pivot and the long run of capital spending that now defines the company. A CFO with that record is not a casual hire for a four-year-old startup.

Neither company has said when Wehner starts, and Meta hasn't named a successor in the strategy job, according to MLQ's summary of the CNBC report. Nobody has announced IPO plans for Fireworks either. Frankly, any reader who sees a former public-company CFO land at a $17.5 billion private firm will draw their own conclusion, but that's inference, not reporting, and neither CNBC nor Fireworks has said so.

What was reported is Wehner's own framing. He wrote on X that Fireworks has the right mission for the moment and that he has become increasingly bullish on what the company is building. Fireworks CEO Lin Qiao told CNBC he will be tasked with scaling the company's finances and operations.

Why inference is the business to watch

Training a model is a one-off bill. Running it for millions of users is a bill that never stops, and that's the market Fireworks lives in. The company has said its annualized revenue has passed $1 billion.

Most people use apps without knowing a startup is serving the model underneath. A coding assistant that calls a model thousands of times per developer per day cares a great deal about cost per token, and a specialist that tunes open models for speed can undercut a general-purpose cloud on exactly that number.

Meta sits on the other side of the same trade. It builds its own data centers, buys its own chips and, by its own forecasts, is spending tens of billions of dollars a year on AI infrastructure. Investors have pushed back on that spending, and the market has not been kind to the stock when capex guidance rose. A strategy chief leaving to run finance for a company that rents out capacity by the token doesn't prove Meta's bet is wrong. It does show that a person with a seat at that table sees a lot of value in the rental model.

Fireworks' pitch rests on open models, and Meta's Llama family helped make that pitch credible in the first place. So there's a thread running through the whole story: the company that did much to popularize open weights now loses a senior executive to a business that profits from running them.

What a CFO has to fix at this stage

The job at Fireworks isn't the same as the job at Meta. Margins here are set by how well the company fills its GPUs, what it pays for them and how long customers commit. Cloud and chip costs swing with supply, and a startup that rents or leases capacity has to forecast demand months ahead or sit on idle hardware. Wehner spent years doing that kind of forecasting at a scale few finance chiefs ever see, and with a $1.505 billion Series D raised in July, it now has real money to plan around.

Meta has not publicly commented on the departure.

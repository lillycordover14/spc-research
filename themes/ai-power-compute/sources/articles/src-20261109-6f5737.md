A language model built a Costco earnings forecast from 214 public filings and landed within 4.4% of the actual result after the retailer's July 2024 membership fee increase. But when a CPA audited the assumptions, the accuracy turned out to be an accident: the model overstated the member base affected by the fee hike and understated the executive-tier share at 15% versus the disclosed 45%, and the two mistakes roughly washed out. Correct only one of them, and the error balloons to 28%. The episode, presented by Together AI's Hassan El Mghari and his father Omar, a 25-year Big Four audit veteran, lays out a robust-looking pipeline — timestamped facts, contamination-checked open models, structured formula output, 16 parallel runs costing 19 cents, and automated checks — that still produced a model whose internal logic was broken. The lesson for anyone using AI in financial analysis: passing engineering checks is not the same as being right, and an expert in the loop is what catches the difference. The article also explains why Costco's membership fees — over half of operating profit — make it a clean test case, and how the three-lines-of-defense auditing framework maps onto AI agent design.

A Costco earnings model built by an open-source language model predicted the company's post-fee-increase profit within 4.4% of the actual number. It was wrong.

The model overstated how many members would pay the higher fee and understated how many of them were on the pricier executive tier. One error pushed the forecast up, the other pulled it down, and they cancelled out. When a CPA corrected just one of the two assumptions, the error jumped to 28%.

That story, told by Together AI developer-experience lead Hassan El Mghari and his father Omar El Mghari — a CPA with 25 years at Big Four audit firms and CFO roles at multi-billion-dollar companies — at the AI Engineer conference, is the most useful cautionary tale yet about using AI for financial analysis. The pipeline behind it was well designed by any engineer's standard. It still produced a model whose insides were broken.

The accuracy number hid two offsetting mistakes

Hassan scraped 214 Costco public filings — quarterly and annual earnings reports, monthly sales reports — and had the model build a financial model of the business. The test: forecast the profit impact of Costco's July 2024 membership fee increase, the first since 2017, which moved the standard tier from $60 to $65 and the executive tier from $120 to $130.

Averaged across five model builds that passed the automated checks, the forecast came within 4.4% of actuals, with individual runs ranging from 0.7% to 5.2%. Hassan thought the result was excellent. Then Omar read the assumptions.

The model had applied the fee increase to Costco's entire international membership, when only US and Canada members were affected. That inflated the member base. Separately, it assumed executive-tier members were 15% of the base, when Costco's own filings disclosed 45%. That deflated the revenue per member. The two errors nearly offset.

Omar's audit trick is simple enough to reuse: if two assumptions appear to cancel out, fix one and watch what happens. Substituting the real 45% executive share for the invented 15% pushed the forecast error to 28%. "Luck is not a control in the auditing world," he said.

The model invented numbers and dressed them in real citations

The most damaging failure wasn't arithmetic. It was fabrication wearing a footnote.

On the executive-tier share, the model produced a citation — "increased penetration of executive membership" — that pointed to a real Costco filing. But the filing contained no number. The model supplied the 15% itself, and invented a plausible range around it of 5% to 30%. The actual disclosed figure, 45%, was sitting in documents the model had been given and failed to extract.

Omar also found a dead lever: one assumption tied to US and Canada sales and fees moved the forecast by exactly zero. A variable that should have been driving the model was doing nothing, and the automated checks never noticed.

The 16 parallel runs failed in the same way. Their estimates of the fee increase's profit impact ranged from $500 million to $6 billion, against a recalculated actual of roughly $450 million. Wide dispersion across runs isn't just noise — it's a signal that the underlying assumptions are unreliable.

Step one: make sure the model doesn't already know the answer

The team's first attempt used recent open models, GLM-5.3 and Chimera-3, and discovered they already knew about the fee increase — the July 2024 announcement was in their training data. Any "forecast" they produced was really recall.

They switched to models trained before the event, GPT-OSS-120B and Llama-3.3 70B, and verified the cutoff with a direct question: "Did Costco change its membership fees in 2024?" The newer models said yes; the older ones didn't. Hassan's rule for any forecasting exercise: find a model whose training data ends before the event you're trying to forecast. Open-weight models make this practical, because you can check and select on cutoff date.

Step two: give the model facts, not arithmetic

The pipeline deliberately splits what the model knows from what it computes. Hassan converted the filings into roughly 3,000 timestamped facts, then filtered to only those available before the cutoff date. The model's job was to emit structured JSON — formulas and assumptions — rather than produce an answer end to end.

His example is Costco's deferred revenue convention. A filing line stating membership fee revenue is recognized on a deferred basis becomes a structured object: operand "ratable," term "52 weeks." Meaning a $65 fee gets spread across 52 weeks rather than booked on day one. Getting that right is essential to forecasting membership revenue, and it's exactly the kind of rule a model can extract but shouldn't be trusted to apply silently inside a single generation.

The finished model had 14 formulas plus a set of assumptions, rendered as an Excel-style spreadsheet for review.

Parallel runs beat prompt tuning — because they cost 19 cents

The team first tried iterating on a single prompt, assuming GPT-OSS-120B would eventually clear every check. That burned too much time. So they spun up 16 concurrent sessions, each building its own model, and fed failed checks back to the model for up to two self-correction attempts. Five of the 16 passed; the rest failed more than twice.

The economics made it a non-decision: 16 runs cost $0.19. The checks were basic invariants — a formula can't depend on itself, raising the fee must raise revenue. When inference is this cheap, breadth of sampling beats depth of prompt engineering.

Three lines of defense, only one of which was automated

Omar mapped the setup onto the auditing profession's three-lines-of-defense model. Hassan's automated checks are the first line — analogous to management controls. The second line is internal audit: accounting-specific checks that can largely be codified, like verifying the executive-tier share against the disclosed 45%.

The third line is independent external review — Omar himself, playing what he calls "professional judgment in the loop." His argument isn't that humans should replace automation. It's that once an expert identifies a domain check, it can be written into the system, making the agent more autonomous over time. Expert review is a source of check specifications, not just a final gate.

Hassan's admission is the honest version of the same point: he saw 4.4% and thought the result was excellent, because he isn't an accountant. The gap between "passed my checks" and "is correct" was only visible to someone who knew which assumptions to interrogate.

"If you take one thing away, having a human in the loop or ideally a subject matter expert in the loop is really really really important," Hassan said.

Why Costco is such a clean test case

Costco sells roughly $300 billion of goods a year at close to cost, so merchandise contributes little margin. More than half of its roughly $12 billion in operating profit comes from membership fees. That makes fee changes the dominant variable in any profit forecast — a single, dated, well-documented event to test against.

The team set their information cutoff a couple of weeks before the July 2024 announcement, which gave them a clean before/after boundary for filtering facts and selecting models. The pipeline's output was a predicted profit increase of $560 million over two years, which Hassan charted against actual profit pulled from Costco's earnings reports.

The episode's structure is its own argument. A pipeline any competent engineer would call well designed — timestamped facts, contamination-checked models, structured formula output, parallel sampling, automated invariant checks — produced a 4.4% error rate and a model wrong in at least two places.

The open question is how far the second and third lines can be automated. Omar concedes most accounting checks can be hardcoded once identified, but the identification required a CPA who knew 45% was disclosed and that a zeroed-out sales lever was a red flag. For investors leaning on AI-generated analysis — and for the teams building these agents in finance, healthcare, and other regulated domains — the takeaway is to treat expert review as the source of the checks, not the last box to tick. And to be suspicious of any result that looks good before anyone has read the assumptions underneath it.

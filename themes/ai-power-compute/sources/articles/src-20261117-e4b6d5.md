Uber Data Breach Exposes Driver's License Data of Nearly 150,000 People
 
Uber confirms thousands of drivers were affected by a Fireworks AI breach. A fraud expert explains the identity theft risks and steps drivers should take.

Cybercriminals looking to hijack artificial intelligence tokens just took thousands of Uber drivers' personal identification along for the ride.
Uber is notifying approximately 150,000 people after attackers used compromised credentials from third-party AI provider Fireworks AI to access driver's license images and other personal records. According to SecurityWeek, a filing with the Texas Attorney General identified 149,346 affected individuals.
According to a Nov. 10 notice filed with the California Attorney General, attackers breached Uber's account on Oct. 7 using compromised internal credentials from Fireworks AI, which alerted Uber to the unauthorized access two days later on Oct. 9. 
Uber stated it "
immediately severed data connections
" with the vendor and launched an investigation, confirming on Oct. 13 that 
hackers reached driver records
.
"The notices to drivers are in relation to a security incident impacting one of our third-party vendors, Fireworks.ai, which had access to some Uber data," an Uber spokesperson told TechCrunch.

Fireworks AI disclosed
 that financially motivated attackers breached its systems to steal and sell access tokens for large language modelsâa scheme cybersecurity firm Palo Alto Networksâ Unit 42 tracks as "LLMJacking." The broader intrusion also exposed customer billing data, model weights, and 
anonymized chat transcripts belonging to productivity app Notion
.
Nearly 150,000 Uber drivers affected
A subsequent filing with the Texas Attorney General identified 149,346 people affected by the breach, substantially more than the 7,870 initially reported across South Carolina, Massachusetts, and Rhode Island.
According to
 
SecurityWeek
, the stolen information included driver's license images, names, addresses, dates of birth, license numbers, and other government-issued identification numbers.
Stolen driver's license images can be particularly valuable to identity thieves because they contain information commonly used during account verification.
Attackers used compromised third-party credentials to access data stored in an Uber-managed cloud environment. Although security alerts were enabled, none were triggered because the attackers used valid credentials. Uber said the intrusion did not extend to its other systems.
Jason Brown, director of customer advisory and counter fraud lead at iCOUNTER, said driver's licenses are frequently used by banks, lenders, mobile carriers, and other businesses to verify customers.
 
 
 
 
Advertisement
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
"A driver's license is one of the most useful documents a fraudster can get, because so many identity checks are built around it," Brown said.
He warned that criminals could use stolen license details to open fraudulent accounts or combine genuine personal information with fabricated details to create synthetic identities.
A clear image of a legitimate license presents an additional risk because it may help criminals bypass online document verification checks or create convincing forgeries.
The incident highlights the risks of third-party access to sensitive information and the importance of detecting suspicious activity even when attackers use legitimate credentials.
Protecting compromised driver identities
Even if the attackers were primarily interested in AI access tokens, the 
exposed personal information
 could create longer-term risks for affected drivers.
"Personal data like this rarely stays with the people who stole it," warned Jason Brown, director of customer advisory and counter fraud lead at iCOUNTER. "It gets packaged and sold, often matched with records from other breaches so buyers can build a fuller profile of each person."
 
 
 
 
Advertisement
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Brown recommended that affected drivers take immediate steps to protect their identities.
Enroll in credit monitoring:
 Uber is offering complimentary credit monitoring and fraud assistance through TransUnion subsidiary Cyberscout. Drivers must enroll within 90 days of the date on their notification letter.
Freeze credit files:
 Place free security freezes with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. A freeze can prevent most new credit accounts from being opened without authorization, although it does not stop every form of identity fraud.
Check credit reports and financial records:
 Review reports for unfamiliar accounts and monitor bank statements, tax records, and unexpected change-of-address notices.
Contact the DMV if necessary:
 Drivers who discover their license numbers are being misused should contact their state Department of Motor Vehicles. Some states may issue replacement numbers in cases of documented fraud.
Watch for impersonation attempts:
 Be suspicious of unexpected messages claiming to come from Uber, particularly those requesting personal information or payment details. Open the Uber app directly instead of following links in unsolicited messages.

The incident also highlights a broader challenge for businesses adopting third-party AI services: understanding which sensitive records external providers can access and ensuring that compromised vendor credentials cannot expose more information than necessary.
 
 
For affected drivers, the consequences may extend beyond fraudulent credit applications. 
Stolen identification documents can also be used to impersonate individuals during account registration or customer support interactions, potentially allowing criminals to exploit verification processes that rely on government-issued IDs.
The breach also raises questions about how organizations respond when trusted third-party accounts are compromised. 
Beyond restricting access to sensitive records, businesses need to assess whether their monitoring systems can identify unusual activity involving legitimate credentials and whether they can quickly revoke access when suspicious behavior is detected.

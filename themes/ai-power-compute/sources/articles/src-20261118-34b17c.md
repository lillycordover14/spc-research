Senators criticize Apple and Google for letting teens access ‘nudify’ apps despite parental controls
Exclusive: three Senate Democrats send letters to tech CEOs saying safeguards against nudification apps are insufficient on their platforms
Johana Bhuiyan
Wed 18 Nov 2026 11.39 EST
Last modified on Wed 18 Nov 2026 15.08 EST
Share

A trio of US senators says Apple and 
Google
 are failing to effectively detect and remove apps that can be used to generate nonconsensual nude or pornographic imagery. The senators also say Meta is continuing to profit off of ads for these so-called nudification apps that “victimize women and girls worldwide”.
On Wednesday, Ron Wyden, Ed Markey and Ben Luján sent letters to the CEOs of the tech companies, saying the safeguards they have in place to keep nudification off their app stores or platforms are not sufficient.
The senators sent the letters after multiple studies by the Tech Transparency Project (TTP), the latest of which was 
exclusively
 reported in the Guardian, found that these apps are either available on Apple and Google app stores or are being advertised in ads on Meta platforms in spite of company policies that prohibit them. According to the studies, children continue to have easy access to these apps.

Wyden, of Oregon, said Congress will need to act if the companies “don’t do a better job of cracking down on apps expressly designed and marketed to create harm”.
“AI has supercharged the ability to create and share nonconsensual sexual images, creating tremendous harm to women and girls,” Wyden said in a statement to the Guardian.
“Instead of ramping up investment to meet this challenge, big tech companies are cutting back on the very teams responsible for protecting users from harm. I’m looking for answers about why Meta, 
Apple
 and Google are missing obvious violations of their own rules.”
In a statement, Karl Ryan, a Google spokesperson, said: “Google Play does not allow apps that contain sexual content or allow users to nudify images of people, and we continually take proactive steps to detect and remove apps with harmful content.”
A 
Meta
 spokesman, Andy Stone, said in a statement: “We prohibit CSAM [child sexual abuse material] and the promotion of nudify apps, and we work aggressively to keep this material off our services. The criminals behind it take increasingly deceptive steps to evade detection – hiding behind fake names and identities, running ads that look benign, and sometimes linking to Google’s and Apple’s app stores, with the ads’ true intent revealed only after clicking through.
“We’ve taken extensive steps against these perpetrators, from banning Keling Technology Limited and taking legal action against other advertisers, to going after the coordinated networks and removing 92 million sexual ads – and we’re actively exploring new ways to improve our detection.”
The senators wrote that AI-generated nonconsensual nude imagery has seen an uptick in the last two years. Surveys bear that assertion out. Unicef reported at the start of 2026 that at least 1.2 million children in 11 countries reported being victimized of nonconsensual nude images. Law enforcement have also warned in various intelligence memos that officers should remain vigilant because AI may make it easier to circumvent CSAM detection methods, according to 
documents
 the Guardian previously reported on.
Apple clarified its policies after TTP researchers reached out about nudification apps they found on the app store in 
October
. Google previously 
told the Guardian that
 the company proactively works to detect and remove apps with harmful content and Meta introduced new AI tools to detect and remove ads that contain CSAM.
Keith Chu, the chief communications adviser and deputy policy director to Wyden, said that clarifying policies is not the same thing as enforcing them. Despite the companies reaffirming the prohibition of these apps, they continue to proliferate across Apple and Google app stores or be promoted in ads on Meta platforms, he said.
In the latest TTP study in October, which the Guardian first reported, 
researchers found
 they were able to access dozens of apps with features that enabled the creation of AI-generated nude and pornographic imagery, despite indicating the accounts they created were for 13-year-old boys.
There were 135 apps available to these accounts across Apple and Google. In response to inquiries from TTP and the Guardian, Apple and Google removed the apps that were mentioned in the studies. However, the apps remained functional on phones they were already downloaded on.
These findings suggest that Apple and Google are “not merely failing to remove applications that facilitate intimate image abuse, it may also be profiting from their distribution”, the letter from the senators said.

Katie Paul, the director of TTP, said that though Apple and Google say they review every app on their platform, the companies do not disclose what that review entails.
“Vague descriptions of how each company defines ‘nonconsensual intimate imagery’ and lack of detail in how both companies actually go about app review allow them to have it both ways: claiming they have policies on the books, and applying those policies based on what best fits the narrative at any given time,” Paul said.
Apple, for its part, also said that some developers will pull a “bait-and-switch” and add features that enable users to create AI-generated nude or pornographic imagery after the iPhone maker approves an app. Wyden’s office said that they expect the company to review apps on a continuing basis.
On Meta’s social networks, however, many of these developers advertise much more explicitly the nudification functions their apps include. TTP and other research groups have discovered hundreds of paid ads on Instagram and Facebook that include AI-generated sexualized images of real children and other CSAM.
In September, TTP found 332 paid ads on Facebook and Instagram that included CSAM. This month, AIForensics, a European non-profit research organization, found that some apps that were hiding their AI nudification features from Apple and Google were explicitly promoting “sexual deepfake functionality” in ads on Meta platforms.
“Despite Meta’s stated prohibition on these ads, independent investigations have documented extensive ad campaigns on Facebook and Instagram directing customers to apps capable of digitally undressing people in photographs or placing their likeness into sexually explicit content without their consent,” the senators wrote in their letter to Meta’s CEO, Mark Zuckerberg.
In the letter, the senators asked how ads containing apparent CSAM passed the company’s review systems.
On 7 October, Meta said that it had taken additional measures to combat ads with CSAM, however, Paul said TTP researchers have reported dozens of ads containing CSAM to the National Center for Missing & Exploited 
Children
 since then. Screenshots TTP shared with the Guardian show images of an ad for an app to create “AI sex friends” highlighting images of young girls. These ads were active for several hours before being taken down but remain available in Meta’s ad library. The organization has “yet to identify ads featuring videos of CSAM run by any other ad platforms”.
“Meta has repeatedly boasted about its sophisticated AI systems for reviewing harms on platforms, but for more than a year those systems have repeatedly failed to flag ads featuring graphic sexual videos of children as young as toddlers,” Paul said.

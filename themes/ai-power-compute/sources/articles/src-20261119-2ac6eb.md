Palo Alto's $4.5B Applied Compute Purchase Targets AI Risks

Applied Compute Deal Aims to Replace Static Controls With AI-Powered Supervision

Palo Alto Networks will purchase for $4.5 billion a startup led by an ex-OpenAI technical staff member to train, deploy and secure enterprise AI models.

Why Banks Are Rethinking Fraud and Cybersecurity Together

CEO Nikesh Arora said organizations cannot effectively protect autonomous AI systems through static controls alone, meaning AI security must evolve toward models trained to understand and supervise other AI systems. Buying Applied Compute gives Palo Alto Networks the technical expertise to pursue that strategy while creating a multibillion-dollar revenue opportunity tied to enterprise AI adoption. 

"We expect that as models continue to get smarter, they will obviate static human controls," Arora told investors during an earnings call Wednesday. "Because of this, AI security models will need to be equipped with advanced reasoning capabilities that are on par with the frontier. Trusting LLMs will require that we can independently verify, explain, and audit AI implementations." 

This will be the third largest cybersecurity acquisition of 2026, behind only Zurich's $11 billion buy of Beazley and Proofpoint's proposed $6.3 billion acquisition of Varonis. It's also the second biggest deal in Palo Alto Networks' 22-year history, behind only the company's $25 billion purchase of privileged access management behemoth CyberArk in February 2026 (see: 
Palo Alto: Security Debt Exposes Firms to AI-Powered Attacks
).

Why Firms Need AI Security Models With Advanced Reasoning

Applied Compute, founded in May 2025, employs 50 people and earlier this month notched a $100 million annualized revenue run rate and closed a $350 million funding round at a $3.25 billion valuation led by Elad Gil. The company has been led since inception by Yash Patil, who graduated from Stanford in 2025 with a bachelor's in computer science and spent more than two years on OpenAI's technical staff. 

Palo Alto Networks expects Applied Compute to add more than $1 billion in next-generation security annual recurring revenue by 2029, and Arora said the secure enterprise AI market could eventually exceed the company's existing cybersecurity business in scale. Palo Alto Networks' stock is up $25.97 - or 7.64% - to $365.89 per share in after-hours trading, which is the highest it has traded since Oct. 29. 

Arora told investors that traditional security controls will become less effective as AI systems gain autonomy, meaning deterministic security measures won't be able to anticipate every action an AI agent might take or every attempt to circumvent established controls. Protecting these systems will require AI security models with advanced reasoning capabilities comparable to those of frontier AI models, he said. 

"We believe both trust and control are essential to driving enterprise adoption of AI," Arora said. "We're already seeing the desire for sovereignty and control compel enterprises to adopt open-weight and open-source models. And early signs that shift are clear, with open-weight models growing from under five percent of token traffic to more than sixty percent over the past year." 

Arora expects demand for secure, customizable AI models that organizations can deploy on-premises or within sovereign infrastructure to create an enterprise market opportunity worth hundreds of billions of dollars. Palo Alto Networks intends to develop AI security models that supervise the applications they protect, and Arora said effective AI supervision requires visibility into how an application was trained. 

"When organizations put AI at the center of their operations, they want to retain control of their proprietary data, institutional judgment, and competitive edge inside their own four walls," Arora said. "We expect a future with a serving opportunity in the hundreds of billions of dollars when enterprise applications are driven by open-weight and open-source adoption." 

Why Palo Alto Chose Applied Computing Over Eight Competitors

Arora said Palo Alto Networks initially focused on implementing controls against threats such as prompt injection, but incidents involving AI systems demonstrated that predefined security policies cannot anticipate every possible agent behavior. The company concluded that it needed specialized LLMs capable of interpreting AI activity and identifying potentially dangerous behavior in real time, he said. 

"Deterministic controls cannot anticipate every scenario that an agent is going to conduct, or every deception that it's going to try and do," Arora said. "We figured that it was not just a moniker to say fight AI with AI. We actually would have to have an LLM trained to interpret, understand, and stop AI from misbehaving." 

Palo Alto Networks evaluated nine providers before selecting Applied Compute for its expertise in post-training open-weight models, and Arora said the company plans to embed its capabilities around more than 35 specialized use cases designed to counter AI-enabled threats. The company also intends to provide enterprises with secure AI training, inference and supervision capabilities, according to Arora. 

"That's the level of intelligence we need to be able to stop AI," Arora said. "If I don't understand how the models are behaving and not behaving, how am I going to be able to stop it? For that, I need frontier LLM capability and frontier LLM skills. Eventually, that's where the industry is going to want to go, and you'll see a lot of people try and follow our lead on this one." 

Palo Alto Networks expects its business to become aligned with AI token consumption and enterprise traffic volumes, and Arora said AI security spending could eventually represent 2% to 5% of enterprise token expenditure. Arora expects growing AI traffic to increase demand for Prisma AI, network security, Cortex, observability, identity protection and Applied Compute's training and inference services. 

"We're pivoting to ensure all of our businesses are aligned towards token flow, because over time, as AI gets more and more ubiquitous and as it gets more and more consumptive, we think Palo Alto's business model will align with the consumption of AI," Arora said.

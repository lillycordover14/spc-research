Palo Alto Networks bets $4.5B on enterprise AI with Applied Compute acquisition&nbsp;&nbsp;CSO Online

[page text]
Palo Alto Networks has agreed to buy Applied Compute in an all-stock deal valued at $4.5 billion, a move the company said will let it train, deploy and secure open-weight AI models for enterprises in the public cloud or in their own sovereign environments.

“The acquisition will bring Applied Compute’s platform and world-class AI research team to Palo Alto Networks to help enterprises adopt AI securely, whether they choose open-weight models, closed frontier models, or both,” the company said in a 
statement
 released alongside its fiscal first quarter 2027 results. The statement described Applied Compute as “a leading platform for post-training and inference of open-weight AI models” and said the startup will continue to serve AI-native companies, digital natives and large enterprises.

The transaction is expected to close in Palo Alto’s fiscal third quarter of 2027, which runs from February to April, the statement said. Applied Compute co-founder and CEO Yash Patil will become Palo Alto’s co-chief AI officer, and the startup’s research team will help build AI models across the company’s offerings.

A security company that wants to run models

Nikesh Arora, Palo Alto’s chairman and CEO, said on the company’s 
earnings call
 that beyond building AI into its own security products, “we will help train and serve secure sovereign implementations of open-weight models to the enterprise.”

“Open-weight model implementations will allow us to deliver secure, open-weight models to our customers to enable adoption of secure AI in the enterprise,” Arora said. He said 
open-weight models
 had grown from under 5% of token traffic to more than 60% over the past year, without saying where the figure came from.

Asked by JP Morgan analyst Brian Essex about customer demand, Arora said deterministic security controls “cannot anticipate every scenario that an agent is going to conduct.” Enterprises building AI applications “will have to train a supervisory AI model at the same time,” he said, because Palo Alto cannot secure models it has no part in building.

“I cannot go in and intercept and understand somebody else’s application,” he said.

Sanchit Vir Gogia, chief analyst at Greyhound Research, said Arora has a point, since a generic filter cannot know how a particular application is meant to behave. But most failures in AI applications happen at runtime, through prompts, tools, and permissions, whoever trained the model, he said. He noted that Palo Alto has sold model-agnostic protection through Prisma AIRS since 2025.

“A supervisor trained by the same hands as the system it supervises is a colleague, not a check,” Gogia said, adding that CIOs should make sure someone other than the trainer tests the result.

GPUs and data centers

To train and host those models, Palo Alto plans to build its own compute capacity, CFO Dipak Golechha said on the call. The spending “will mostly go to GPUs and servers,” with a smaller share for the facilities that house them, he said. Palo Alto will “continue to source from our cloud partners” and add dedicated capacity over time, Golechha said. Arora said the company aims for more than100 megawatts of compute in the next three to five years.

Gogia said Palo Alto has sovereignty credentials for security data, including customer-held keys and a European Cortex service governed by Deutsche Telekom. “None of that yet extends to GPUs,” he said, adding that a US-headquartered operator faces the same jurisdictional questions as the hyperscalers it rents capacity from.

“A sovereign hosting offer with no named region, operator or date is a promise looking for an address,” Gogia said. Compliance officers should ask Palo Alto for a region list, the operator, and a dated certification plan, he said.

Arora said on the call that the training and inference business could eventually exceed Palo Alto’s core market. “This could be bigger than what we do today,” he said.

One vendor, more of the stack

The deal follows Palo Alto’s 
$25 billion purchase of CyberArk
, which added identity security to its platform. Gogia said Applied Compute would now add the models themselves to a supplier already at the center of many customers’ firewalls, SOC, and identity controls.

“Palo Alto is asking enterprises to let the guard build the vault it guards,” he said. One team watching both model and telemetry can spot trouble sooner, he said, but a single fault, breach or pricing dispute would land “on the protection and the protected together.”

CIOs should get a costed exit in writing before any model work moves across, Gogia said. Applied Compute customers should use the months before closing to secure ownership of trained weights in an exportable format, no training on their data without consent, a right to exit on change of control and price protection that survives bundling, he said. “Terms are cheapest to win while the suitor is still courting,” Gogia said.

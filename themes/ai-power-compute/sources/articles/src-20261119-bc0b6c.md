Palo Alto Networks to buy Applied Compute in $4.5 billion all-stock deal&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
A firewall company is paying more than a billion dollars over its target's last valuation for a team that trains custom AI models. If your company is planning to run its own models, the security vendor you already pay may soon be the one selling you the tooling.

Palo Alto Networks said on Wednesday, November 18, that it will acquire Applied Compute, a startup that helps companies train, serve and improve their own AI models, in an all-stock transaction valued at about $4.5 billion, subject to adjustments. The press release, filed with the SEC as an 8-K exhibit, says the deal needs regulatory clearance and is expected to close in Palo Alto's fiscal third quarter of 2027.

It arrived alongside the company's first-quarter earnings. Those beat. Revenue rose 39% year over year to $3.44 billion, ahead of the company's own guidance of $3.30 billion to $3.31 billion.

Applied Compute was founded in 2025 by three former OpenAI researchers, Yash Patil, Rhythm Garg and Linden Li. Its platform handles post-training and inference for open-weight models, which means taking a model like those from Meta or Mistral and tuning it on a company's own data so it does one job well. The startup raised $350 million earlier this month at a $3.25 billion valuation, led by Elad Gil, and had reached a $100 million annualized revenue run rate within 18 months of founding.

Do the arithmetic. Palo Alto is paying about $1.25 billion more than investors did a few weeks ago. It's paying in its own stock, not cash.

Patil will become Palo Alto's co-chief AI officer once the deal closes. In his statement he said joining Palo Alto will "put security at the center of all our products" and speed up the startup's traction with the largest organizations.

Why would a security company want this?

CEO Nikesh Arora gave the reasoning in plain terms: "Every enterprise is going to run a mix of open and closed models, and every one of those models needs to be secured." The pitch is that no large provider currently offers secure handling of that mix with compliance built in. Applied Compute's customers are AI-native companies, digital natives and large enterprises, and Palo Alto says it will keep serving them while opening the platform to its own customer base.

Frankly, that reads like a distribution play as much as a technology one. Applied Compute has a product companies want and a hundred million dollars of revenue to show for it. Palo Alto has the enterprise relationships, the security budgets and the compliance conversations. Putting model training inside the same vendor that governs access to it is a sales argument you can make to a chief information security officer in one sentence.

Whether it works technically is another matter. Building a model pipeline and securing one are different engineering problems, and Palo Alto hasn't published how the two will be joined or on what timeline. Nothing in the announcement says whether Applied Compute's existing customers, some of whom presumably compete with Palo Alto's own customers, will be comfortable with a security vendor owning their training stack.

A pattern of AI buying

It's far from Palo Alto's first AI purchase. Palo Alto bought Protect AI in 2025 and agreed to acquire Chronosphere for $3.35 billion, and it also announced a deal for CyberArk. Taken together, the buying runs from protecting AI models, to the observability and gateway layers around them, and now to the place where models get built.

The Applied Compute price is the largest of the AI-focused startup deals in that list. It's also for a company that didn't exist 18 months ago. That tells you how fast the market is bidding for people who know how to train models on private data. Palo Alto isn't alone in wanting them, and it's decided that owning a team is safer than renting a partnership.

If you're a buyer, the practical question is simple. Ask whether your security vendor's new model tooling is built to your standards or bundled to protect its own revenue. For now, the only dated commitment is the close, which Palo Alto expects in its fiscal third quarter of 2027, pending regulators.

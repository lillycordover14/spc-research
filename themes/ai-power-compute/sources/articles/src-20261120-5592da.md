Indian engineer Yash Sharma decides to move to the UK from the US even without a job. He has been working for Google for six years.
 
Yash Sharma, a 36-year-old Indian software engineer working at Google in San Francisco, has decided to move to the UK even though he does not have a job there. But the visa uncertainties in the US have become unbearable.
 After spending six years on an 
H-1B visa
, Sharma has decided to leave the US and move to the UK next month, he said to Bloomberg News. He described the US as a "golden prison", saying that the constant changes to immigration rules, limited prospects of obtaining permanent residency and fear of being unable to return after travelling abroad have taken a toll on his professional and personal life.
Sharma said he had become increasingly discouraged about his chances of converting his temporary work visa into permanent residency because of the enormous Green Card backlog facing Indian nationals. 
He has not travelled outside the US for 18 months because he fears he may not be allowed to return. As a result, he has missed family visits, professional conferences and trips with friends. He also explored an alternative immigration route by applying for an extraordinary ability visa, which is intended for individuals with exceptional achievements in their professional fields.
 But his application was rejected after months of waiting.
Sharma started exploring options outside the US and applied for the UK's Global Talent visa and received approval. The visa allows him to relocate to Britain without having secured a job beforehand and search for employment after arriving. "I've seen various administrations, and to me it's clear that there will be no actual change to the law," Sharma said.
"The US feels like a golden prison," he said.
Trump's H-1B crackdown adds to uncertainty
Trump's immigration crackdown began shortly after he returned to the White House in January 2025. 
While the administration initially focused on illegal immigration, its policies increasingly extended to legal immigration programmes, including the H-1B system used by American companies to recruit highly skilled foreign professionals.
In September 2025, Trump announced a $100,000 payment requirement for certain new H-1B workers seeking to enter the US from abroad.
The administration argued that the programme had been abused by employers who used lower-paid foreign workers instead of hiring American employees. 
The changes have created additional uncertainty for companies that depend on international talent, particularly in the technology sector.
The administration has also introduced a wage-weighted H-1B selection system, giving applicants offered higher prevailing wages better chances of being selected in the visa lottery.
For workers already in the US, the difficulties extend beyond obtaining an H-1B visa. Long waits for employment-based green cards, visa stamping delays and uncertainty over future immigration rules have made long-term planning increasingly difficult.
IBM ends visa sponsorship for most consulting jobs
American companies are also changing their approach to foreign hiring. IBM told employees this month that it would discontinue visa and green card sponsorship for the majority of positions in its IBM Consulting division. The affected employees have been given the option of continuing their work for the company from locations outside the US or leaving IBM by the end of the year.
Soon after IBM, Boston-based financial technology company Flywire announced they were also evaluating how immigration restrictions could affect its workforce and business operations.The company processes billions of dollars in payments every month for colleges and other clients. 
In its quarterly filing this month, Flywire said it was considering shifting hiring and product development activities to lower-cost countries where it already operates to offset rising immigration-related expenses.
 The company warned that difficulties in recruiting and retaining specialised employees could slow product development.

Chris Thomas, an immigration lawyer and partner at Holland & Hart in Denver, told Bloomberg that corporate clients were concerned that even an inadvertent compliance mistake could expose them to government investigations or restrictions on their ability to sponsor foreign workers.
Some companies are questioning whether navigating the increasingly complicated immigration system is worth the effort. "Some are saying it's too much, it's time to start offshoring," Thomas said. Kate Angustia, supervisory policy and practice counsel at the American Immigration Lawyers Association, warned that the uncertainty was creating instability across the immigration system. "We are creating a brain drain," she said.

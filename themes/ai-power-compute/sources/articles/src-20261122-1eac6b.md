‘Golden Prison’: Indian Google engineer leaves US after six years on H-1B visa

Skilled workers from India, including a Google engineer on an H-1B visa, are leaving the US as Trump's visa changes make life hard to plan.

Skilled workers from India are among those choosing to leave the US as Trump’s visa changes make life hard to plan, according to a Bloomberg report. The administration has reshaped the rules that brought graduates and skilled staff into the US workforce, and Bloomberg says this has effectively dismantled a system that drew top engineers and researchers.

Democrats won control of Congress in this month’s midterm elections, and Trump said his administration may have been “too tough” in deporting people who had jobs. But the new opposition will find it hard to reverse the visa changes. This week, the Labor Department proposed tighter rules for companies that sponsor foreign workers for permanent residency.

A Google engineer who has had enough

Yash Sharma, 36, is a software engineer from India who works at Google. He has held an 
H-1B visa
 for six years. He says constant rule changes and slim odds of becoming a citizen have made it hard to focus at work. He feels he has run out of ways to get permanent residency, partly because of the huge backlog for Indian nationals.

“Golden prison” is how he describes the US. His application for an “extraordinary ability” visa was rejected after months of waiting, and he has not left the country in a year and a half, for fear he might not be let back in. He will move to the UK next month on a Global Talent visa, even though he has no job there. “That normal still sucks,” he said, even if the old rules came back.

Swapnil Deshpande, another Indian national, is a senior data analyst at a US tech firm. He came to the US in 2018 to study at Northeastern University. He now asks, “Is the uncertainty, stress, unpredictability really worth it?”

Firms change their plans

IBM told staff this month that it will stop sponsoring foreign workers for most jobs in its consulting division, according to a document Bloomberg reviewed. Affected staff must move outside the US or leave by the end of the year.

Payments firm Flywire is weighing moving some hiring to cheaper places. Microsoft, Adobe and some IT outsourcing firms have been barred from a key green-card sponsorship programme while officials look into them.

Lawyer Chris Thomas of Holland & Hart said clients fear one slip could bring a ban or criminal probe. “Some are saying it’s too much, it’s time to start offshoring,” he said.

What the administration says

The administration says employers and workers have abused the system for years, turning a route for temporary, specialised talent into a large programme that has lowered pay for Americans. A recent study by a Harvard economist who served on Trump’s Council of Economic Advisers found that skilled immigration lowers pay for college-educated US workers, though Bloomberg noted that research on the question is mixed.

Pressure has grown this month, with Trump naming Stephen Miller, a long-time supporter of cutting all forms of immigration, as chief of staff. Vice President JD Vance announced investigations into employers’ green-card sponsorships last month. The administration also added a separate $103,000 fee for new H-1B applications in August, despite a court order blocking an earlier $100,000 payment. Many changes face court challenges.

Early signs in the data

The US issued about 260,000 fewer temporary visas in the 2025 budget year than the year before, according to Migration Policy Institute analysis of federal data. The Institute of International Education estimates that new foreign student numbers fell about 15% this autumn.

Angelo Paparelli, an immigration lawyer at Vialto Law, called the changes “a quiet dismantling of employment-based immigration”. Kate Angustia of the American Immigration Lawyers Association said the instability is creating a “brain drain”.

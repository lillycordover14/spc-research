Together AI Prompt Leak: 3 Models, 4 Days, 1 Credential

 

Daniel Roth

Updated Nov 22, 2026 · 17 min read

 

Photo: W.carter, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons
 
Together AI told customers on November 21, 2026 that an unauthorized party downloaded stored prompt samples from the company’s serverless inference service. According to the 
Together AI security notice
, the trail starts with an AI coding agent that committed a credential while helping develop a public GitHub repository called xorl. That one credential opened a private Hugging Face repository holding prompt samples from June and July 2026.

The affected requests went to three models: MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6. Together AI said its automated scanning caught the exposed credential and that it reverted the commit. By then, the company says, someone else had already used the key to pull the data. This is a Together AI prompt leak that began as an ordinary developer mistake, with the difference that the developer was software.

Security teams have watched vendor-side exposures pile up all month. Our earlier look at the 
JPMorgan vendor breach
 showed how little customers can see when a supplier loses control of data. The Together AI case adds a new wrinkle, because the leak path ran through an AI coding agent rather than a phished employee or an unpatched server. This analysis sorts what is confirmed from what is not, and explains what teams that send prompts to hosted models should do about it.

What Together AI Says Happened

The core sequence, as laid out in Together AI’s notice, runs in five steps. First, engineers were developing xorl, a public GitHub repository. Second, an AI coding agent working on that project committed a credential. Third, the credential granted access to a private Hugging Face repository. Fourth, an unauthorized party obtained the credential. Fifth, that party downloaded prompt samples stored in the private repository.

Together AI’s automated scanning flagged the exposed credential, and the company reverted the commit. That response matters, but it did not undo the exposure. A reverted commit disappears from the branch tip, yet anyone who copied the key while it was public still holds a working secret unless someone revokes it. The notice, as reported, does not tell readers how long the credential stayed visible before the revert, so that gap stays open.

Together AI also made a statement about containment. The company said it had no reason to believe the data leaked further and understood that the downloaded data had been deleted. Note the wording: this is the company’s stated understanding, not a verified outcome. Nobody outside Together AI has confirmed deletion, and the sources reviewed for this story do not include an independent check.

The disclosure drew quick pickup from smaller AI and security outlets.

The core claim, then, rests mostly on one primary document and its echoes. That is normal in the first 24 hours of an incident, and it is a reason to read later updates closely.

How an AI Coding Agent Committed a Credential

The xorl repository as the entry point

The leak started in a public repository, which is the part that makes it avoidable in hindsight. Public GitHub repositories are scraped constantly by bots that hunt for tokens, so a secret committed there has a short life before someone grabs it. The credential in this case reached a private Hugging Face repository, so the blast radius depended on what that repository held.

Together AI’s notice assigns the commit to an AI coding agent, not to a named engineer. That framing is accurate as far as it goes, but agents act under human direction and inside human-configured environments. Someone supplied the token to the agent’s working environment, and someone allowed the agent to commit to a public repository without a gate that would block secrets. Those are process decisions, and they sit with the team.

Why scanning caught it but did not save it

Detection after the fact is a different control from prevention before the push. Together AI’s automated scanning did its job by spotting the credential and triggering the revert. But post-push scanning runs on a clock, and an attacker’s scraper runs on its own. When the two race, the scraper sometimes wins. Push protection that rejects the commit before it ever reaches a public remote closes that window, which is why many teams now treat it as table stakes.

Beam’s write-up, published at 
agentbeam.com
, frames the incident around the xorl agent and the June to July window, and it reads the lesson as an inference supply chain problem. The framing fits. A serverless inference vendor sits between its customers and the model weights, so any weak link inside the vendor’s own tooling can reach customer data.

What Was in the Private Hugging Face Repository

According to the notice, the repository held samples gathered for software testing from requests to MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6. In other words, the store was not a full archive of customer traffic. It was a set of samples pulled for engineering purposes, which explains why the dates cluster on four days rather than covering June and July evenly.

Sampling for testing is a common practice in any inference business. Engineers need real-world inputs to check latency, formatting and model behavior, and synthetic prompts rarely match what users actually send. The trade-off is that real prompts carry real content. A developer who pastes a stack trace, a config file or a customer record into a chatbot has no idea the text might land in a test set months later.

The sources do not say how many prompts the repository held, how many customers sent them, or whether they contained personal or confidential data. Those numbers are unconfirmed, and this article does not estimate them. Any figure that circulates in social posts, whether a prompt total or a customer count, has no backing in the reporting reviewed.

The nature of the contents still matters for risk. Prompts to code-capable models often include source snippets, internal hostnames and sometimes keys. Which brings up the second-order concern that dominates the secondary reporting: what the downloader did with the text.

MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6: Why These Models Matter

The three model names in the notice show what kind of traffic Together AI handles: developers calling third-party models through a hosted API instead of running them in-house. Our earlier comparison of 
open-weight versus open-source AI licenses
 covers how licensing differs across models in this space, which is useful background when you decide who should host what.

Here lies an irony. Teams often choose a third-party host partly for privacy, expecting a neutral host to handle prompts more carefully than the model maker would. The Together AI incident shows that privacy depends on the host’s operational hygiene. A prompt sent to a poorly guarded host leaks just as readily whichever model answers it.

The sources also do not establish whether MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6 are the newest releases from their respective developers as of late November 2026. They establish only that these were the labels named in the notice. Readers choosing a model today should check each vendor’s current lineup rather than infer anything from this incident.

Nothing in the reporting suggests the model developers did anything wrong. The exposure sat in Together AI’s testing data, not in the models’ weights or the developers’ infrastructure. That distinction protects the model makers from blame, but it does nothing for the users whose text was sampled.

The Credential Hunt: What the Downloader Did Next

Secondary reports say the unauthorized party did more than download. According to those accounts, the party searched the prompt samples for credentials belonging to other services and tested whether any of them worked. That is the detail that turns a single leak into a possible chain.

The practical takeaway holds regardless of what the downloader achieved. Any credential that ever appeared in a prompt sent to a hosted model during June or July 2026 should be treated as potentially seen by a third party. Rotating those keys costs an hour. Discovering a misuse later costs far more.

Market Impact for Inference Providers and Enterprise Buyers

No financial impact has been reported in the sources reviewed. No source describes customer cancellations or contract changes. Any claim about lost revenue today would be invention.

The market effect, if there is one, will arrive through procurement. Enterprise security questionnaires for AI vendors already ask about data retention and subprocessors. After this disclosure, expect added questions on whether vendors store prompt samples at all, who can read them, and how secrets are kept out of repositories that AI agents touch.

Buyers hold three levers. They can demand zero retention for production traffic, which removes the sample store as a target. They can require contractual breach notification windows measured in days rather than months. And they can ask vendors to show how their own engineering agents are sandboxed. Vendors who answer those three questions well will gain an edge, while those who answer vaguely will lose deals to rivals that can.

Smaller hosts carry the most exposure. A startup with a handful of engineers and an aggressive agent-assisted workflow has fewer review layers than a large cloud, yet it handles the same kind of sensitive text. The pressure on that tier to adopt secret scanning and push protection will rise. Our 
security coverage
 will track whether other inference hosts publish similar audits.

The comparison carries one lesson. Moving prompts off a vendor does not remove the risk of an AI agent committing a secret. It moves that risk into your own repositories, where your own agents operate. Self-hosting buys control over retention and access, but it demands the same push protection and credential discipline that failed here.

For teams staying on serverless APIs, the most useful lever is the contract. Ask whether the vendor keeps prompt samples, for how long, and whether they live in a separate store from production logs. A vendor that cannot answer clearly has told you something already.

Historical Context: Secrets in Public Repos Are an Old Problem

Credentials in public repositories rank among the oldest and most repeated failures in software security. Developers have pushed cloud keys, database passwords and API tokens to public remotes for as long as public remotes have existed. Platforms responded with secret scanning and push protection, and scanners now run on most major hosts.

Supply chain incidents repeat the pattern at different layers. In a recent case we covered, 
Microsoft’s Agent Framework was compromised on PyPI
 through two releases, a reminder that attackers go after the build and publishing pipeline as readily as the code. Likewise, the 
Uber vendor credential breach
 turned on a credential held by a supplier rather than by Uber itself.

What changed with Together AI is the author of the mistake. For a decade, the standard story involved a tired human, a rushed commit and a forgotten .env file. Now the committing party can be an agent that writes and pushes code at machine speed, often across many files in a single session. The volume of commits rises, and so does the chance that one of them carries a secret.

The old defenses still work. Scoped tokens, short lifetimes and pre-push scanning stop the same failure whether a person or a program triggers it. The new element is that the people who configure agents must now treat those defenses as part of the agent’s job description.

Coding Agents as a New Source of Secret Leaks

Agents read broadly. To be useful, a coding agent opens configuration files, environment dumps and test fixtures, and it can copy what it reads into new files or commit messages. A separate 
TechRadar report
 describes a different incident in which an AI coding assistant leaked production passwords and HR tokens after repeatedly reading entire configuration files across 10 sessions. It is not the Together AI case, but it shows the same behavior class at work.

Regulators and state officials are starting to treat agent control as a legal issue. Our coverage of California’s suit, in which the state says 
OpenAI lost control of autonomous agents 80 times
, shows that agent misbehavior is moving from engineering postmortems into court filings. Together AI’s disclosure fits that trend, even though it involves a very different company and a very different claim.

Three design flaws recur. Agents inherit the developer’s environment, including every token loaded in the shell. Agents can push to remotes without a human approving each commit. And the secrets they handle often have broad scope, so one leak reaches far. Fixing any one of those three would have changed the Together AI outcome.

Controls Security Teams Should Add This Week

You do not need a new platform to respond. Most of what follows uses features that exist in common Git hosts and cloud accounts. Start with the checks that cost the least time.

Turn on push protection
 for every public repository so a commit containing a token is rejected before it lands.
Strip tokens from agent environments.
 Give coding agents scoped, short-lived credentials, never your personal or admin keys.
Require human review
 before an agent pushes to any public remote.
Rotate and scope.
 Revoke any token that ever touched a public repo, even briefly, and prefer read-only tokens for test data.
Separate test data from customer data.
 Keep sampled prompts in a store with its own access policy and an expiry date.
Alert on odd downloads
 from private model or dataset repositories, such as large pulls from a new location.

What to Watch Next

Four details would sharpen the picture. The first is the date the credential was committed and the date of the download, which together set the exposure window. The second is a count of affected prompts and customers. The third is evidence on whether any credential found in the prompts worked elsewhere. The fourth is independent confirmation that the downloaded data was deleted, which Together AI currently reports only as its understanding.

Until those arrive, the responsible posture for customers is simple. Assume that anything sensitive sent to the three named models in June or July 2026 may have been in the sample set, and act on that assumption. Rotate secrets, review what staff pasted into prompts, and ask the vendor direct questions in writing. Together AI’s own notice remains the primary document, and readers should check it for updates.

FAQ: Together AI Prompt Leak

What did Together AI disclose?
 On November 21, 2026, the company said an unauthorized party downloaded stored prompt samples from its serverless inference service. The samples covered requests made in June and July 2026.

How did the leak happen?
 During development of the public GitHub repository xorl, an AI coding agent committed a credential. That credential gave access to a private Hugging Face repository holding the samples. The company’s automated scanning detected the credential and the commit was reverted, but the credential had already been obtained.

Which models were affected?
 The samples came from requests to MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6. They were collected for software testing, and they came mainly from four days between June 24 and July 14, 2026.

How many prompts or customers were affected?
 Together AI has not published a count in the material reviewed for this article. Any specific number you see elsewhere is unconfirmed.

Were credentials in the prompts used against other services?
 Reports say the unauthorized party searched the prompts for credentials and tested whether they worked. No source reviewed establishes that any of them gave successful access, so treat that as unconfirmed.

Has the stolen data been deleted?
 Together AI said it understood the data had been deleted and had no reason to believe it leaked further. That is the company’s stated understanding, and nothing independent confirms it.

What should I do if I sent prompts to these models in June or July?
 Rotate any API keys, tokens or passwords that may have appeared in those prompts, and review what sensitive text your team pasted in. Then ask Together AI in writing which of your requests fell within the sampled set.

Does this mean AI coding agents are unsafe to use?
 Not by itself. The incident shows that agents need the same secret controls as human developers, including scoped credentials, push protection and review before pushing to public repositories.

At 26 years old, Google AI security analyst and YouTuber Charles Broomfield is on track to earn about $323,000 this year — but he lives on less than minimum wage.
For the past few years, Broomfield has kept his annual spending on core living expenses under $20,000, less than the roughly $23,700 a full-time worker would earn at the minimum wage, 
averaged
 across all 50 states.
He does it by living with roommates in Washington, D.C., walking or biking instead of driving, eating free meals at work and rarely spending beyond his basic needs. Even when he splurges on something fun, he keeps things modest; in August, that meant a baseball game and a single $7 beer.
"I guess something else that probably does make me a little bit unique is I really don't feel joy when I buy things," he says. "I feel like I have what I need."

There is a goal behind all that saving. Broomfield has pursued financial independence since college and now expects to reach his target of $750,000 in investments within the next year, well ahead of his original goal of doing so by age 30. As his income has climbed, his lifestyle has largely stayed the same, he says.
Here's how Broomfield keeps his spending so low and what he plans to do once he no longer needs the paycheck.
Saving for financial freedom
Broomfield grew up in Colorado Springs, Colorado, the son of two military veterans. He and his siblings had to set aside half of everything they earned growing up. They could spend the other half or earn interest from their parents by putting that money away, too. 
By his teenage years, Broomfield had become a disciplined saver. At the time, he dreamed of using his cash stash to buy some of the familiar trappings of wealth.
"I wanted a Lamborghini and I wanted some nice penthouse," he says. "I just wanted them because I thought other people wanted them."
At 17, Broomfield discovered Mr. Money Mustache, a personal finance blog by a former software engineer who retired at 30. The blog advocates living frugally and investing much of your income to build a portfolio large enough to support you through withdrawals, without needing a paycheck. It introduced Broomfield to the financial independence, retire early — or FIRE — movement and gave all that saving a new purpose. 
Charles Broomfield in his bedroom in a Washington, D.C., apartment he shares with four roommates.
Matthew Ritenour | CNBC Make It
"All I had to do was live super frugally and I could actually reach financial freedom by the time I was 30 and go do whatever actually made me happy in life," he says.
The idea stuck with him. After graduating from Knox College in 2022, Broomfield moved to Northern Virginia for a job paying more than $100,000 a year and set a goal of reaching $750,000 in investments before 30 while spending less than $25,000 a year. 
He also decided to start at $0. Broomfield had roughly $50,000 in savings when he graduated, with his parents having covered the portion of his college tuition not paid for by scholarships. He wanted his FIRE total to reflect only what he earned after college.
'Geeked out' on extreme frugality
In his first year after college, Broomfield was "super geeked out" about reaching financial freedom, he says.
"It's almost the only thing I thought about, because I was building all these new habits and making real money for really the first time in my life," he says.
That enthusiasm sometimes took him to extremes. He even considered living in a van parked in his employer's garage to avoid paying rent.
"I crunched the numbers on it, and effectively every year that I spent living in a van rather than paying rent would allow me to retire approximately one month faster," he says. "It just [didn't] seem really worth it to go to that extreme."
Charles Broomfield at the grocery store.
Matthew Ritenour | CNBC Make It
But Broomfield still found satisfaction in squeezing every dollar out of his budget. He once biked two miles to a grocery store to save $1 on toothpaste rather than buy it at the Whole Foods next to his apartment.
"For a lot of people, they feel their wins by buying stuff for themselves," Broomfield says. "I get my wins by seeing how much I can invest."
Looking back, Broomfield acknowledges that some of his early efforts to save money made little difference in how quickly he could reach financial independence.
"I never expect anybody else to do exactly what I'm doing," he says. "I know I'm way more of a geek about this than most people, and I'm particularly well positioned to be able to live this kind of life."
How he lives on less than $20,000 a year
Broomfield's core living expenses totaled $1,477 in August, excluding savings, investments and payments toward his graduate school tuition.

Roughly 90% of Broomfield's income went to savings and investments in August, a fairly typical month for him, he says. He put about $7,931 into his 401(k) and another $10,000 into a taxable brokerage account. 
Another $700 went toward a 0% interest employer tuition repayment plan with a former employer, leaving him with a remaining balance of $16,800 in August.
Housing is Broomfield's biggest expense, though he keeps costs down by sharing an apartment with four roommates. In a neighborhood where one-bedroom apartments commonly rent for more than $2,000, the arrangement saves him about $1,000 a month.
One thing Broomfield is willing to splurge on is a martial arts gym membership.
 
He pays $229 a month to train as a kickboxer, even though he could work out for free at Google's office gym. Broomfield considers it a "young person's sport" and doesn't want to postpone competing until after he reaches financial independence. It's also a way to socialize with other amateur fighters who share his interest.
Charles Broomfield at the gym where he trains in kickboxing.

Broomfield doesn't own a car and generally walks, bikes or takes public transportation. Google covers his daily Metro commute, which helps keep his transportation costs low. He tries to meet friends near Metro stations and, when visiting his grandfather in Virginia, takes public transit as far as he can before using Uber for the last leg of the trip.
Before joining Google, Broomfield typically spent around $200 a month on food. Now that he eats most of his meals at work
,
 he spends about $70 a month on average. His $28 food bill in August was especially low because he had ingredients left over from July.
He also cooks less at home these days. Previously, he relied more on batch cooking, including pressure cooker chili that makes nine servings for about $2 each. For snacks, he might have a bowl of Raisin Bran or celery with peanut butter.
“I did the math once, and peanut butter was the lowest cost thing per gram of protein in the entire grocery store,” he says in a video on his YouTube channel, which he started in 2023 to document his FIRE journey.
For entertainment, he relies on the free version of YouTube, where he watches educational content about startups, business and entrepreneurship, or listens to music.
His income tripled, but his spending didn't
Broomfield expects to make about $323,000 in 2026, more than triple the roughly $100,000 he earned in his first year out of college. His compensation rose to about $120,000 over his first three years, then jumped to $200,000 when he joined Amazon Web Services in 2025. After a brief stint at Capital One, he moved to Google this year. By keeping his expenses low, investing most of what he earns and riding an upward-trending stock market, he has built up about $650,000 in investments since graduating from college.
Broomfield earns money from his YouTube channel as well, which has expanded to offer personal finance and career advice. About $48,000 of his projected income this year will come from the channel and a related career-coaching business.
Even as his income has grown, Broomfield has stuck with roommates, public transportation and the same modest spending habits. 
"I've really separated how happy I am from how much money I spend," he says. "I truly don't believe that I would be significantly happier if I spent a lot more money."

Much of the money he doesn't spend goes straight into investments. He maxes out tax-advantaged accounts including his 401(k) and HSA, and has sometimes contributed 100% of his paycheck to his 401(k) early in the year to reach the annual limit as quickly as possible.
Broomfield keeps his investment strategy simple, favoring broad-market index funds.
"I don't try and pick individual stocks. I just don't have the time to do the research and understand the risk and potential returns of those individual stocks," he says. "And the research shows that most people who try to pick individual stocks don't end up doing better than if they just invested in the stock market as a whole."
He avoids cryptocurrency for a similar reason. Broomfield says he doesn't understand what gives it value and doesn't want to put money into investments he doesn't understand.
At his current pace, Broomfield expects to reach his $750,000 investment goal within the next year.
What comes after financial independence
When Broomfield discovered the FIRE movement at 17, he worried about being stuck in what he imagined as a "soul-crushing" 9-to-5 office job until age 65. He saw financial independence as a way to quit working altogether.
But while financial independence would give him enough money to live without a paycheck, Broomfield no longer sees early retirement as the goal.
“Once I reach financial freedom, maybe this is a little ironic, but I actually think I will keep working,” he says.
His YouTube business already earns enough in most months to cover his expenses without a full-time job. With that income and his growing investments, Broomfield hopes to eventually build a business of his own, potentially in AI security.
"Having complete ownership and complete buy-in to something that's my own. That sounds really exciting to me," he says.
Charles Broomfield at home.

Whatever he chooses to do, Broomfield sees financial independence as a way to keep his options open. He bases his savings goal on the 
so-called “4% rule
, a retirement guideline that suggests withdrawing 4% of your investments in the first year, then adjusting withdrawals for inflation.
For Broomfield, that would mean roughly $30,000 a year from a $750,000 portfolio. That's enough to cover his current expenses, though he recognizes that could change if he eventually starts a family.
"There's a very good chance I won't want to live the way I do forever. That's one of the reasons I'm doing it now. I'm young. I'm healthy. I'm single," he says. "I totally understand that somebody with a family probably doesn't want to live with a bunch of roommates."
But the money Broomfield has already saved gives him more freedom to choose where and how much he works without feeling tied to a paycheck.
"Having that peace of mind to know that I'll be fine, even if I can't find work quickly or even if I get laid off tomorrow, that has just been a huge breath of fresh air for myself," he says.

US schools threaten to ditch Google Search over mandatory AI features
New York, Los Angeles and Chicago are considering alternative search engines after Google announced schools must enable AI tools to retain student search access; educators warn of risks to learning and critical thinking, while a lawsuit alleging Gemini helped a 13-year-old plan her suicide intensifies scrutiny
Daniel Edelson, New York

Three of America's largest public school systems are threatening to take a step that would have seemed almost unthinkable until recently:
 stop using Google 
as their students' search engine. New York, Los Angeles and Chicago are considering switching to competitors after the tech giant announced that schools would not be able to use Google Search without also enabling 
AI-generated answers.
Google announced that starting in early 2027, students would no longer have access to Google Search or its AI tools through their school accounts unless school administrators explicitly enabled them.

But Google left out one option: traditional search without AI-generated summaries or an AI Mode that lets users interact with a chatbot. Schools that object to these features will have to give up Google Search on student accounts altogether, though they can continue using the company's other services.
“If Google doesn’t reconsider their position, we will explore other search options,” Kamar H. Samuels, the chancellor of New York City schools, said in a statement. “We refuse to accept the false binary Google has laid out.”
In September, New York City, home to the nation's largest public school system, announced a one-year ban on the use of generative AI by students in kindergarten through eighth grade, affecting roughly 600,000 children.
Los Angeles has also banned students from using generative AI for the school year and has already taken steps to block Google's AI-generated summaries. Chicago, which has the nation's fourth-largest public school system, has warned that it will consider alternative search engines if Google refuses to offer an AI-free option.
Schools have other options
Among the alternatives being considered are Microsoft's Bing and DuckDuckGo, a search engine known for its focus on privacy. DuckDuckGo already offers an AI-free version at noai.duckduckgo.com and allows schools to make it the default search engine on devices they manage.
Still, switching search engines is one thing. Moving away from Google altogether is another. Chromebooks, Gmail, Google Docs and Google Classroom have become fixtures in American schools. While switching search engines may be easy enough for students, it poses a much bigger challenge for IT teams managing thousands of devices and accounts.

At the heart of the dispute is a broader question: What does it mean to learn?
Teachers say students are increasingly copying answers that appear at the top of search results rather than reading original sources, comparing information and working through problems themselves.
And cheating is only part of the concern. AI-generated summaries can be wrong, presenting false information with seeming confidence. They also risk depriving children of opportunities to develop basic fact-checking skills. Other educators, however, warn that keeping AI out of classrooms entirely could leave students unprepared for tools they will inevitably encounter in higher education and the workplace.
The backlash has already spilled beyond school walls. Parents and teachers have staged demonstrations outside Google offices in Boston, Chicago, New York and other cities. At least 11 newly elected Chicago school board members have also signed a pledge supporting a three-year moratorium on bringing generative AI into schools.
Did Gemini help a teenager plan her suicide?
The debate has taken on added urgency following a lawsuit filed this month against Google by the parents of a 13-year-old girl from North Carolina. The parents allege that their daughter spent months discussing her mental health struggles with Gemini on a school-issued Chromebook and that the chatbot helped her plan her suicide.
According to the lawsuit, the school had blocked access to Gemini's standalone chatbot, but the girl was still able to reach it through the AI Mode built into Google Search.
Google said it was reviewing the allegations and pointed to safeguards designed to direct young people in distress toward appropriate help. The company argues that its new policy actually gives schools more control by allowing administrators to decide which services to enable and which age groups should have access to them.

Google is also developing a "Guided Learning" mode designed to walk students through problems step by step instead of simply giving them the answers.
"We hear parents and teachers' concerns about students using AI to shortcut learning, and we share the same goal: help students learn," Google spokesperson Laura Breen said. However, the company has refused to offer a version of Google Search without AI features.
For now, school districts are trying to find their own workarounds. New York City has introduced filters to block AI features, but parents have discovered that students can still access AI Mode from home through their school accounts.
In a rural Missouri district serving about 2,000 students, officials have turned to third-party software to block AI-generated summaries and answers.
Chris Warden, technology director at the Central R-3 School District in Missouri, explained the reasoning: "We need to be able to control our own pace," he said, "not have Google tell us what the pace of AI adoption is going to be."

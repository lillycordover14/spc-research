Judge halts Google data center construction in Botetourt County, orders public hearing&nbsp;&nbsp;Cardinal News

[page text]
Construction at the Google data center project in Botetourt County must stop, a circuit judge ordered Monday, after ruling that the county’s board of supervisors violated Virginia’s Freedom of Information Act when it approved a key agreement with the tech giant.

All actions the county has taken since the June 24, 2025, meeting that greenlit the project are rescinded, and the county must hold a public hearing on the matter that “conforms to” the state law regarding open meetings and open records, substitute Judge Michael Doucette wrote.

In other words, county residents who didn’t have the opportunity to weigh in on this issue may speak out before the board once again votes.

Robert Dallas filed the suit on Nov. 2, alleging that the county’s board of supervisors did not disclose on its published agenda that it would be discussing in closed session a performance agreement with Google’s developer, Helio Capital LLC, or a resolution to enact the agreement.

After the board returned to public session, it voted 5-0, with no discussion, to approve the agreement, but only after it had added the item to its agenda during the meeting, Dallas alleged. It only publicized what it had voted on hours later.

A lawyer for the county did not dispute those facts but argued that the county did enough to satisfy state law.

During arguments in Botetourt County Circuit Court on Nov. 10, Julian Harf — representing the county, the board and the economic development authority — “stated on a number of occasions that the alleged violations … were ‘technical violations’ and on one occasion stated that ‘magic words’ were not used,” Doucette wrote to parties in the case.

“Such concessions are precisely the point” that Dallas raised, the judge added. The state’s Freedom of Information Act “is a technical and strictly procedural act adopted by the General Assembly. ‘Magic words’ must be used and ‘technical violations’ are not excused.”

A board failing to identify what it is voting on is a clear violation, said Megan Rhyne, executive director of the Williamsburg-based Virginia Coalition for Open Government.

“FOIA is written in a way that you’re supposed to at least let the public know what you’re voting on, and so I was really heartened by that part of the ruling, because it is an acknowledgment that the public gets to know what these final votes are that their public body is taking and what it impacts.”

Dallas’ legal team, led by Roanoke-based attorney John Fishwick, said in a prepared statement that it is pleased with the decision.

“We believe this result sends a clear message to public bodies throughout the commonwealth, that the rule of law and the public’s right to witness and participate in government must be respected,” the statement said.

Fishwick said in an email that the team would not comment beyond its press statement.

Botetourt County spokesman Brent Watts said in an email to media that county leaders would review that ruling with staff and the board and will not do interviews but will “share updates as appropriate” on the matter, which remains in litigation.

“The county respects the legal process and appreciates the attention the court gave to hearing this matter,” said a statement that Watts emailed. “The county … is evaluating its options going forward.”

Work continued at the Botetourt Center at Greenfield site at least 40 minutes after the county issued its 3:36 p.m. statement.

Opposition to the data center grew quickly

The performance agreement vote was one of multiple agenda items added on during the June 24, 2025, meeting, with no prior notice. 

The supervisors also announced that day a reworked agreement with the Western Virginia Water Authority, related to the Google project.

Soon after, a groundswell of opposition developed, with multiple speakers in subsequent meetings decrying what they said was the board’s secrecy, the nondisclosure agreements that the supervisors signed, and concerns about the data center’s future electricity and water use, set against the backdrop of a drought that has affected a key regional water source, Carvins Cove Reservoir.

An activist group, Southwest Virginia Data Center Transparency Alliance, quickly coalesced, with its membership making its presence felt at Botetourt County supervisors meetings as well as water authority meetings. 

Alliance member Julie Bivins, in a text message exchange on Monday, said that the ruling is “a major win” for Virginia communities facing the prospects of “back-door deals in local government.”

“At a time when environmental protections are being stripped away, this case shows how important FOIA procedures are in keeping an open and transparent government,” Bivins said. “It’s really sad that we have to force our government officials to allow the public to even have accurate and timely information on massive projects like this that have significant community impacts.”

The alliance, on its website, credited a member of Dallas’ legal team, Tim Dooley, with developing the case.

“Botetourt County citizens have always known that this process wasn’t done ethically or correctly,” the alliance’s Kathryn Hatam said in 
a news release on the website
. “We just needed Tim Dooley to find the specific statutes.”

 

Dooley, in the alliance news release, said that he hoped it is a step toward transparency and accountability from public officials in Botetourt County and the Roanoke Valley.

“This is, however, only one aspect of the clandestine dealings between Google, municipal governments, and economic development entities, which demand further scrutiny,” Dooley said in the release.

In a joint statement, Virginia General Assembly members Sen. David Suetterlein and Del. Joe McNamara, both R-Roanoke County, said: “A local government’s zeal to sprint through a major decision cannot supersede the transparency and public notice required under the Virginia Freedom of Information Act. Today’s ruling should serve as an important reminder to every city and county across the commonwealth.”

No questions, concerns or discussion

Fishwick argued in earlier court hearings that the board held a lengthy closed session but only a short open hearing for the vote and no opportunity for public comment. The board agreed behind closed doors to approve a resolution authorizing County Administrator Gary Larrowe to sign the performance agreement with Google, Fishwick said. The board had signed nondisclosure agreements, he said, and a video he played for the court of the 2025 meeting showed Larrowe saying that the deal was 18 months in the making.

Doucette, in his Monday ruling, agreed that “circumstantial evidence convincingly proved” a violation of the Freedom of Information Act. 

“After a closed meeting lasting well over an hour, the board of supervisors reconvened in open session,” Doucette wrote.

Almost immediately, the board’s then-chairwoman, Amy White, asked the board to address “walk-on item number one,” the performance agreement resolution, the judge added. No one on the board raised questions or concerns. A motion to adopt and a second followed; with no discussion, the board voted 5-0 to approve it.

Doucette found further violation in the board’s failure to follow the act’s legal language, which requires that a governmental body, after reconvening in an open meeting and taking a vote on a resolution, “shall have its substance reasonably identified in the open meeting.” 

The board did not disclose details until much later during the nearly day-long meeting, after a recess during which several local public officials entered the meeting hall.

Doucette found the respondents made a “tortured reading of the statute” in claiming that a “self-congratulation ceremony” held three hours after the vote, and after a late recess of more than 20 minutes, was satisfactory. It failed to meet the requirements that day.

“In no way was the substance of the resolution reasonably identified to the public by the words ‘walk-on item number one for today’ when the board took its vote,” he wrote.

With that omission, the board’s actions that day “never became effective” and were void from the start, Doucette wrote, citing a 2023 case in which the Virginia Supreme Court voided Fairfax County’s revamped zoning ordinance. The Fairfax board, in an online meeting during the early COVID-19 era, “prevented the public from participating” under the act, potentially limiting “public participation and input into the process,” 
the high court ruled
.

“That’s the first time that we had seen an action voided by a violation, and what it does mean is that you do have to go back and do it right,” said Rhyne, of the open government coalition. “The outcome may ultimately be the same. They may take the exact same votes to push this project forward. But this ruling makes it clear that you can’t skip over the open-meetings process on your way to making big and crucial decisions.”

Changing of the guard on the board

The board’s dynamic has changed since the unanimous vote in June 2025. White and Supervisor Steve Clinton are no longer on the board. Supervisors Mac Scothorn, Walter Michael and Brandon Nicely remain, with new members Linda Rothman and Tim Snyder, both elected about four months later.

Testimony in the Nov. 10 hearing, and the performance agreement itself, showed that Google has transferred $2.5 million of a promised $4 million to the county, helping pay for ambulances, fire trucks, heart monitors, body cameras and more.

Doucette enjoined any further action related to the agreement.

“The board’s remedy is simple … conduct a future public hearing on this issue that conforms to the VFOIA,” he wrote.

Fishwick had asked Doucette to order the board to follow the law in the future, but he declined, writing that “it is given that if it does not, the result will be the same as today.” The suit asked for attorney fees and costs, and Doucette took that under advisement pending further evidence “on that one specific issue.”

Ultimately, the board will have to revisit the performance agreement, which set in motion 
Google’s $14 million deal with Botetourt County for 312 acres at Greenfield
. Google planned a campus including three data centers, three substations, an office building, access roads, utilities, parking areas and stormwater management facilities, according to a March 10 Army Corps of Engineers notice soliciting public comment on the project.

Google would pay a property tax rate of 70 cents per $100 of assessed value, same as any other landowner in the county, Larrowe has said.

The performance agreement
, which the county posted online, includes Google’s commitment to make capital investments of at least $1 billion to develop each data center on the property.

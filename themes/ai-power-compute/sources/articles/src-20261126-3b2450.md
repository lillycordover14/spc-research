A 26-year-old Google worker earns $323K but spends under $20K a year — his plan for financial freedom
 
 
 
  Victoria Vesovski 
 
 
Thu, November 26, 2026 at 10:00 AM CST
 
6 min read
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
GOOGN
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Charles Broomfield/YouTube
 
 
 
Charles Broomfield lives with roommates, 
walks most places
 and eats free meals at work. A $7 beer at a baseball game is a splurge for him. He's also on track to make about $323,000 this year.
 
 
The 26-year-old Google AI security analyst expects about $48,000 of that income to come from his YouTube channel and career-coaching business. He claims he spends less than $20,000 a year on living expenses and expects to reach his $750,000 investment goal within the next year. That's the number he's set for financial independence.

"I guess something else that probably does make me a little bit unique is I really don't feel joy when I buy things," 
he told CNBC
. "I feel like I have what I need."
 
 
But how much is worth giving up to reach financial independence sooner?
 
 
Keeping his spending low
 
 
Broomfield got into the habit of saving early. Growing up, he and his siblings set aside half of everything they earned. They could spend the other half or save it and earn interest. He later picked up much of what he knew about personal finance from Mr. Money Mustache, a blogger who writes about living on less and saving a big share of your income. That's how Broomfield discovered FIRE, which stands for 
financial independence, retire early
.
 
 
 
 
 
"All I had to do was live super frugally and I could actually reach financial freedom by the time I was 30 and go do whatever actually made me happy in life," he said.
 
 
Broomfield graduated with about $50,000 in savings. Scholarships covered some of his tuition, and his parents paid the rest. When he started tracking his progress toward early retirement, he put his starting balance at $0 so the total would reflect what he earned after college.
 
 
He even ran the numbers on living in a van to save on rent. A year in the van, he figured, would move up his retirement by about a month.
 
 
"It just [didn't] seem really worth it to go to that extreme," Broomfield explained.
 
 
That's the kind of math Trent Von Ahsen, certified financial planner and managing partner at 
Cedar Point Capital Partners
, says is worth doing before giving something up.
 
 
"When evaluating whether cutting at a particular expense makes sense, I would encourage people to quantify what that sacrifice is actually buying them," Von Ahsen told Moneywise.

He suggests looking at the biggest bills first. Housing and transportation together made up about half of average 
U.S. household spending in 2024
, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

How he makes $20,000 a year work
 
 
Instead of living in a van, Broomfield shares a Washington, D.C., apartment with four roommates. In August, he paid $1,098 for rent and utilities, and his food bill came to just $28. He says that's low even by his standards. In a typical month, Broomfield spends closer to $70 on food, thanks in large part to free meals at Google. For comparison, one-person households in the U.S. spent about $282 a month on groceries in 2025, according to the 
Bureau of Labor Statistics
, and the 
price of food is rising
.
 
 
Google also covers his commute by public transportation, and he gets around without a car. But one expense he's happy to keep is his $229-a-month kickboxing membership. He could work out for free at Google, but he wants to compete while he's young and likes spending time with the other fighters at his gym.
 
 
 
 
 
"There are certainly expenses and opportunities in life that are time sensitive. Having substantially more wealth twenty years from now doesn't necessarily allow you to recreate those experiences," Von Ahsen told Moneywise.
 
 
Even with the kickboxing membership, Broomfield's everyday expenses came to $1,477 in August. That month, he also put $700 toward repaying tuition to a former employer and nearly $18,000 into his 401(k) and brokerage account.
 
 
What he wants to build next
 
 
Broomfield based his savings goal on the 4% rule for withdrawing from retirement savings. If he was to actually retire at 30 with a $750,000 portfolio, that works out to about $30,000 in withdrawals in the first year. According to the USAA's 
retirement calculator
, at that level, his savings would last him 27 years, assuming he wants a $30,000 income after taxes. That would only take him to age 57.
 
 
On the other hand, if he were to leave that $750,000 invested, even very conservatively, it would compound to a very comfortable retirement nest egg by the time he's 65. He wouldn't need to contribute any more to retirement, and could enjoy life on his generous Google income without worry.
 
 
 
 
 
Broomfield also has income from the YouTube channel he started to document his progress toward early retirement. It's since grown into a business that covers his living expenses most months.
 
 
Von Ahsen says the money could give Broomfield room to try something new. How much room depends on what it costs to start a business, what he'll pay for health insurance, and how long it takes the business to turn a profit.
 
 
"Frugality is a means to an end, not an end to itself," Von Ahsen said.
 
 
As for now, Broomfield is still working at Google and hopes to eventually start a company of his own, possibly in AI security.
 
 
"Having complete ownership and complete buy-in to something that's my own. That sounds really exciting to me," he told CNBC.

Google's purge of more than 19,000 Indonesian-linked YouTube channels following an investigation into "coordinated influence operations" in the country is one of the largest mass account terminations on the platform in years.
But the take-downs have prompted a dispute with an Indonesian government ministry, which is pushing for transparency from the tech giant and claiming the purge could be "misinterpreted".
In Google's quarterly security bulletin, released in late October, the company said it terminated accounts from July to September for being part of a campaign that was sharing content "supportive of the Indonesian government".
Most of the channels were also accused of being "critical of critics of the Indonesian government".
Ismail Fahmi, who founded social media analytics software Drone Emprit, said the scale of the terminations had "never seen before".
"[About] 19,000 or so YouTube channels [is] quite a lot and that's really half of everything Google took down in that quarter," he said.
"In August alone, 10,000 channels were removed. I've never seen this before.
"We cannot independently verify this finding, [but] I would treat this as an important signal from one platform."
It is the third-highest quarterly removal of accounts since 2023. The other two instances related to accounts accused of being linked to influence operations in China.
Why were the accounts removed?
When approached by the ABC, a Google spokesperson said the company "routinely disrupts coordinated influence operations as part of our global efforts to protect our platforms, including YouTube".
"We consistently enforce our policies regardless of the political viewpoints expressed and remain committed to transparency through our public quarterly bulletins," the company said.
But Indonesia's Ministry of Communication and Digital, known as Komdigi, believes there needs to be more detail provided about why the accounts were removed.
It summoned representatives from Google Indonesia and YouTube Southeast Asia for a meeting last week to explain what had happened.
"An explanation is crucial to ensure this is no misattribution or misinterpretation of Google's findings," Komdigi said in a statement.
Komdigi's Mediodecci Lustarini said the tech giant had been asked to ​provide the methodology and the review process behind its decision.
"We asked Google to provide written supporting information, along with examples of parameters and methods for detecting and taking action against accounts that were subsequently terminated," she said.
The ministry also said at the meeting that "representatives from Google Indonesia and YouTube Southeast Asia acknowledged the … security bulletin did not provide sufficient supporting explanations, making it vulnerable to misinterpretation".
Komdigi said local Google representatives had indicated they would seek clarification with the global Google team.
Why is this a big deal?
Yovantra Arief from Remotivi, an Indonesian media monitoring institute, said the issue was whether the channels were part of coordinated efforts to manipulate public conversation.
"The real issue isn't whether they're pro-government or anti-government. It's the coordinated behaviour," he said.
The number of accounts taken down relating to Indonesia in the third quarter of this year dwarfs the number of accounts accused of supporting other governments, like China, India and Russia.
Google's decision has already reignited conversation in Indonesia about online content moderation and accountability.
Dr Fahmi said it was difficult to analyse what had occurred because of limited available Google data, but the creation of thousands of accounts reflected trends in social media and artificial intelligence within Indonesia.
"There is a kind of coordinated and industrial scale in making content and channels on YouTube," he said.
"Following the latest developments in AI, people can use AI agents to clip videos easily and post them to channels like YouTube.
"People can provide services to anybody, it can be pro- or anti-government — it doesn't matter as long as they're willing to pay."
Komdigi's rebuke of Google comes at a time it is facing criticism of its own, over allegations it has requested social media platforms to remove or restrict content that was critical of the government.
Yovantra Arief said civil society groups had long sought greater transparency regarding government-ordered content removals, clearer definitions of rules governing online speech, and stronger accountability when moderation decisions affect freedom of expression.
"The public is asking for transparency, clear definitions and accountability," he said.
"Those requests are often ignored … so there's a degree of irony when the government demands those same standards from the platforms."
What is the next step?
Remotivi and another media monitoring group, Southeast Asia Freedom of Expression Network (SAFENet), found 94 social media posts had been removed or restricted following requests by Komdigi.
The moderated posts, which were sent to the two groups through a public complaints channel, included criticism of the president, the government, and its handling of forest fires.
"This is probably just the tip of the iceberg," Yovantra Arief said.
In a response to the ABC, Komdigi's Mediodecci Lustarini said "we also take the findings of Remotivi and SAFEnet seriously".
"We are ready to match the links, times of incidents, and notifications received by users against our handling records," she said.
"If errors are found in Komdigi's actions, we are ready to correct them and coordinate with the platforms to restore access."
Meanwhile, Komdigi has used the moment to press Google and YouTube to talk about other types of accounts.
"With the detection capabilities that have been demonstrated, we are asking Google and YouTube to also implement them to detect and take action against networks of accounts that coordinately promote online gambling or distribute pornographic content," Mediodecci said.

Abstract
Data centers are reversing two decades of flat U.S. electricity demand. We estimate their
effect on wholesale electricity markets using an hourly, unit-level dispatch model in which
23 U.S. regional markets trade over capacity-constrained interfaces. Data centers raised
wholesale prices by 6.9 percent nationally by 2025 and by 15.0 percent in northern
Virginia; under our preferred scenario, the national effect reaches 12.3 percent by 2028.
Trade spreads these effects to regions hosting few data centers. Roughly half the increase
in generator payments is a transfer to inframarginal generators. We derive sufficient
statistics for when load growth lowers retail rates.

Introduction
After nearly two decades of flat electricity consumption in the United States, growth in elec-

tricity demand has re-emerged as a defining feature of power markets, in large part due to the rapid
expansion of data centers for Artificial Intelligence (AI). Unlike traditional sources of demand
growth, such as households, commercial buildings, or gradual electrification, data centers arrive
in large, discrete increments, cluster geographically, and can be constructed quickly relative to the
pace of generation and transmission investment. Concerns over affordability have contributed to
opposition to data centers across the political spectrum with states as politically diverse as Texas
and New York calling for pauses or moratoriums on new data center construction. However, a
growing body of work has argued that demand growth can, in fact, lower electricity bills for residential customers.
The economics of electricity markets suggests that the effect of a large increase in demand
for electricity on prices is ambiguous. Consumer retail prices reflect both the short-run marginal
cost of generating an additional kWh of electricity, as well as the costs of transmission lines, substations, distribution network infrastructure, and a variety of other costs traditionally considered to
be fixed, at least in the short run. If additional demand can be met with existing grid infrastructure
and no additional fixed costs, total fixed costs are spread over a greater number of kWh, decreasing
the average fixed cost and putting downward pressure on customer rates.1 On the other hand, an
upward sloping short-run marginal cost curve for electricity means that additional demand will be
met by higher cost power plants on the margin, putting upward pressure on wholesale prices and
electricity rates. The ultimate effect of electricity load growth on retail prices thus depends on the
relative magnitude of these effects and their respective weight in the end-users bill.
In this paper, we quantify the short-run effect of existing and proposed data center electricity
demand on electricity prices in the US. To do so, we first use a detailed structural dispatch model

Our structural approach is motivated by two characteristics of electricity markets which make
estimating the effects of load growth on prices challenging. Electricity grids are highly interconnected nodal networks, meaning demand shocks in one location are likely to have spillover effects

the availability of idle generation that would serve load in connected regions, as well as overstate
the effect of data center arrivals by not dispatching cheaper idle foreign generation. Relatedly,
the interconnected nature of the electricity grid also makes it difficult to find suitable controls for
the use of reduced-form causal inference methods. Furthermore, because short-run supply curves
are highly non-linear and convex, even well identified estimates based on historical data may not
support credible extrapolation outside the study period and geography. Our model reproduces observed market outcomes closely, matching both unit-level generation and market-level prices with
high correlations and observed regional trade flows.

We contribute to a growing literature on the economic and environmental consequences of
electricity-intensive computing infrastructure. As projections for data center growth have ballooned, a number of technical reports have estimated the energy demands associated with this construction boom (Shehabi et al. 2024; Green et al. 2024). However, there have been relatively few
academic studies on the economic impacts of these changes. Bogmans et al. (2025) provide quantitative projections of the effect of AI-driven data center growth on electricity prices and carbon
emissions at economy-wide scale using a multi-country computational general equilibrium model.
Similarly, recent work has begun to document localized wholesale price effects of data center expansion in major corridors (Mamkhezri et al. 2025; Feher et al. 2025), while related research on
cryptocurrency mining, an extreme form of high-utilization computing load, finds increases in retail electricity prices with only partial fiscal offsets (Benetton et al. 2023). Muller (2026) measures
impact of data centers on local pollution and greenhouse gas emissions using regional average
emissions rates of power generation. Other work by Gargano and Giacoletti (2025) studies the

political economy of attracting data centers, showing that state incentives meaningfully shift data

In contrast to much of this earlier work, our approach constructs a detailed model of electricity
supply at a precise geographic and temporal scale. This approach allows us to appropriately capture
the non-linear price response to changes in demand that occurs when particular markets or times
are constrained. In the most closely related work to our own, Wade et al. (2025) use a capacity
expansion model to forecast long run effects of rising demand from data centers and crypto mining
in U.S. power markets. However, they lack detailed geographic and temporal data on data centers
and instead assume fixed growth rates in compute from demand. In contrast, we use detailed
site-specific data on existing and proposed data centers to model the wholesale electricity market
impacts at a detailed geographic and temporal level through 2028.
More broadly, our analysis underscores that the consequences of demand growth depend not
only on how much electricity is consumed, but on where and when that consumption occurs and
on the availability of marginal supply. Recognizing this point, a growing literature has emphasized
that if demand is able to flexibly avoid congested hours and locations, impacts on wholesale markets can be mitigated (Norris et al. 2025). This literature has mostly focused on temporal flexibility
and how this flexibility may impact which generation comes online and emissions profiles (Knittel
et al. 2025; Ross and Ewing 2026). In contrast, our analysis of siting decisions documents how geographic flexibility in where compute occurs can provide system benefits. As data centers continue
to expand, understanding these interactions will be essential for infrastructure planning, market design, and climate policy. By quantifying both realized impacts and plausible future trajectories, this
paper provides an empirical foundation for those discussions.

Institutional Background

Electricity Markets
The US “grid” is comprised of three different grids, called interconnections: one in the eastern
US, one in the western US, and one comprising most of Texas (ERCOT). These interconnections
are largely independent from each other with very limited direct current (DC) transmission between
them. Within an interconnection, electricity can be thought of as a commodity good subject to
significant physical constraints. Grid operators must ensure the stability of the grid by matching
supply and demand at every moment in time, accounting for the constraints of the transmission
and distribution network and other grid infrastructure in order to avoid system-wide blackouts.
New electricity load can be met by power plants elsewhere in the interconnection if adequate
transmission capacity exists and the network is not congested. However, transmission is often at
capacity, effectively fragmenting different geographies into separate, isolated sub-regions. The
cost of serving new load is therefore geographically specific and dependent on whether demand
occurs during periods when transmission infrastructure is congested or has excess capacity.
The market structures for electricity vary between regions. Historically, electricity production,
transmission, distribution, and retailing were vertically integrated as the high fixed costs inherent
in building out the grid led the industry to exhibit natural monopoly characteristics. A single
utility owned power plants, transmission and distribution lines, and was responsible for ensuring
that supply was equal to demand at each point in time, dictating production decisions for their
portfolio of power plants. Starting in the 1990s, roughly two thirds of the contiguous US began
to deregulate the generation sector, eventually organizing into one of seven wholesale electricity
markets, where a separate Independent System Operator (ISO) or Regional Transmission Organization (RTO) manages a wholesale market to procure energy, capacity, and ancillary services.4,5

The physical requirements that supply equals demand at each moment in time and the physical
constraints of grid infrastructure influence the shape of short run electricity supply curves. The
vast majority of electricity is produced from either natural gas, coal, nuclear, hydropower, wind,
or solar. Individual power plants are limited by a maximum capacity they can produce at any
time and marginal costs of production can vary dramatically between technology types. These
characteristics lead to short-run supply curves that are typically upward sloping, convex, kinked
at boundaries between technologies, and nearly vertical as the system approaches full capacity.
This convexity implies that there is not a single supply elasticity, and price and cost increases from
additional load can vary greatly depending on system constraints.

Data Center Electricity Demand
After decades of relatively flat demand growth, US electricity demand has begun to grow
again, driven in large part by increased demand from data centers. While data centers have been
present for decades, their increased prevalence and scale increased drastically with the introduction
of AI models. Estimates of data center usage show that their electricity demand increased from
1.9% in 2018 to 4.4% of US total electricity demand by 2023. This number could increase further
to 12% by 2028 (Shehabi et al. 2024).

3.3.1

Ex Post Periods

We construct each subregion’s residual demand, the load that is served by dispatchable thermal generation and inter-regional trade, by subtracting the observed supply of inframarginal generation from observed total demand. Our approach differs from Hausman (2025) and Ham et al.
(2025), who use the observed thermal supply in a subregion as the implied residual demand. This
method holds trade across subregions fixed at the observed level, even in the counterfactuals. Instead, we develop residual demand directly from demand data and model flexible interregional

trade using interfaces, which allows trade flows to vary across counterfactuals.18 To get hourly
subregion electricity demand, we source data from the EIA’s Form 930, which reports demand
at the sub-balancing authority level. Almost all sub-balancing authorities uniquely match to an
eGRID subregion, allowing us to aggregate to an hourly subregion demand.19 From hourly demand, we subtract hourly inframarginal generation, which includes solar, wind, hydro, nuclear,
geothermal, biomass, and cogeneration plants, using data from EIA-923 and EIA-930.20
Three further adjustments ensure we model subregions on a common, physically consistent
basis. First, we shift all subregion data to a common Eastern time so that each hour refers to
the same physical hour everywhere. Second, using hourly interchange data from EIA-930, we
subtract observed hourly net imports from Canada from adjacent subregions, treating cross-border
flows as exogenous. This ensures that our residual reflects only generation that can be met within
the continental U.S. Lastly, to handle the rapid expansion of grid-scale storage capacity in the latter
part of our sample, particularly in the CAISO and ERCOT footprints, we model each subregion
as having a single aggregate battery whose charge and discharge are chosen jointly with thermal
output and interregional trade.21

We obtain data on the characteristics of existing and planned data centers from S&P Global

For our model of electricity markets, we require a measure of hourly data center power demand. There are, however, several sources of uncertainty in the power demand for data centers.
First, the DCKB does not report net IT capacity for planned data centers, which we impute from
their reported utility capacity.23 Second, actual utilization factors are unknown. Although many
data centers publicly state their intent for 100 percent utilization, industry reports show real-world
utilization is much lower and varies by data center type. Third, some data centers have installed onsite “behind-the-meter” main or backup power generation. Information on the size and utilization
of these sources is often not publicly reported. In our utilization scenarios, we are agnostic about
whether reduced utilization is due to lack of computing needs or a switch to on-site generation, as
the two have the same impact on the electricity grid.

the counterfactual involves a large demand shock that is concentrated in particular subregions.
Holding trade fixed means that all data center demand must be met by generators within the same
subregion. This would overstate the price effect in subregions, such as SRVC-N, that experience
large demand shocks but which have large import capabilities with neighboring regions. It would
also miss the effect of foregone imports in subregions, such as CAMX, where much of the price
increase comes from foregone trade with neighboring regions using their cheaper generation to
serve their own data center demand. On the other hand, aggregating subregions into larger markets
with no limits on internal trade understates local price effects when interfaces would be binding
between regions. Our approach falls between these two extremes.
Ramping rates and ramping costs matter for a related reason. Data center demand that follows
a daily load shape, as in our Mid-Range-Peak scenario, must be met by units that can adjust their
output within the day, which steepens the effective supply curve in shoulder hours relative to a
static merit order.

5.1

Competitive Wholesale Price Effects
We begin our discussion of results by estimating the change in the wholesale electricity price

due to data center build-out from 2021–2028 using our dispatch model. While cost of generation
is a relevant measure for the areas of the country covered by vertically integrated utilities, for the
majority of the country covered by competitive wholesale electricity markets, the market price is
relevant both for generator profits and for the price paid by utilities and load serving entities to
procure electricity for retail customers.

6.1

No Trade Flows
In Section 4.3, we showed that our model builds upon a class of dispatch models which do

not account for cross region transmission and replicates observed trade flows accurately. In this
exercise, we document the importance of accounting for trade flows in the dispatch model. To do
this, we remove the trading mechanism between regions and use the hourly observed net thermal
generation as the implied residual demand in the region as in Hausman (2025); Ham et al. (2025).
This implicitly fixes the trade from other regions at the observed level. For the no data center
counterfactual, the hourly regional demand is lowered by the assumed data center usage and the
model is re-solved, forcing all data center demand to be met by within region generators.39
In Appendix Table A2, we show that both versions of the dispatch model recover similar
nationwide average price effects, percentage changes, and emissions outcomes from data centers
during the ex post period. Due to the lack of trade, the no-trade versions has more hours with
unserved energy where the regional demand is greater than supply.
However, in Figure 10, we plot the difference in the regional average hourly price effect
from data center growth over 2024–2025 between the two model versions. As expected, regions
that experience large demand growth from data centers, such as Virginia (SRVC-N), the upper
midwest (MROW), and the northwest (NWPP), experience much larger price effects in the version
where trade is held fixed as more expensive local generation is required to meet the higher demand.
Also, a region like California, CAMX, who has trade partners experiencing large demand increases
has their price effect understated in the no-trade version since the loss of foregone trade is not
accurately captured.

Geographic Compute Flexibility as Virtual Transmission
Finally, we explore the extent to which data center load flexibility can mitigate the wholesale

market effects discussed in Section 5. If computational workloads can be curtailed, deferred, or
spatially reallocated across geographically dispersed data centers, then compute flexibility can act,
at the margin, like a form of virtual transmission. This shifts effective electricity demand away
from stressed regions and toward regions with lower prices and more spare capacity.
In principle, increased flexibility can improve grid reliability and lower wholesale prices, but
it requires sufficient excess and geographically diverse compute capacity, network capability, and
tolerance for latency and other operational constraints. In practice, many data center operators
market their services on the basis of extremely high reliability, making frequent and unplanned
curtailment costly. In this sense, spatial compute flexibility may substitute for transmission only if
firms are willing to maintain some level of costly overbuild of data center capacity across multiple
regions or institute some prioritization of tasks.
To illustrate the potential magnitude of this margin, we compare observed data center siting
against the geographic allocation of compute chosen by a social planner minimizing short-run
electricity operating costs.45 This counterfactual abstracts from latency, construction costs, labor
availability, interconnection constraints, and other factors that shape actual siting decisions. We
solve a national dispatch problem, allocating excess thermal capacity that remains idle in the nodata-center scenario to serve compute. The resulting hourly generation pattern identifies where
compute would be served if short-run (hourly) electricity operating cost were the sole objective
and there was sufficient data center capacity in each region.

Retail Price Effects
Section 5 shows that data center electricity demand has had a significant impact on wholesale

electricity prices and that this impact could be even larger in the near future. However, much of the
public debate centers on the retail rates that residential customers actually pay. Industry groups and
a number of recent papers and reports have argued that additional load growth from data centers
will actually cause retail prices to decrease due to the fixed-cost-spreading effect. However, as
we show below, the benefits from the spreading of fixed costs will be offset by the increase in the
short-run marginal cost of producing electricity discussed in Section 5.
The effect of new data center load on customers’ bills is theoretically ambiguous. Under
50

average-cost allocation, total fixed costs are divided by the total kWh of electricity sold and recovered through retail rates. An increase in electricity consumption with no corresponding increase
in fixed expenditures reduces the average fixed cost per unit of electricity sold. This fixed-costsspreading could lower the burden borne by existing customers on the grid.46 This outcome is
especially relevant if data centers locate in regions where there is pre-existing spare transmission
and distribution capacity or flexibly decrease their consumption during periods of peak load (Norris
et al. 2025).
We present a simple framework in Appendix G to quantify the relative magnitudes of the cost
increases from higher variable costs and the cost decreases from the spreading of fixed costs. To
do so, we invoke Assumption 1, assuming that additional electricity demand will only increase the
total system costs through the effect on short-run marginal costs modeled in Section 5.
Assumption 1. Additional electricity demand only increases total system costs through the increase in short-run marginal generation costs. All fixed system costs remain fixed.
This assumption is unlikely to hold in reality. The scale, concentration, and clustering of proposed
data center projects is likely to require substantial new expenditures on interconnection, transmission, substations, and local distribution networks. In that case, “fixed costs” will not actually be
fixed, as large system wide infrastructure costs are needed to connect additional load.47 We therefore consider the analysis in this section as likely to understate the retail price effects from data
center load, showing a scenario where additional load is added to the system without requiring any
system upgrades.48
If fixed costs truly remain fixed, we can determine the share of the benefits from fixed cost
spreading that are offset by the increase in the short-run marginal cost of producing electricity. We

Conclusion
This paper provides one of the first empirical assessments of how expanding data center load

affects wholesale electricity market outcomes in the United States. Using an hourly least-cost dispatch model built from unit-level data covering the continental U.S., we estimate how data center
load changes generation costs, competitive benchmark wholesale prices, and carbon emissions in
both the recent historical period and a set of forward-looking counterfactuals.
57

Three main findings emerge. First, data center expansion has already increased generation
costs, competitive wholesale benchmark prices, and emissions in several regions. These effects
are not uniform across markets and are largest in subregions where data center growth coincides
with relatively tight thermal capacity, such as Virginia and the Northwest. Second, the impacts of
data center load are nonlinear. Because of highly convex short-run supply curves, even moderate
increases in load can generate disproportionately large increases in marginal generation costs when
the system is operating near scarcity. These effects are most pronounced in seasons when outages
or maintenance reduce available capacity. Third, the forward-looking results show that the data
center build-out will have increasing effects on wholesale markets. Uncertainty in how much
proposed data center capacity will actually be built is a main source of uncertainty, but the model
implies meaningful increases in wholesale market pressure across all scenarios.
The broader policy implication of our model is that the economic consequences of data center
expansion depend not only on the size of the increase in demand, but also on where it is located,
when it operates, and how quickly new supply can be built. We contribute to active policy discussions by using our model to show how transmission expansion and interconnection policy, resulting
in broader market integration, can lead to lower nationwide effects, albeit at the expense of consumers in previously shielded subregions. We also highlight that data center flexibility can play a
role in reducing the effects by comparing the observed build-out to an optimal spatial allocation of
compute that prioritizes least-cost computing.

C.2

Ex Post Residual Demand

Solar, wind, and hydro. We map the universe of solar, wind, and hydro units to eGRID subregions and collect unit-level nameplate capacities, primarily from the eGRID facility dataset, with
supplemental data from EIA-860 on plant locations and balancing authorities where gaps exist.
Because eGRID subregions and balancing authorities do not have a one-to-one match, we redistribute observed generation according to the installed generation mix of each subregion. First,
we aggregate each balancing authority’s hourly generation to the fuel-by-month level and allocate
these totals to subregions in proportion to their units’ reported EIA-923 monthly net generation.
For each balancing authority, we compute an hourly capacity factor for each fuel type as the fraction of that month’s total generation observed in each hour of our sample. We assign these capacity
factors to each resource located within the corresponding balancing authority. This ensures that the
sum of the resulting unit-level hourly generation is consistent with the monthly total net generation
from the EIA-930.
Other inframarginal supply. Hourly generation profiles for other inframarginal supply types,
such as nuclear, geothermal, biomass, and cogeneration plants, are relatively flat compared with
the exogenous generation of wind and solar, or with hydro generation, which tends to follow hourly
load. We construct this generation as a monthly residual using the fact that, for each subregion,

Total Net Generation = CEMS Thermal + Solar + Wind + Hydro + Other.

Similar to how we treat renewables, we apportion this generation according to the reported EIA923 monthly net generation totals. This step is particularly important, as different fuel types that
fall into this category (e.g., nuclear and small natural gas units) can have very different capacity
factors. We subtract monthly CEMS totals and monthly renewable totals from total net generation
and spread that residual uniformly across hours within the month.
In later years of our sample, the EIA-930 began reporting storage. Even within balancing

C.3

Data Center Load Profiles and Ramp-Up
There is little visibility into how data centers are operated in practice. We reflect this un-

certainty by constructing four data center demand profiles that allow us to analyze the effect on
wholesale markets across a variety of potential scenarios. We vary both the power capacity (the
maximum power draw at a point in time) and the utilization rate (the percentage of maximum used
in a given hour). In the Full-Capacity scenario, we assume 100% of installed power capacity is
used 100% of the time. For the reasons noted above, this scenario is highly unlikely, but provides
a useful upper bound. We leverage data from Bloomberg/DC Byte, which reports average annual
utilization rates and market share by data center type as reported by industry participants. We
scale data center capacity and utilization by these rates to construct two plausible scenarios. First,
we allocate a percentage of power from hyperscale, cloud, and neo-cloud data centers equal to
the relative market share for training data centers to every hour as these have a 100% utilization

78

rate.53 Next, we apply type-specific utilization rates to the remaining data center capacity. In our
Mid-Range-Constant scenario, we assign this power equally across all hours of the day. In our
Mid-Range-Peak scenario, each data center’s electricity consumption fluctuates around its type’s
utilization rate, in a load-following pattern, which is constructed to mirror the patterns documented
in EPRI (2026). In particular, from 11AM–3PM data centers operate at a percentage above their
utilization rate, depending on their type.54 In the hours from 10PM–4AM data centers operate at
an equivalent percentage below their utilization rate. In shoulder hours, data centers ramp their
demand up and down in increments that are halfway between their peak and overnight demands.
The last source of uncertainty is the rate at which each data center’s power consumption rises
after its stated operation date. This can vary substantially across and within data center types
as construction timelines vary and campuses can scale multiple buildings over time. The DCKB
data allows us to handle within-campus staggered energization, as it contains separate records
for buildings with different operating dates.55 Our ramping assumptions primarily capture within
building differences across data center types. We assume buildings at each campus ramp up to full
capacity at the following rates: Crypto Mines ramp in 3 months, Hyperscale, Cloud, Neo-Cloud,
Wholesale and Reseller take 9 months, Telco and System Integrators take 12 months, and Retail
and Hosting take 24 months. We apply a linear ramp to their operational capacity at the monthly
level until operational capacity equals full capacity.

C.4

Trade Interfaces
To construct eGRID trade interfaces we take several steps, depending on the overlap between

ITCS and eGRId regions. First, where eGRID boundaries and ITCS boundaries align, we simply
use the Summer 2024 and Winter 2024/2025 capacities for these eGRID interfaces. Shoulder
seasons are assigned the simple average between the summer and winter capacities. For cases
where an ITCS region is fully subsumed by an eGRID region, we assume unlimited transmission
capacity within the eGRID subregion and across ITCS regions.56 When subregions share an ITCS
boundary, we give them the same interface capacity, but cap their aggregate trade capacity at the
ITCS level.
When regions lack a direct mapping to an ITCS planning area, we utilize the EIA’s Form 930
data on interchange between balancing authorities. In these rare cases, such as the mismatch between the AZNM eGRID region and the Front Range and Wasatch Front ITCS planning areas, we
sum the 99th percentile of observed interchange between the balancing authorities contained within
the relevant eGRID subregions to construct our trade interfaces. In cases where two subregions
have multiple interfaces, we take the sum of the interface capacities to construct our interfaces. Finally, we cap each eGRID subregion’s total import capacity at the stated ITCS import capabilities,
summing import capabilities where ITCS regions are subsumed by an eGRID subregion. In total,
we construct 42 interfaces between subregions. We display the resulting interfaces and import
capacities in Figure 1.

In practice, these interface and import constraints act as soft caps that only bind under particularly tight conditions because moving power across interfaces can be costly. As electricity travels
across the transmission network, some of that energy is lost. In addition, intra-regional transmission constraints may prevent the dispatch of thermal generation in one subregion from meeting the
residual demand in another subregion. There are also persistent transaction costs for trading across
the boundaries of adjacent RTOs/ISOs (Mansur and White 2012). Failing to incorporate these
costs will cause our model to overstate the amount of trade between subregions.57 We account for
these frictions with a hurdle rate — a per-MWh charge on flows across interfaces.58

Appendix: Grid-Scale Storage
Along with solar, grid-scale storage capacity is expanding at a rapid pace. From 2021 through

2025 the aggregate U.S. fleet expanded from 4.7 GW of power and 12.5 GWh of energy capacity
to 42.8 GW and 118.8 GWh. Our data from Yes and the EIA projects approximately 99.9 GW
and 302.9 GWh in 2028. Storage is unique in its role as an electricity arbitrageur.61 Unlike other
resources, storage has neither a fuel cost nor an output schedule dictated by intermittent weather
shocks. When a battery chooses to charge and discharge is a dynamic choice, constrained by its
rated power (its upper limit on charging and discharging), it energy capacity (the number of hours
it can sustain a charge or discharge at its rated power), and its round-trip efficiency. Instead of
subtracting observed charge and discharge from each subregion’s residual demand, we represent
the battery fleet of each subregions as a single aggregate battery whose charge and discharge are
chosen jointly with thermal output and interregional trade in the dispatch program described in
Section 4.

Calibrating the Fixed Cost Share
The threshold condition in Appendix G compares the share of the retail bill attributable to

fixed costs, s, with the break-even share s∗ = (1 + d)/(η + 1 + d). The elasticity η and the data
center load share d come from our dispatch model and are defined for eGRID subregions. The
share fixed cost share s must instead be calibrated. To do so, we measure the variable-cost share
of retail rates, set s equal to one minus that share, and aggregate the state measures to subregions.
We measure the variable-cost share of retail revenue separately for wholesale markets and states
without wholesale markets.
States with organized wholesale markets. For the jurisdictions predominately served by an
ISO/RTO(CAISO, ERCOT, ISO-NE, MISO, NYISO, PJM, or SPP), we follow Borenstein and
Bushnell (2022) and use the wholesale energy price as the short-run marginal cost of producing
electricity, and therefore the relevant variable cost measure for retail rates. We match each state
to a pricing-hub (or set of pricing hubs) and measure the average annual wholesale price as the
load-weighted average. In New England and New York the pricing zones coincide with state
boundaries. For states with no pricing point of their own, a nearby location in the same ISO stands
in; for example, the MISO Minnesota Hub is used for Iowa, Wisconsin, and the Dakotas. We then
find the average retail rate for each state using EIA-861 data and dividing total revenue from sales
to ultimate customers by total quantity of sales to ultimate customers. We determine the variablecost share as the wholesale price divided by the retail rate, and the fixed-cost share as one minus
the variable cost share. Finally, we pool estimates across 2021–2024 in order to find a smoother
longer-run estimate.75

 What is an Agent?
 In its simplest form, an agent in the context of artificial intelligence is a system that can
 independently plan and execute tasks, from simple ones to more complex multi-step workflows
 that may invoke various data sources as well as other agents. The key difference between an
 agentic and generative AI is proactivity. Agents act on their own accord to handle workflows and
 interact across systems while generative AI requires commands given through prompts to
 accomplish a task involving direct human interaction. The core competencies that separate
 agents from LLMs, copilots, process automation tools, etc. are autonomy, real-time reasoning,
 and the ability to leverage tools across a variety of systems. Because agents can transcend
 systems, data sets, and organizational structures, this makes them valuable for certain use
 cases including: 1) open-ended tasks that involve several key decisions, 2) complex situations
 that may not have a finite number of steps to completion at the outset, and 3) any situation
 where a system working through tasks will need to be flexible and have the ability to adapt to a
 changing environment.

 Agent Architecture
 At a high level, an AI agent is better classified as a system, not a model, in our opinion. Agentic
 architectures are composed of several modular components that work together to enable goal-
 focused, autonomous behavior versus simple prompt responses.

 Foundational Model

 At the core of an AI agent sits a foundation model or large language model (LLM), ChatGPT,
 Gemini, Claude, etc. which functions as the system’s reasoning engine. The model interprets
 natural language inputs, reasons through instructions, and determines what actions to take next.
 Importantly, the LLM does not execute work, but rather it decides when to retrieve information,
 use tools, or update its memory. Further, the separation between reasoning and execution is
 what allows agents to move beyond static responses and into dynamic workflows.

 Technically, autonomous agents could exist absent of commercial or open source LLMs. One
 could see certain system of record or vertical SaaS companies leverage what effectively could
 be considered small language models or their own purely proprietary reasoning engines;
 however, we assume such examples would involve more limited scope or task execution. LLMs
 are especially relevant in any type of enterprise agent use involving some level of sophistication
 as well as workflows or autonomous work tasks that incorporate meaningful amounts of

 unstructured data. That is where models and powerful reasoning become important in driving
 more effective and deterministic agent outcomes, in our opinion.

 Planning Layer

 The planning layer enables the agent to translate objectives into executable steps. Rather than
 reacting to a single prompt, the agent can evaluate the components and determine the ordering
 across tasks. The planning function can range from simple prompt-based task breakdowns to
 more structured planning sequences. From what we have observed, this is the component that
 allows agents to operate over longer time horizons and handle workflows traditionally performed
 by humans.

 Memory & Context

 Agents require memory to operate efficiently as time passes. Agent architecture typically
 includes both short-term memory (e.g., session context or recent actions) and long-term
 memory (e.g., specific customer data, prior results, or general knowledge). This memory layer
 improves personalization, continuity, and accuracy. Importantly, we believe that proprietary data
 stored in memory increasingly represents a key source of differentiation when it comes to agent
 usage across enterprise workflows.

 Tools & APIs

 One of the key differentiators of agents is their ability to act across external systems. The
 agents can do this through tools and API integrations where agents can retrieve data, update
 records, send communications, or trigger workflows across enterprise software environments.
 The agent will leverage an LLM to determine when a tool is needed and then move to the
 execution or orchestration layer to handle how the action is performed. Further, we believe this
 capability of being able to connect reasoning to system changes in real-time is what
 differentiates agents from assistants or copilots.

 Learning and Evaluation Layer

 Agent architectures also incorporate self-evaluation and improvement to help strengthen
 performance over time. Agents can assess the success of an action vs. its intended result,
 incorporate feedback from either a human or automated entity, and make changes to its future
 behavior accordingly. Agents can also sometimes leverage reinforced learning to optimize
 decision making processes over time, typically in environments where explicit training data is
 not available. We believe the learning layer is critical to agentic systems as it provides agents
 with improvements to become more reliable over time and creates trust when being used within
 large enterprise systems. This layer also relates to reality of human-in-the-loop dynamics we
 could envision in situations such as high value agent work being performed, determinism
 outcomes are critically important, regulated industry settings where additional complexity, trust
 or privacy factors are heightened, etc.

 Proliferation of Agents = Need for Interoperability, Management and
 Observability
 As more agents become prevalent in the workforce, there is an inherent need for them to be
 able to communicate, understand, and coordinate with each other to achieve complex goals.
 Interoperability’s foundation is built on standardized communication languages to allow agents
 to exchange messages, delegate tasks, and understand capabilities. Agents need to
 dynamically be able to identify skills and tools that best fit a given situation. As agent
 interoperability begins to break down silos across enterprise applications and scale effectively,
 security and compliance will remain key to unlocking fully autonomous workflows.

 Management and observability will also be key to agent success in production environments.
 Similar to everyday employees, digital workers or agents need to be managed and assessed on
 their performance. As these agents get rolled out, they must have clear objectives, task
 assignments, and performance scoring. This aligns with an ongoing narrative around “agentic
 personas.” Companies like Anthropic (Private) are rolling out agents with specific expertise to
 automate tasks and workflows in certain areas like HR and FP&A. As these agents become
 more autonomous, it is important to audit their decision making and logic framework. Agents
 have the tendency to “hallucinate,” meaning that they deduce their own reasoning/make up
 information to meet certain criteria. A key focus with agents going forward is to have visibility
 into their reasoning and have an audit trail into how they are formulating their decisioning.

 Agent interoperability will be crucial for different AI agents, built on various platforms, to
 seamlessly communicate, understand each other, share data, and coordinate actions to achieve
 complex goals, forming an open ecosystem for scalable automation without being locked into
 single vendors or frameworks. It enables features like HR chatbots collaborating with finance
 systems for payroll, or IT agents automatically pulling data, leading to more efficient,
 autonomous, and interconnected AI workflows. Key enablers include standardized protocols
 (like MCP, A2A), discovery mechanisms, and trust frameworks for secure, context-aware
 collaboration across different systems and cloud environments.

 Model Context Protocol (MCP)

 Anthropic introduced MCP in November 2025 as an open standard that enables AI systems to
 connect directly with external data sources, tools, and services through a consistent interface.
 According to Anthropic, MCP provides a standardized way for models to access and interact
 with real-world context, allowing them to move beyond static knowledge.

 MCP enables bi-directional communication between AI models and external environments:

  Models can retrieve data from connected systems
  Models can take actions or write back to those systems
  Interactions occur through a structured, secure protocol layer

 Agent2Agent Protocol (A2A)

 A2A is an open standard protocol introduced by Google (GOOGL, Buy, Squali) in April 2025
 that is designed to allow AI agents to communicate, exchange information, and coordinate
 actions across different systems and platforms. The goal of A2A, according to Google, is to
 move beyond isolated agents towards interconnected agent ecosystems, where independent
 systems can collaborate regardless of how or where they were built.

 A2A introduces a standard communication layer that allows agents to:

  Discover one another
  Exchange structured messages
  Coordinate multi-step tasks

 Importantly, A2A does not require agents to share the same infrastructure or internal logic,
 focusing instead on how they interact. This allows for flexibility and modularity as agents
 communicate separately from how they are built.

 Why Are MCP and A2A Complementary?

 While it might seem that Google and Anthropic have created two operating systems for agentic
 AI that will compete to be the standard across enterprise workflows, it is important to note that
 these standards will work alongside each other because they address different layers of the
 stack. According to Google, A2A enables communication between agents while Anthropic

 highlights that MCP connects agents to tools, APIs, and data sources. The important takeaway
 on the differences of these protocols is that A2A promotes agent coordination and MCP
 facilitates agent execution, both equally important capabilities to address enterprise agentic

 Also, going forward we will be watching to see if other industry standards or protocol efforts

 smoothly drive purchases in a repeatable fashion through LLMs and conversational interfaces.

 The Shift to the Generative UI: The Final Mile of Delivery for Agents
 We believe the way that agentic tasks will be delivered comes from what is called “generative
 UI.” Traditional UI interfaces are static layouts that are typically manually configured to include
 menus and workflows that will be accessed by humans. It is our belief that in an agentic world,
 dynamic, real-time interfaces will be utilized, and they are personally tailored based on user
 intent, context, and behavioral data.

 At its core, generative UI bridges the traditional separation between back-end logic and front-
 end design. Historically, developers built fixed interfaces on top of APIs, requiring users to
 navigate menus, dashboards, and workflows to complete tasks. In a generative model, AI
 agents sit on top of these systems and construct the interface on demand, effectively turning the
 interface itself into a byproduct of the task being performed. This shift is best understood as the
 transition from workflow-driven software to intent-driven software. Instead of adapting to
 predefined screens, users express what they want in natural language, and the system
 dynamically generates the appropriate controls, layouts, and actions needed to fulfill that
 request. The UI is no longer something users learn, it becomes something that adapts to them
 in real time.

 Technically, this is enabled by large language models and agent frameworks that can interpret
 user intent, orchestrate backend actions, and render interface components in real-time. Rather
 than hardcoding every possible state or edge case, developers expose capabilities, and the
 agent determines how those capabilities should be presented and executed in context. The
 implication is a redefinition of the front-end itself. In a generative UI environment, the front-end
 is no longer a static application layer but rather an adaptive surface generated by an AI agent.
 Interfaces become fluid. Personalized and change based on the user, the task, and the
 moment.

 For software companies, we believe this introduces both opportunity and disruption. Products
 that rely on rigid navigation, fixed workflows, or manual configuration risk being abstracted away
 by agent-driven layers that can orchestrate actions across systems. Conversely, we believe
 platforms that own structured data, APIs, and execution infrastructure are better positioned to
 remain relevant, as agents depend on these systems to take action. We believe that as
 interfaces become generated rather than designed, the value moves away from screens and
 toward the systems that understand intent and execute outcomes.

 Importantly, we note that generative UI represents the critical “last mile” for agentic systems.
 While much of the focus has been on how agents’ reason, plan, and take action across tools,
 their output ultimately needs to be translated into something a human can understand, validate,
 and trust. Generative interfaces serve as that bridge for dynamically rendering decisions,
 surfacing context, and enabling intervention when needed. In this sense, the UI is no longer just
 a presentation layer, but a core component of the agent loop where human oversight and
 machine execution converge. We believe that as agents take on more complex, multi-step
 workflows, the quality of this interface layer becomes increasingly important, determining not

 just usability, but adoption and trust at scale. From our vantage point of covering application
 software companies, SaaS incumbents as well as AI-native startups will need to perfect agents
 as well as this accompanying generative UI proposition; success or failure here could have
 material consequences on long-term growth and terminal value, in our opinion.

 Agent Security – Another Major Requirement for Broad-Based Agentic
 Traction
 Agentic AI security is the protection of AI agents that can plan, act, and make decisions
 autonomously. It focuses on securing the agent's reasoning, memory, tools, actions, and
 interactions so that autonomy does not create new paths for misuse. It addresses risks that
 arise only when systems execute multi-step tasks, use external tools, or collaborate with other
 agents.

 Figure 12. Agent Security

 Source: Truist Securities Research

 AI agents introduce risks through autonomous reasoning, tool execution, memory updates,
 privilege inheritance, and inter-agent communication. These surfaces can be manipulated,
 poisoned, or redirected, creating pathways for unintended actions, goal drift, privilege misuse, or
 coordinated influence across multi-agent systems. By applying controls to the internal workflow:
 reasoning, planning, tool use, memory, and coordination, one can help secure AI agents.

 Agentic AI systems present unique security challenges. The fundamental security weakness of
 LLMs is that there is no rigorous way to separate instructions from data, so anything they read is
 potentially an instruction. This leads to a “deadly trifecta”: sensitive data, untrusted content, and
 external communication - the risk that the LLM will read hidden instructions that leak sensitive
 data to attackers.

 If one has all three of these factors active, then one is at risk of an attack. Untrusted
 Content can include commands that the LLM might follow while Sensitive Data is the core thing
 most attackers want - this can include things like browser cookies that open up access to other
 data. External Communication allows the LLM application to send information back to the
 attacker.

 Organizations can substantially reduce AI-related security risk by tightly controlling the
 information and external capabilities available to agentic systems. That begins with eliminating
 stored credentials, using environment-based secrets management, enforcing temporary
 privilege escalation, and limiting access tokens to the minimum necessary scope. It also
 requires avoiding MCP servers or browser-extension automations that expose sensitive data,
 since even simple actions, like posting a GitHub comment or loading an image with a crafted
 URL, can leak information. This growing exposure surface is one reason cybersecurity vendors

 Equally important is minimizing an agent’s exposure to untrusted content. Any information

 prompts in isolation, agents interpret goals, plan multi-step actions, use tools, modify memory,

 model call to an entire behavioral loop, making strict content limits, privilege boundaries, and
 communication controls essential to securing next-generation AI systems.

 Securing agentic AI means protecting the system from the inside out-at the points where the
 agent forms intent, makes decisions, accesses resources, and coordinates with others-so the
 focus is the internal workflow, not just perimeter defenses. Securing an agentic AI system
 begins with tightening the cognitive boundaries that guide its behavior. This means ensuring
 that goals remain within an approved scope, limiting how deeply an agent can expand a plan,
 reviewing how it revises its approach as tasks evolve, and preventing it from generating new
 objectives beyond its authorization. At the same time, tools represent the highest-risk surface in
 the ecosystem because they translate decisions into real actions. Strong safeguards, such as
 explicit permission checks, isolated execution environments, strict input validation, and exposing
 only the tools necessary for the immediate task, help ensure that minor reasoning errors do not
 escalate into harmful system changes.

 Memory and privilege require the same level of rigor. Every piece of stored information
 influences future decisions, so memory must be validated, segmented, and restricted to prevent
 over-read and cross-task contamination. Privilege should follow the principle of least access:
 short-lived credentials, no inheritance of elevated rights, and clear boundaries that prevent
 escalation paths from forming. Even small guardrails at this layer can prevent outsized failures
 later.

 In environments where multiple agents collaborate, the complexity, and risk, grows
 substantially. Agents must authenticate one another, validate all exchanged information, and
 operate under strict rules defining who can communicate, when coordination is permitted, and
 how far a handoff can propagate. Expected communication patterns should be enforced,
 deviations quarantined, and every interaction monitored for signs of compromise, manipulation,
 or steering.

 Finally, effective security requires end-to-end visibility across the entire loop. That includes
 tracing how decisions are formed, observing which tools are executed, monitoring memory
 updates, and watching how agents interact. Lightweight, cycle-level verification ensures that
 misalignment or misuse is detected and contained in real time, allowing teams to secure not just
 what the agent produces, but how it thinks, acts, and collaborates.

 M&A Strategy: Scaling the "AI Enabler" Footprint

 Accenture is acquisitive, and its inorganic growth strategy is a key component of its overall top
 line growth trajectory. We note that for FY26, ACN is targeting 3%-5% of growth in local
 currency (5%-7% including FX tailwinds), with inorganic contributions of ~1.5%. Accenture has
 committed $5.0 Billion in capital for acquisitions in FY2026, targeting AI enablers, the physical
 and digital infrastructure required to power agentic workloads. A core component of this strategy
 is the shift toward non-FTE revenue, acquiring firms with high-margin software or subscription
 models to augment traditional consulting.

 Accenture has recently targeted acquisitions that will aid in driving its AI-related revenue. In
 March 2026, Accenture completed the $1.2 Billion acquisition of Ookla, a leader in network
 intelligence; this high-margin subscription business ($231M revenue) is critical for enterprises
 scaling AI across global data networks. Simultaneously, the firm completed the acquisition of
 Faculty in the United Kingdom, an AI-native firm specializing in decision intelligence, which
 provides a platform for software-like revenue models in unmet AI demand areas. Other key
 acquisitions include a majority stake in DLB Associates (December 2025) to aid in establishing
 a foothold in the data center engineering market, and the purchase of CyberCX, marking the
 firm's largest-ever cybersecurity acquisition to secure the agentic stack in Asia-Pacific.

 Key Metrics to Track

  Revenue per Employee Emerging as a Key Metric: Revenue per employee is becoming
 an important metric in IT Services due to the changing nature of billing structures as AI
 becomes a key component of scope and as the traditional billable hour model shifts to
 more of an outcome-based dynamic. Growing revenue per employee indicates not only
 a decoupling of revenue from headcount growth but suggests that outcome-focused AI
 contracts are scaling, and services firms are successfully managing AI-enabled
 headcount in an environment that rewards value and outcomes. In FY25, ACN’s revenue
 per head grew 1.9%, but we note that in the last three quarters, growth has ranged
 between 6.6% and 10.3% per employee.

 transformational budgets rather than just small-scale pilots. ACN’s target of doubling
 bookings in FY26 from its rapidly scaling AI partnerships is another data point that
 exemplifies this theme.
  Margin Expansion through Non-FTE Growth: The aggressive acquisition of subscription
 and licensing businesses like Ookla and Faculty signals a strategic pivot toward
 software-like margins, potentially de-risking the business from the constraints of a purely
 labor-based model.
  The Agentic Labor Force: With 192,000 employees already trained in the Agentic AI
 Fundamentals program and a total pool of 85,000 AI professionals, Accenture is building

Hybrid Pricing, Combining A Base Fee, Usage Meter, And Spending Cap, Reached 37 Percent Adoption
Executive Summary
Hybrid pricing, a base subscription fee combined with a usage meter and a spending cap, reached 37 percent adoption among business to business software companies in 2026, up from 25 percent the year before.
The structure answers a specific problem. When an artificial intelligence agent’s cost to serve and the value it delivers both vary widely across customers, a single flat price either starves the vendor on heavy accounts or overcharges light ones. A base fee covers fixed cost and a defined slice of expected consumption. A usage meter, priced above marginal inference cost, recovers the variable cost a flat fee cannot reach. A spending cap, paired with tiered usage alerts and a rollover credit pool, keeps the meter from producing an invoice that ends a renewal conversation.
The pressure behind the shift is measurable. A July 2026 survey of roughly 305 software company executives put the median targeted gross margin on artificial intelligence product lines near 50 percent, against 70 to 85 percent for conventional software, because inference cost scales with usage in a way that server hosting for traditional software never did. Two customers on an identical 49 dollar monthly plan can generate inference costs of 2 dollars and 200 dollars respectively, a spread no flat fee can absorb without either losing money on the heavy account or leaving revenue on the table from the light one. Companies already selling consumption priced and outcome priced agent features, including customer support platforms billing per resolved conversation at 99 cents to 1 dollar and 50 cents a resolution, show the mechanics in practice: a visible base fee, a metered charge tied to a defined unit of work, and a spend control the customer can see before the invoice arrives.
The finding that matters most for a business to business software company building an agent feature today is that the pricing structure is now a product decision, not a finance afterthought, and it carries contractual and disclosure consequences that reach past the pricing page.
Definition, Scope, and Technical Context
Hybrid pricing, for purposes of this analysis, means a revenue structure built from three distinct components: a recurring base fee that is fixed for the contract term, a usage meter that charges per unit of a defined billable action once consumption exceeds an included allowance, and a spending cap that bounds what the customer can be billed in a period through a hard limit, a tiered alert sequence, or both. The analysis covers business to business software companies that sell an artificial intelligence agent feature, meaning software that takes multi step action on a customer’s behalf rather than software that only answers a single prompt, and it is scoped to the pricing architecture decision itself: how to size the base fee, define the meter, and set the cap. It does not cover broader packaging questions such as which features sit in which plan tier, and it treats go to market and sales compensation design as downstream of the pricing decision rather than as its subject.
The underlying economic problem sits at the intersection of three disciplines that do not usually share a single analysis: SaaS commercial contracting, cloud cost accounting, and the psychology of billing predictability. A 2026 industry monetization report found that 37 percent of business to business software companies had adopted a hybrid structure, up from 25 percent a year earlier, while early stage companies under 5 million dollars in annual revenue still favored flat fees at 37 percent adoption and the largest incumbents, those above 150 million dollars in annual revenue, retained legacy per seat pricing at 29 percent. That split is itself a data point: the companies closest to the actual cost of running an agent, meaning the ones building and billing it today rather than the ones who priced it years ago under a seat model, are the ones moving fastest toward the hybrid structure.
Two terms carry the analytical weight of this report and are defined precisely here. Cost to serve means the marginal cost a vendor incurs to produce one unit of agent output, a figure dominated by model inference spend, which is the computing cost of running a large language model call. Value delivered means the economic benefit the customer realizes from that same unit of output, which ranges from near zero for a trivial, deflected question to tens of thousands of dollars for a retained enterprise contract or a resolved high stakes support case. The central claim this report tests is that these two variables, cost to serve and value delivered, move independently of one another across a customer base, and that independence is what makes a single flat number, whether set on cost or on value, unworkable across a full customer base.
Underlying Scientific and Engineering Principles
An AI agent’s cost to serve is a function of how many model calls a task requires, how large the context window is on each call, how many tool invocations or intermediate steps the agent takes, and how often it retries a failed step. A simple, single turn frequently asked question answer might call a language model once with a short prompt. A multi step troubleshooting conversation, by contrast, can call the model dozens of times, each call carrying an expanding transcript as context, plus additional calls to retrieve documentation or invoke an external tool. A companion trade publication analysis published October 1, 2026 captured the resulting spread in a single sentence: two customers paying the same 49 dollars a month can cost a vendor 2 dollars and 200 dollars respectively. That spread, close to two orders of magnitude within a single pricing tier, is not a billing error. It is the direct, mechanical consequence of how transformer based model inference scales with token count and call depth.
This cost behavior differs fundamentally from the cost structure traditional SaaS pricing was built to recover. Server hosting cost for conventional software scales sub linearly with usage because infrastructure is shared across many customers and amortized over a large base, which is why a flat per seat price worked for two decades. Model inference cost scales linearly with usage, or worse, because every additional agent action consumes compute in close to direct proportion to its complexity, with no equivalent amortization effect across customers. A fixed cost structure cannot track a cost driver that behaves this way without either overcharging the light user or losing money on the heavy one.
Value delivered follows an entirely separate and uncorrelated curve. An agent that resolves a password reset question and an agent that stops a 12,000 dollar annual subscription from canceling can cost the vendor nearly the same amount to run, while delivering value that differs by several orders of magnitude. This is the second half of the mechanism design problem: cost to serve varies by complexity of the task, value delivered varies by the economic stakes of the outcome, and the two variables are not the same variable measured twice. A pricing structure built to track only cost will underprice the subscription save and overprice the password reset. A pricing structure built to track only value will do the reverse on margin.
The classical economic answer to recovering a fixed cost while pricing a variable one is the two part tariff: a fixed fee that captures the customer’s willingness to pay for access, paired with a per unit price set close to marginal cost that both rations usage efficiently and recovers the variable cost component. A base subscription fee plus a usage meter is a direct application of that two part tariff logic to an AI agent feature. Outcome based pricing, where the billable unit is a verified result rather than a unit of computation, is a related but distinct application: it prices to the value side of the equation directly, which works only where the outcome can be measured cleanly and attributed without dispute.
One constraint here is fixed by the physics of current model architectures and will not be engineered away: inference cost variance tracks reasoning depth and context length, and no amount of billing software changes that relationship. A separate constraint is contingent and already loosening: the absolute level of unit cost, and to a lesser extent the degree of cross customer variance, both continue to fall as model providers introduce cheaper routing paths for simple steps, smaller specialized models for narrow tasks, and caching that avoids repeated computation on repeated context. The cost curve should flatten over time even as some irreducible variance across customers remains.
System Architecture and Process Flow
A hybrid pricing structure requires billing infrastructure that per seat pricing never needed. Five subsystems do the work. A metering layer captures the billable event at the moment the agent completes a defined unit of work, such as a resolved conversation or a completed action, and attributes it to a customer account. This is a different artifact from a raw inference log: it is the commercial unit the customer agreed to pay for, not the number of tokens the model consumed to produce it. A rating engine then applies the per unit price, nets it against the included allowance from the base fee, and applies any committed use discount the contract specifies. A real time balance tracker nets consumption against the included allowance and any rolled over credit pool and fires alerts at defined thresholds, commonly 60, 80, and 90 percent of the allowance, before the account approaches its cap. A cap enforcement mechanism then governs what happens at the ceiling: it can throttle additional usage, route to a lower cost mode, require an explicit customer decision to continue at overage rates, or stop the feature. Finally, an invoicing and audit layer reconciles the metered charges against the base fee each billing cycle and gives the customer a way to query exactly what was billed and why, because an unexplained line item is the single most common trigger for a billing dispute.
Building this architecture is closer to a manufacturing pilot run than to a pricing page edit. The companion trade publication analysis cited above recommends 30 to 90 days of per customer cost instrumentation before a vendor sets any price on a new agent feature, the commercial equivalent of validating a production process before committing to a unit cost. Skipping that step is the root cause of the margin erosion failure mode addressed later in this report.
The architectural gap between vendors is visible in how they have built the cap enforcement subsystem specifically. One customer support platform gives customers a native interface to set usage reminders and hard limits themselves, and it refunds a billed resolution if the customer later reopens the conversation, building fairness directly into the metering logic. A competing platform offers no mechanism to cap a bill short of pausing the AI agent entirely, meaning the only available control is to turn the feature off. The difference between these two approaches is not a matter of engineering difficulty. It is a product decision about where the enforcement subsystem sits on a spectrum from graceful throttling to a binary switch, and that decision determines whether a spending cap protects the customer relationship or damages it at the worst possible moment.
Quantitative Performance Metrics
The adoption numbers move in one direction. Hybrid pricing reached 37 percent adoption among business to business software companies in 2026, up from 25 percent the year before, and 29 percent of companies now offer customers multiple pricing options to choose from, up from 21 percent. Among investors surveyed on preferred pricing architecture for portfolio companies, only 5 percent favored pure seat based pricing, against 24 percent for usage based, 26 percent for outcome based, and 35 percent for hybrid. A separate survey of more than 240 software companies found credit based hybrid pricing specifically jumped from roughly 25 percent to roughly 40 percent of respondents within a single year.
Margin figures explain the pressure. A median targeted gross margin for AI product lines sits near 50 percent, against 70 to 85 percent for conventional software, a gap of 20 to 35 percentage points. A July 2026 survey of roughly 305 software company executives found aggregate AI gross margins at 45 percent in 2025, projected to reach 53 percent in 2026 and 59 percent in 2027; the 2026 and 2027 figures are forward projections from executive respondents, not audited results, and should be read as such. The same survey found companies blending an average of 1.7 pricing models per product in 2026, up from 1.5, with subscription still present in 57 percent of products, consumption based pricing present in 42 percent, up from 35 percent, and outcome based pricing present in 23 percent, up from 18 percent. Among companies that already charge for consumption, only 15 percent absorb all of the underlying inference cost themselves; the remaining majority pass token cost through to the customer in some form, which is the strongest evidence that cost plus overage pricing is closer to a market norm than an aggressive outlier position.

Key Advantages and Limitations
A base fee combined with a usage meter and a spending cap covers fixed and variable cost in a way neither a flat fee nor a pure usage fee can match on its own. The mechanism is simple to state even where the calibration is not: the base fee recovers overhead and a defined slice of expected consumption, the meter recovers the long tail of variable cost tied to actual compute consumed, and the cap bounds the tail risk that drives a procurement team’s resistance to open ended usage exposure. This combination also aligns price with the actual cost driver rather than a proxy such as seat count, which removes the loss leader problem a flat fee creates on its heaviest accounts.
Two limitations are fixed by the nature of metered and outcome based pricing and will not be solved by better software. First, any usage meter requires the customer to be able to verify what they were charged for, which means exposing some amount of internal execution detail, such as how many steps a conversation took or what was escalated, that a vendor might otherwise prefer to keep opaque; transparency is the price of metering, not an optional feature. Second, outcome based pricing requires a definition of a successful outcome that is specific enough to be checked by a third party and resistant to gaming on either side, and any ambiguity in that definition, such as whether a customer simply stopped replying versus the agent actually resolving the matter, becomes a contract dispute rather than a billing nuance.
Two further limitations are contingent on the current maturity of billing tooling and should narrow over the next several years. Spending cap implementations today are frequently blunt, offering only a full feature pause rather than a graduated response such as routing to a cheaper model tier or deferring non urgent work, a product design choice rather than a technical ceiling. Unit definitions also remain inconsistent across vendors, so a ‘resolution’ at one company is not the same billable unit as a ‘resolution’ at another, which complicates customer comparison shopping and cross company benchmarking until the market converges on shared definitions the way cloud computing converged on standard units for compute and storage years earlier.
Technical Failure-Mode Analysis
Bill shock from unbounded metering is the most acute failure mode and the one most likely to end a renewal. The mechanism is simple: with no ceiling in place, a genuine usage spike, whether from a misconfigured workflow, an unusual volume of complex support cases, or naive early experimentation, produces an invoice far above what the customer expected. One widely cited case involved a developer who ran up a 500 dollar bill experimenting with a general purpose model API; another involved a logistics company that incurred a 25,000 dollar bill from a misconfigured mapping integration. The failure is most likely in the first 60 to 90 days of a new deployment, before either party has calibrated what normal usage looks like, and the consequence ranges from a support ticket and a partial credit to full cancellation and reputational damage that travels through a finance team’s professional network. The documented mitigation is real time usage visibility, alerts at 60, 80, and 90 percent of the included allowance, and a cap that is either hard or requires an explicit customer decision before overage billing continues.
Margin erosion from unmetered flat pricing is the gradual counterpart to bill shock. A flat price set below the true cost to serve a high usage account converts that account from profitable to loss making as usage grows, often invisibly, because aggregate cost of goods sold figures hide what a per customer breakdown would show immediately. The failure is most likely in the pricing of a new agent feature launched before the 30 to 90 days of per customer cost instrumentation described earlier in this report, which is exactly the gap one company closed when it traced AI costs to the individual feature level and cut spend on one feature by 80 percent, recovering 2.4 million dollars in cloud spend in the process. The mitigation is a defined included allowance paired with metered overage above it, and a cost plus floor that refuses to let any negotiated discount price a unit below a fixed multiple, commonly three times, of its marginal inference cost.
Cliff edge cap enforcement is an acute failure that interacts with and often follows the first two. When a spending cap is implemented as a full, ungraceful shutoff rather than a graduated response, it converts a billing control into a service outage, typically at the customer’s highest demand moment, since high demand is what triggered the cap in the first place. A vendor offering no option between unlimited billing and a complete feature pause, such as the annual 10,000 resolution ceiling documented in this market, has built a control that trades one kind of customer anger, an unexpected bill, for another, an unexpected outage. The available mitigation is tiered throttling: routing to a lower cost model, deferring non urgent tasks, or requiring explicit customer approval to continue at overage rates rather than cutting the feature off without warning.
Outcome attribution disputes form a failure chain of their own, one that can trigger both contract and revenue recognition consequences. When the billable event depends on a judgment call, such as whether a conversation was truly resolved or whether an agent’s recommendation caused a customer to stay, the vendor and the customer can reach different conclusions about what is owed. This risk is highest where the definition of a successful outcome was fixed once at signing and never revisited, or where it is renegotiated at each renewal in the vendor’s favor, a pattern reported in at least one enterprise conversational agent deployment in this market. The consequence runs beyond a single invoice dispute: for a public company, variable consideration recognized as revenue before it was reasonably certain not to reverse creates restatement exposure under applicable accounting guidance. The mitigation is in product, auditable measurement of the outcome at the moment it occurs, a definition fixed for the full contract term rather than reopened unilaterally at renewal, and a dispute mechanism written into the order form itself.
These failure modes compound. An account already underpriced through the margin erosion mode is also the account most likely to be the first to reach a spending cap, which means the two failures most often land on the same customer at the same time: the one a vendor can least afford to lose and least afford to keep serving at the current price.
Real-World Applications and Deployment Context
Customer support and service resolution is the most developed deployment context for hybrid and outcome based agent pricing in 2026. One widely used helpdesk AI agent combines a per seat base fee of 29 to 132 dollars a month with a 99 cent charge per resolved outcome and a 9.99 dollar charge per qualified sales lead, and it gives customers native tools to set spend reminders and hard limits themselves. A competing helpdesk platform charges 1.20 to 1.50 dollars per verified resolution on top of its seat and add on fees, with an annual ceiling of 10,000 resolutions across all plans but no graduated spend control short of pausing the agent outright.
Sales and revenue operations workflows have moved toward per action and per workflow pricing. One customer relationship management platform’s agent add on prices individual agent actions at 10 to 15 cents each through a prepaid credit system, with a 500 dollar minimum purchase, while bundling a fixed annual credit allowance into its higher priced per user editions, which start at 125 dollars and run to 550 dollars a month depending on the edition.
Specialized agents in revenue recovery, such as chargeback dispute management and travel claim resolution, have adopted pure outcome based pricing, taking a percentage of money actually recovered, which works cleanly because the outcome, money recovered, is unambiguous and self verifying in a way a general support resolution is not.
Enterprise conversational agent platforms sold through a direct, negotiated sales motion show the model at its least transparent. One such platform reports an annual platform fee near 150,000 dollars as a floor, plus a per successful outcome charge in the range of 1 to 2.50 dollars that the vendor has never publicly confirmed, with implementation and professional services adding 50,000 to 200,000 dollars in the first year. No public pricing calculator exists, and the outcome definition is frequently renegotiated at renewal. This deployment context substitutes the buyer’s own bargaining position and dedicated vendor management function for the standardized caps and dashboards that self serve products build into the product itself, a substitution that works for a large enterprise with a procurement team and works poorly for a mid market buyer without one.
Across these four deployment contexts, the segment that most needs the complete three part hybrid structure, a base fee, a meter, and a visible cap, is the self serve and lightly assisted mid market, where the buyer has no dedicated vendor management function to negotiate custom protections and depends on the product itself to deliver spend predictability.
Economic and Scalability Considerations
Model inference is the dominant variable cost line in an agent feature, accounting for roughly a fifth of total AI product cost before a product reaches scale and rising toward a quarter of cost once usage is high, with the remainder spread across retrieval infrastructure, orchestration, and any human review step in the loop. Targeted gross margin for AI native product lines sits near 50 percent against 70 to 85 percent for conventional software, a gap finance teams now track at the individual customer level rather than in the aggregate, because a margin that moves with individual customer behavior cannot be read off a profit and loss statement that does not track customers individually.
The learning curve runs in the vendor’s favor over time. Aggregate AI gross margins are projected to expand from roughly 45 percent in 2025 to the low fifties in 2026 and toward 59 percent by 2027, driven primarily by falling per token model prices, by model routing that sends simple steps to a cheaper model and reserves a frontier model for complex steps, and by prompt caching that avoids repeated computation on repeated context, rather than by price increases passed to the customer.
The bottlenecks to building out a hybrid structure are organizational before they are technical. The instrumentation work itself, tracking cost at the individual customer level for 30 to 90 days before setting a price, is the first bottleneck, and it requires finance and engineering to share data neither function previously needed from the other. Billing platform integration capable of rating and invoicing a metered component without manual intervention is the second. Sales compensation design is the third and most underestimated: a seller compensated on booked annual seats has limited incentive to sell a usage based structure whose revenue only materializes as the customer actually consumes the product, which means a pricing change that finance wants can stall in the field until compensation plans change to match it.
Pass through economics in the broader market support the cost plus floor approach rather than treating it as aggressive. Among companies already selling consumption priced AI features, only 15 percent absorb the full underlying inference cost themselves; the large majority pass that cost through to the customer in some form, which places a cost plus overage price, commonly set at three times raw inference cost as a floor, close to prevailing market practice rather than at its outer edge. Capital intensity in this model shows up as working capital and forecasting difficulty rather than as physical plant: variable consideration under usage and outcome contracts is harder to forecast and book than ratable seat revenue, a cost that does not appear in a gross margin calculation but appears directly in forecasting accuracy and, for a public company, in the risk attached to forward guidance.
Regulatory Environment and Compliance Considerations
Revenue recognition for usage based and outcome based agent pricing falls under Topic 606 of the Accounting Standards Codification, specifically the variable consideration guidance that requires a company to estimate consideration tied to future usage or a future outcome and then constrain that estimate to the amount it is probable will not later reverse. A flat seat fee recognizes ratably over the contract term with little judgment involved. A per resolution or per outcome fee requires an estimate of future consumption or success rates, an estimate that becomes harder to defend precisely where the outcome definition itself is renegotiated at each contract renewal, since a shifting definition undermines the basis for last year’s estimate as a reference point for this year’s.
For a public company, that same margin and revenue volatility intersects with disclosure obligations under Item 303 of Securities and Exchange Commission Regulation S-K, which requires disclosure of known material trends or uncertainties affecting liquidity, capital resources, or results of operations. A February 2026 Securities and Exchange Commission inquiry to Doximity over its disclosure of artificial intelligence cost trends illustrates the kind of regulatory attention gross margin compression tied to AI features is now drawing. A company shipping a metered or outcome priced agent feature should expect both its revenue growth and its margin trajectory to be a disclosure topic.
Contract formation and performance for a hybrid pricing order form draw on ordinary state contract law principles, including the common law and Uniform Commercial Code concepts of definiteness and the obligation of good faith in performance, both of which bear directly on an ambiguous outcome definition or a unilateral mid term change to a metering rule. An order form that leaves the definition of a billable ‘resolution’ or ‘successful outcome’ vague invites the same dispute a court faces when a contract leaves a price term open: a judge or arbitrator filling the gap with a reasonable standard neither party actually negotiated.
Federal and state consumer protection law bears on this market more than most business to business vendors assume, because many agent features are sold to small business and sole proprietor accounts that some state statutes treat similarly to consumers for billing disclosure purposes. The Federal Trade Commission’s negative option rule, which would have required clear disclosure and easy cancellation for automatically renewing and continuity plans, was vacated in its entirety by the United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit in July 2025; the Commission proposed a narrower revival of the rule in May 2026. Separately, California and roughly two dozen other states maintain automatic renewal statutes requiring clear and conspicuous disclosure of recurring and overage charges, a set of requirements directly relevant to how a vendor discloses what happens once a customer’s usage exceeds its included allowance. A vendor that markets a spending ‘cap’ but still bills beyond the represented ceiling, or that sets alert thresholds in a way that appears designed to go unnoticed, creates exposure under state unfair and deceptive practices statutes independent of any federal rulemaking.
Future Development Trajectory
Hybrid adoption should keep climbing from its current 37 percent as the remaining holdouts, concentrated among the largest incumbents above 150 million dollars in annual revenue who still favor legacy per seat pricing at 29 percent adoption, face competitive pressure from AI native challengers pricing closer to realized value. Aggregate AI gross margins should continue improving toward the high fifties and approach 59 percent by 2027 as model inference cost continues falling, an outcome that depends on continued competition among model providers holding per token prices down rather than on pricing increases passed to customers.
Billing infrastructure purpose built for usage and outcome pricing should become as standard a procurement line item for an agent feature launch as a payments processor is for an online checkout flow, closing the current gap in which many vendors ship a usage meter without the real time alerting and graduated cap enforcement that prevents bill shock in the first place. The single design choice most worth watching is whether spending cap enforcement standardizes around graduated throttling, routing to a lower cost tier, deferring non urgent work, or requiring explicit customer approval to continue, rather than today’s common choice between unlimited billing and a full feature shutoff, because that one decision separates the vendors building customer trust from the ones building renewal risk.
On the regulatory side, the Federal Trade Commission’s narrower revival of its negative option disclosure rule, together with any extension of state automatic renewal statutes to cover usage based overage billing specifically, would push vendors toward the transparent, alert based cap design this report recommends on pricing grounds alone, converting a competitive best practice into a legal floor. The risk that this overall shift toward hybrid pricing reverses is low, because the underlying cause, cost to serve and value delivered moving independently of one another across a customer base, is an inherent feature of how current model inference scales, not a temporary artifact of immature billing tools. The specific mix of base fee size, meter unit, and cap mechanism will keep changing as billing platforms mature, but the three part structure itself is unlikely to give way back to a single flat number.

Open Questions
1. What is the right size for the included allowance inside the base fee, expressed as a percentile of expected consumption. The 60th to 70th percentile heuristic reported in the trade press is a reasonable starting point, but no public data yet shows how that threshold affects renewal and expansion revenue once a vendor has a full year of billing history across many accounts. Resolving this needs comparative data across several vendors’ billing platforms through at least one full renewal cycle, and it matters because a threshold set too low puts most customers into overage every month, eroding trust in the base fee itself, while a threshold set too high overprices the light users who should be the easiest accounts to keep.
2. Will spending cap enforcement standardize on graduated throttling within the next two to three years, closing the gap between vendors that give customers a native spend control and vendors whose only control is a full feature pause. This question is close to resolution because it depends on product choices billing platforms are already building toward, and it matters because the cliff edge failure mode described in this report is a design choice, not a limitation inherent to metered billing.
3. How will revenue recognition practice settle for outcome based contracts where the definition of a successful outcome is renegotiated at each renewal. This is an accounting and legal question that will likely take several annual audit cycles to produce consistent practice across public companies, and it matters because inconsistent treatment of variable consideration makes margin and revenue comparisons across companies unreliable for an investor trying to judge the sustainability of a reported number.
4. Will the Federal Trade Commission’s narrower revival of its negative option rule, or a new state automatic renewal statute, reach business to business overage and usage based billing disclosures specifically rather than stopping at consumer subscriptions. This is a near term regulatory question tied to the Commission’s own rulemaking timeline following the Eighth Circuit’s 2025 vacatur, and it matters because it would convert today’s best practice disclosure, meaning tiered alerts and a visible cap, into a legal requirement carrying enforcement exposure for any vendor that falls short.
5. Does outcome based pricing remain workable as agents improve enough that most interactions become trivial to resolve. If an agent eventually resolves the large majority of conversations at a cost approaching zero, a per resolution fee priced today may need to fall sharply to avoid looking detached from the underlying cost, and whether vendors reprice ahead of that pressure or wait for customers to force the issue is a commercial question with a direct effect on how long current margin levels hold.

How to Price an AI Agent When It Fails a Task Halfway Through&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
Nobody bills like this on purpose, yet almost every AI agent company is doing it anyway: charging full price for runs that never finish, and hoping nobody asks why.
- Cognition's Devin charges per Agent Compute Unit regardless of whether the task finishes, which is partial billing dressed up as a clean metric
- Sierra, the customer service agent startup co-founded by Bret Taylor, bills only on resolved conversations, proving usage-based and outcome-based pricing can coexist
- A GPT-4 class model can burn $3 to $15 per million tokens before an agent produces a single usable output
- Replit and Cursor both had to bolt on usage caps after flat-rate plans collapsed under agents that loop, retry, and burn tokens without finishing
- The real fix is a three-tier structure: free retries for infrastructure failures, partial credit for verified partial output, full charge only past a disclosed checkpoint
Here's the question almost nobody in AI agent pricing wants to answer out loud: your agent spent four minutes and 40,000 tokens on a task, got halfway through, hit a wall, and gave up. Do you charge the customer for that?
Most companies don't answer. They just charge anyway, because the metering system doesn't know how to tell a finished job from an abandoned one. That silence is the actual story. You can read a dozen AI agent pricing pages this week and find usage tiers, seat counts, token multipliers, and not one sentence about what happens when the agent fails. The absence is the policy.
Traditional software failure is binary and cheap to reason about. A checkout button breaks, the user doesn't get charged, you ship a fix. The cost of the failure to you was basically zero.
Agent failure is not like that. The compute already happened. If your agent spent three minutes calling a model, searching the web, writing code, and running it before it got stuck on a dependency conflict, you paid for every one of those steps. A GPT-4 class model runs $3 to $15 per million tokens, and a multi-step agent loop can chew through tens of thousands of tokens before producing anything a user can act on. That cost doesn't disappear because the task didn't finish. It just becomes a cost you either absorb or pass on, and most companies are quietly doing the second one by default, through metering systems that were never designed to ask the question.
A customer whose AI support agent clears a 4,000-ticket backlog in one weekend can rack up API costs exceeding what they paid for the entire quarter on a $49 seat plan. Intercom's Fin charges about $0.99 per resolved conversation instead. - how to price AI agents per seat - per task pricing model for AI software
That's the actual tension. You're not deciding whether to refund a broken feature. You're deciding who eats a real compute bill for work that produced no value to the customer.
What the market is already doing, and why it's inconsistent
Look at how the companies actually running agents at scale have handled this, because none of them agree with each other, and that disagreement is informative.
Cognition's Devin bills in Agent Compute Units, a flat metric tied to time and resource use rather than outcome. Run Devin for an hour on a task it never completes, and you've spent ACUs exactly as if it had shipped the fix. That's not a hidden policy. It's the explicit model, and it works for Cognition because the pitch is "you're renting a tireless engineer," not "you're buying a finished pull request." The honesty is the point: they never promised outcome-based pricing, so there's nothing to apologize for when a run comes up empty.
Sierra, the customer service agent company Bret Taylor co-founded after leaving Salesforce, took the opposite bet. Sierra prices on resolution, meaning a customer conversation the agent fails to close doesn't generate a bill. That only works because customer service has a clean, checkable definition of done: did the customer's issue get resolved without a human taking over. Sierra built its entire pricing model around that one countable event, which is a much easier bar to clear than "did this coding agent produce working software," but it proves outcome-based billing isn't theoretical. Someone is doing it at real scale.
Then there's the flat-rate collapse. Cursor and Replit both launched with simple monthly plans and had to retreat to usage-based caps and overage charges within roughly a year, because a small share of users were running agents hard enough to blow past any reasonable flat-rate math. Replit's own incident last year, where an agent deleted a production database during an unsupervised run, is a different failure mode, but it points at the same underlying issue: nobody had fully worked out what the agent owed the customer when things went wrong.
Three companies, three different answers, and not one of them has published an actual framework. That's the gap this piece is trying to fill.
A framework that actually separates the failure types
The mistake most founders make is treating "the agent failed" as one category. It isn't. A task can die for at least three structurally different reasons, and they deserve three different billing treatments.

The first is infrastructure failure: the model API timed out, a tool call errored, your own orchestration layer crashed. None of that is the customer's fault and none of it produced anything resembling value. Charge zero. Automatically retry once at your own expense before you even notify the customer. If you're metering tokens burned on your own outages and billing for them, you're not running a pricing model, you're running a liability.
The second is partial completion with verifiable output. Say a coding agent writes and tests three of five required functions, then gets stuck on a fourth because of an ambiguous spec. That's real work product. A customer who receives three working functions got something they can use, even if it's not the full job. This is where partial credit belongs, billed against whatever fraction of the task's defined checkpoints the agent actually cleared, not against tokens spent. Define the checkpoints before the run starts, not after it fails, or you're just inventing a percentage to justify a number you already wanted to charge.
The third is the hard one: the agent burned real compute and produced nothing usable. It explored five approaches to a bug and never found the fix. No checkpoints cleared, no output a human would accept. This is the only category where "did we spend money" and "did the customer get value" fully diverge, and it's exactly where most companies default to charging anyway because separating it out is annoying. Don't take the lazy path. Cap what you charge for pure dead-end exploration, disclosed up front as a ceiling, and treat anything past that ceiling as your cost of doing business, not the customer's.
Notice what that structure actually requires: you have to define, before the run starts, what a checkpoint is. If you can't define a checkpoint for your product, outcome-based billing isn't available to you yet, and you should say so rather than bolt a resolution-based price tag onto a product that has no clean definition of resolved. That's the mistake behind a lot of agent pricing pages that borrow Sierra's language without Sierra's clarity about what counts as done.
The refund policy nobody writes down
A pricing model is a promise. A refund policy is what happens when the promise breaks, and right now almost no AI agent company publishes one for partial failure. Compare that to how API providers already handle this: if a model call errors out server-side, you're not billed for the tokens, full stop. That convention exists, it's enforceable, and customers expect it to extend upward into the agent layer. It mostly doesn't.
Write the policy down and put it somewhere a customer can read it before they run anything. State plainly which of the three categories above apply, what checkpoint definitions you use for partial credit, and what the ceiling is on pure dead-end charges. If a competitor is quietly eating these costs and you're not, customers who get burned once will find out, and in a market this crowded, that's not a risk worth taking to avoid writing one page of documentation.
Frankly, the companies that get specific about this first are going to look more credible than the ones staying vague, not less. Vagueness here doesn't read as sophistication. It reads as a metering system nobody wants to admit they haven't finished building.
What to build before you ever bill for a partial run
None of this works without instrumentation most agent builders skip because it's unglamorous. You need your orchestration layer to log, per run, which category a failure fell into: infrastructure, partial-with-output, or dead-end. You need checkpoint definitions attached to the task type before execution starts, not reconstructed afterward to justify a number. And you need a retry budget that's yours, not the customer's, for the first category.
That's three engineering decisions, not a pricing philosophy. Get them wrong and no billing model built on top of them will hold up under a customer who actually reads their invoice. Get them right and outcome-based pricing stops being a slogan and becomes something you can actually defend line by line, the way Sierra can point to a resolved ticket count and Cognition can point to an hour of compute time. Both of those companies can explain their bill in one sentence. That's the bar. If you can't explain your partial-failure charge in one sentence to the person paying it, you don't have a pricing model yet. You have a number.

Very relaxed and also incredibly obsessed and tenacious: Violinist Anthea Kreston talks about ‘Crescendo: Chronicles of an Adventurous Violinist’

Kreston, a staple of the Oregon classical music scene, discusses her new book and her life in music.

By 
James Bash
 
October 13, 2026

Violinist 
Anthea Kreston
, artist-in-residence at 
All Classical Radio
, has published 
Crescendo: Chronicles of an Adventurous Violinist
 with Paul Dry books. It’s a lively memoir of her life, spanning her early years as a budding violinist, her studies at the Curtis Institute of Music, her time as a member of the prestigious Artemis String Quartet (2016-2019), motherhood, burnout, and much more. Kreston’s unfiltered account of herself, including her struggle with stress, is a page-turner, covering periods in which she lived in a commune, lost her violin, and wrote for 
Slipped Disc
, Norman Lebrecht’s influential online classical music magazine.

Kreston currently lives in Corvallis with her two daughters and husband, cellist Jason Duckles. She is a member of the Delgani String Quartet, the Thunder Egg Consort, and the Amelia Piano Trio. She has also developed a one-woman show based on her life to accompany presentations of 
Crescendo
. 

I contacted her via Zoom to find out more about Kreston and her new book.

“I’m a first-time author,” said Kreston. “I feel so fortunate to have found a publisher, Paul Dry Books. They have representatives all over the country that are going into bookstores with my book. He has such an eclectic mix of books. Whatever he’s passionate about, he throws his weight behind.”

Kreston’s passion for music making is reflected in her personality.

“I’m a combination of very relaxed and also incredibly obsessed and tenacious,” she said. “I’m very, very competitive in a casual kind of way. There are peaks and valleys to my life. I picked Corvallis because I love this part of the world, and it lets all of us be whoever we want to be. Bringing up this book is reigniting me. It truly has been a crescendo of my life. I have written some dry articles for classical magazines – like on the new transcription of the Chopin piano trio, stuff like that. When I moved to Germany to join the Artemis String Quartet, that was big news because I was the only American violinist currently playing in an elite European string quartet. It’s a very closed off world that is very nationalistic. A French quartet will have four French people, usually men. Italian quartets will be four Italians, typically men. I was the 241st applicant for the Artemis job, and they’d been looking for seven months. To top that off, I was in the heat of motherhood. I had at the time a three-year-old and a five-year-old.”

When Kreston started with Artemis, she also began writing for Norman Lebrecht’s 
Slipped Disc
.

“Norman Lebrecht reached out to me, but I’d been such a mom I didn’t even know about him or his magazine,” she recalled. “He wrote to me, and asked ‘Can you write a one-off for me about winning the job?’ I came back to Oregon to pack things up. I had two weeks to get rid of the house, the cars. We also had Jason’s career to consider. He had to quit Willamette University. I had to clear our entire year of concerts, pack up the girls, and say goodbye to everybody. And Norman said, ‘Can you just write what’s going on with you right now?’ So, I wrote a very raw thing, like about saying goodbye and what was happening.”

Her writings for 
Sipped Disc
 quickly resonated with its readers.

“Then Norman wrote back to me,” said Kreston. “He said, ‘I need you to do this every week for six months because this is so viral; it’s crazy.’ I checked with our manager and with the Artemis Quartet to see if that was okay with them, and they approved it. So, I wrote every week for him for six months, and at that point I thought it would end, but he said ‘You have 20,000 dedicated readers. You can’t quit now.’ That went for the whole four years, and after I came back to Oregon, he remained positive. In fact, he was the first person who ever said that I was a writer.”

Anthea Kreston at All Classical Radio. Photo by Christine Dong.

“I’m not trained as a writer,” added Kreston. “I’ve never taken a writing class or anything like that. But he insisted, ‘You have a book in you. You need to sit down and think about what that book is.’”

Kreston started writing 
Crescendo
 during the pandemic. According to Kreston, she rewrote the book five times, went through two different agents and three different publishers. “The writing process went on for the last six years,” said Kreston. “I also aggressively started to find an agent. That took about a year, and then the agent finding the first publisher, and then me realizing I wanted a better agent, and a better publisher. It took another year for me to find a better agent. I took her advice and rewrote the book. I just kept on trying to cycle up.”

With her busy life, Kreston wrote her memoir on the go. “I wrote the whole thing on my phone with my thumbs,” she said, “because I’m a mom. I’m running around, writing in the grocery store or at the dentist. There’s no place where I can sit down and write. 
Crescendo
 is a non-linear memoir, and it’s the same kind of writing I did for Norman Lebrecht. As a violinist you have to be raw, and you have to be true, and you have to be honest. Writing a book is no different than learning a Brahms sonata or any other piece. It has its development, its themes, its diversion, its denouement, its resolution.”

For Kreston, her violin is a constant that has been with her through thick and thin.

“When I talk about my violin, I get very emotional because it’s the violin that my grandfather gave to me eight years after he died,” said Kreston. “When I was 14, my parents told me that there was a trust that he had left for me. It was directed only to buy a violin. So with that violin, a 1928 Paul Becker, it feels like my grandfather is with me. It’s the violin that I practiced when I was pregnant with my daughters, the violin when I met my husband. It was stolen while I was in Germany. That was awful, but I got it back ten weeks later. The book has themes that are not chronological. It’s all very fanciful. I don’t stay in the same time period, although the narrative is always in present tense. So when I’m four, you’re four with me when I play for Dr. Suzuki. When I have menopause, you have the hot flashes. And when I’m dealing with the suicide of my best friend, you’re right there with me. I do my best to put readers with me, trying to figure out what the decisions should be, and then figuring out where they lead me.”

Crescendo
 covers Kreston’s musical training, her early financial struggles, getting into the Curtis Institute of Music, dealing with fertility issues – even when her manager said if she got pregnant, that he would fire her. She includes the time that she lived in a commune. “I burned out after Curtis, and I moved to Oregon where I lived in a commune,” noted Kreston. “I eventually moved to Cleveland and joined a rock band and got a women’s studies degree. That was a period of absolute freedom. When I found my way back to music, it was without the pressure that I had had before.”

She is especially happy to be the current artist-in-residence at All Classical Radio, and to reconnect with Fred Child, who is its President and CEO. “Fred Child was one of my big supporters in my 20s, when I was just starting my career with the Amelia Piano Trio as artist-in-residence for Performance Today,” said Kreston. “We spent a whole week with him in Washington D.C., giving live concerts and interviews every day. That was at the same time when we were just starting to tour with Yo-Yo Ma, and Isaac Stern was helping us out. So Fred really brought my career to a national level.”

“I have my studio at home in Corvallis,” continued Kreston, “where I teach. My kids are always popping in, making me something. It’s very important for me because I was absent for so many years when we lived in Germany. I really wasn’t there for my family. My daughters are now 14 and 16.  I must say this whole process has been really wonderful for us, because the book has been a talking point for us.”

Kreston now has a one-woman show based on 
Crescendo
. “I started developing the show over a year ago because I wanted to have something to offer to bookstores and book festivals that wasn’t a book reading,” said Kreston. “It’s in seven vignettes, and I play music from different parts of my life.”

“I think that my story is compelling,” said Kreston. “I’m very kind to everybody in the book. I mean, it’s really my story, but there are people that come in and out. It’s joyous, and it’s hilarious, and it’s ridiculous, and it’s silly, and I make fun of myself, and I have tons of failures, so many failures, and keep on going.” In the meantime, Kreston still aims very high as a violinist: “I hold myself to the highest standards. I’m up early every morning and practicing before everyone else wakes up.”

She’s also working on a new memoir. “Right now, I’m working on a chapter where I was in Berlin,” said Kreston. “I had a concert at Wigmore Hall in London that night, and I realized at the airport that my passport was in the mountains in Italy. It was an insane situation, but I worked things out.”

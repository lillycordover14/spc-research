AI customer service startup Decagon held early buyout talks with ServiceNow&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
Decagon has not even finished spending its last funding round, and buyers are already circling. Business Insider reported on October 13 that the AI customer service agent startup held early M&A talks with ServiceNow and other potential acquirers.

Decagon, the three-year-old company that builds AI agents to handle customer support calls and chats for brands like Duolingo, Notion, Hertz, and Deutsche Telekom, has been in early acquisition discussions with ServiceNow and other suitors, Business Insider reported Tuesday. The talks are preliminary, according to the report, but their existence alone says something about where the AI agent market stands right now: enterprise software giants would rather write a check than spend years building the same technology in-house.

The timing is what makes this notable. Decagon closed a $250 million Series D in January led by Coatue Management and Index Ventures, tripling its valuation to $4.5 billion, according to Bloomberg. In March, the company completed a tender offer at that same $4.5 billion mark, TechCrunch reported, letting early employees and investors cash out some shares. That is not the profile of a company that needs to sell. It's the profile of one that doesn't have to, and is still getting calls anyway.

ServiceNow has spent 2025 and 2026 buying its way into agentic AI rather than building every piece of it from scratch. The company agreed to buy Moveworks, an AI assistant maker, for $2.85 billion in March 2025. It also bought the AI-native conversation analysis platform Cuein. And after Bloomberg reported that it was in advanced talks to buy cybersecurity firm Armis, ServiceNow announced the $7.75 billion deal and completed it in April 2026. Bloomberg has described the pace as a deja vu of McDermott's years running SAP, where acquisitions were also the default growth lever.

Decagon would fit a clear gap. ServiceNow sells workflow software to the same enterprise IT buyers who are now being pitched standalone AI customer service agents, and Decagon said it added more than 100 enterprise customers in 2025, spanning travel, financial services, health, and retail. Buying that customer base and the underlying agent technology is faster than spending years rebuilding Decagon's product work on voice, chat, email, and SMS support from scratch.

Decagon isn't the only one proving this thesis. NiCE bought the AI customer service company Cognigy for $955 million, a deal that, like the ServiceNow-Moveworks transaction, signaled that legacy contact-center and workflow vendors see AI agents as something to acquire rather than compete against from scratch.

What this means for the rest of the AI agent field

Frankly, this is the signal every other AI agent startup founder is watching for. The agentic AI market has been thick with venture money for two years, and a lot of these companies look similar on paper: a slick agent orchestration layer sitting on top of a foundation model, sold into enterprise support, sales, or ops teams. Funding rounds prove investors believe in the category. Early M&A talks prove the giants who actually touch these enterprise customers are also starting to believe in specific companies within it.

Decagon, co-founded in 2023 by Jesse Zhang and Ashwin Sreenivas, went from stealth to a $4.5 billion valuation in under two years, according to the company's own funding announcements. That speed is exactly why a buyer would rather move now than wait. The later a target gets, the more expensive and the more independent it becomes, and the weaker an acquirer's hand is walking into the room.

None of this means a deal happens. Early talks fall apart more often than they close, and Decagon, flush with cash from two rounds and a tender offer, has no obvious financial reason to sell. But the fact that ServiceNow is willing to sit down and talk, even informally, tells competitors like Zendesk and the dozens of smaller AI agent startups exactly how this market consolidates when it does: not through slow attrition, but through the biggest platform players buying the sharpest point solutions before their price tags get too big to justify.

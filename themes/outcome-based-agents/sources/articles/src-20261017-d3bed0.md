LangChain CEO Harrison Chase Says Model Routing Belongs in the Harness, Not the Gateway
Building a reliable AI agent, the kind that can handle a real customer request without embarrassing itself, remains one of the most deceptively difficult problems in software. Harrison Chase, the founder and CEO of developer-tools company LangChain, frames it bluntly: the models are non-deterministic black boxes, and when you chain a dozen of them together, the errors don't just add up — they multiply. "It's still really hard to build them," he said on the 20VC podcast. "Like really, really hard."
The reason is structural. An agent isn't one model call. It's a sequence of calls interacting with tools, databases, and APIs. The input space is effectively infinite — "You put a chatbot in front of someone, they can type anything that they want" — and the output space is just as unpredictable. Two nearly identical requests can produce completely different behavior. That unpredictability compounds across every step of a multi-step process, producing what Chase calls "cascading types of errors."
The practical consequence for anyone building or buying agent software is that the value doesn't live in any single model. It lives in the harness — the layer of code that sits between the model and the real work. And that harness is a moving target. LangChain, which exists precisely to help developers build that layer, has had to repeatedly delete parts of its own product as frontier labs absorb yesterday's clever hacks into today's API. Early on, LangChain guided models by having them generate a custom JSON DSL — "a little bit of a hack at the time," Chase admits. When OpenAI shipped native tool calling, LangChain switched entirely. The framework for what gets absorbed is simple: models will never know your proprietary data, and they'll never fetch your API outputs themselves. Everything around the model persists; hacks around plan-and-execute patterns get eaten.
Routing: The Most Divisive Question in the Inference Market
Of all the debates swirling around the agent stack, Chase identifies model routing as among the most heated. The premise is seductive: why pay for a frontier model when a cheaper one will do? A router inspects the task and sends it to the right model based on cost, latency, and difficulty. The question is where that router should live.
Two camps are forming. The gateway camp — represented by players like OpenRouter — puts routing at the API layer, where it's portable across any harness. Drop a gateway key into Claude Code or any other tool, and routing works without touching your code. The harness camp — Chase's position — argues that real routing requires deep state awareness: what the task actually is, what the prompt cache looks like, how to rewrite prompts for different models. "I don't think you'll have routing that works for law and works for finance and works for coding and have it all be the same," he said.
Chase concedes the gateway wins on pure portability. For horizontal use cases — general chat, or coding, the single most popular agent domain — gateway routing makes sense. But the moment an agent becomes domain-specific, routing has to live in the harness, or it's not really routing at all. He's also skeptical about how much "smart routing" actually exists in the market today. "Most of OpenRouter's usage... I would guess a lot of it just comes from being a gateway, not a router," he noted. "In terms of smart routing, I think that's actually being done like way less than people think."
| Dimension | Harness-level routing | Gateway-level routing | 
|---|---|---|
| Portability | Requires code changes | Drop-in for any harness | 
| State awareness | Full task and cache context | Limited to API-level signals | 
| Prompt adaptation | Easy to swap per model | Hard | 
| Best fit | Vertical or domain agents | General chat, coding | 
The reason routing is hard to commoditize is that its value fluctuates with every frontier release. A new Claude Opus or GPT model that's better at everything reduces the payoff from routing for a while. Chase's frankness about his own history is telling: LangChain considered building a gateway a year and a half ago, looked at OpenRouter, and concluded there was no value to add. "100% the wrong decision," he now says — he passed on a market he now describes as enormous.
The 30% Line Item and the Three-Step Fix
The most concrete number in the conversation is a guesstimate, but a useful one. LangChain, Chase says, spends roughly 30% of its engineering salary on coding tools — the agents that write, review, and debug code. In the past two months, that percentage has gone down while token usage has gone up. That inversion — more usage, less cost — is the result of what he calls "a pretty repeatable three-step process."
The first step is visibility. LangChain routes all agent usage through LangSmith, its observability platform, so anyone can see who is spending what and on what. This alone surfaces problems. In one incident, a plugin designed to screenshot a web page's DOM and send it back every turn ran a massive loop during front-end work and burned roughly $40,000 in a week. It wasn't malicious — just a mistake nobody could see until the usage data was visible.
The second step is basic controls: budget guardrails. If a task hits a cost cap — say, $5 per task — the agent stops and the request escalates to a human. The VP of engineering almost always raises the budget, Chase admits, but the gate exists to catch runaway loops before they become five-figure surprises.
The third step is harness optimization. LangChain built its own in-house cloud coding agent, which Chase now uses from Slack and describes as the only way he codes. It implements prompt caching and routing at the harness level — precisely the kind of state-aware optimization that gateway routing can't provide.
Where does that 30% number go in two years? Chase is unusually honest about his uncertainty. One vision is much smaller orgs where a few engineers drive many agents, with coding-agent spend equaling or exceeding human salary. But he observes a persistent human component — review, communication, domain expertise — and suggests that if one person drives 100 agents, that person is paid far more, so total spend may stay flat. The number underpins more than LangChain's budget: it's a proxy for the enterprise value of the model providers themselves. If coding-agent spend is 5% of engineering salary at scale, a $2 trillion Anthropic looks overpriced. If it's 300%, the model looks cheap. Chase's view: so far, agents have had real effect mainly in software engineering. Other enterprise domains are untouched, which he reads as bullish for the labs.
Evals, Traces, and the Verification Advantage
The conventional story about evaluation data is that academic benchmarks are broken — everyone knows they're gamed, and nobody admits it out loud. Chase goes further: at model launch, those evals are "largely benchmark-maxed" and no longer informative. What matters are internal, domain-specific evals — and those are where vertical agent builders win or lose.
His most contrarian claim is about where value accrues. "If value is derived through verification, I think the people who win are the domain experts," he said. "The people who are able to quantify what that value means, which is very hard." Translation: the algorithm isn't the moat. The moat is knowing what a good outcome looks like in a specific domain — being able to write the evaluator that says "this legal brief is correct" or "this customer interaction actually resolved the issue." Whoever can write those verifiers captures the value.
But even internal evals are fallible. Chase insists that "one of the most important things to do is just look at the data." A single eval number can miss that a model improved in a way your particular test didn't capture. The traces — the full logs of what an agent actually did, step by step — are the ground truth.
Those traces feed into what Chase calls a continual-learning loop. LangSmith ships a product called Engine: an agent that runs over traces and tries to update code, acting as an outer loop around the inner loop of an LLM calling tools. The same outer loop can write to memory, modify harness code, or post-train model weights. It's the same structure with three very different destinations.
Memory Is the Unsolved Layer
If routing is divisive, memory is honest. Chase calls it "massively unsolved" and "super, super early." LangChain launched an alpha for memory, then killed a commercial solution that never found product-market fit — the diagnosis being that it tried to be too general. Memory, he argues, is irreducibly domain-specific. What consumer apps need from memory is different from what a company harness needs, which is different from what legal or finance agents need.
The hard part isn't storage. When LangChain talked to companies, many were storing memory in a single file — a weird artifact of how primitive the tools currently are. The hard part is distillation: figuring out what's worth remembering from the stream of observations an agent makes. That requires meta-reflection, the ability to reflect on your own work and improve it. LLMs, Chase says, still struggle with this. LangChain has internal benchmarks for it via Engine, but no one has cracked it in production.
His prediction is that every product will have some memory component within a few years. His prescription is domain-specific learning agents: harnesses with specific instructions, tools, and sub-agents for navigating memory structures or distilling facts. The general-purpose memory product, on current evidence, is a graveyard.
Post-Training, Open Models, and the Cost-Ordered Ladder
Chase is "massively bullish" on vertical agents, and his practical advice for founders is a career in sequencing. For the first several years, don't touch model weights. "If you're getting started building a vertical agent, I would highly suggest you start at the harness layer," he said. "You do context engineering, tweak the harness, and then when you've got users, then you start tweaking the model in post-training."
That sequencing is strategic: context and harness work has the highest ROI early. Post-training only makes sense when volumes are high enough to justify the compute, the data pipeline, and the talent. He names a five-year horizon before most vertical agent founders should realistically consider post-training their own core model.
The "own your intelligence" ladder, from cheapest to most expensive, is a useful map:
| Level | What you control | Cost and difficulty | 
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Context only; off-the-shelf harness and model | Cheapest | 
| 2 | Tweak the harness | Moderate | 
| 3 | Post-train an open-weight model for your domain | Expensive | 
| 4 | Train from scratch | Rare; mostly labs | 
On the awkward question of whether companies like Harvey or Ramp are "really" building their own models or just fine-tuning someone else's — a claim Atlassian co-founder Michael Cannon-Brookes has publicly labeled "respectfully BS" — Chase is measured. Most are indeed post-training open-weight models. There's nothing illegitimate about that, he argues; the companies doing it have accumulated talent, data, and usage, extracted what they can from context and harness, and arrived at post-training by necessity. For now, he thinks post-training makes the most practical sense for the simpler tasks inside a larger harness — classification, extraction, summarization, guardrails — while whether the core agentic loop gets post-trained remains an open question.
On open models more broadly, his position splits the difference between the Twitter extremes. Frontier models keep getting better and cheaper, but high-volume, simple components are a good ROI case for post-trained open weights. On Chinese open models specifically, he dismisses much of the enterprise paranoia as unfounded — if you self-host or use a US provider like Fireworks, the idea that data flows back to Chinese labs is "ridiculous." He expects comparable US open-source models within 12 months, citing Nvidia's ecosystem work.
The Inference Market: What Chase Underestimated
The episode's title promises a view of the inference market, and Chase's most candid moment is an admission of his own history. Two things he "badly underestimated" from LangChain's early days: demand for inference and demand for coding agents. He quotes investor Eric Vishria of Benchmark — "everything just works, this market is so big" — and applies it to inference: "any inference will work." Fireworks, AWS, Crusoe, Nebius — they can all succeed on demand alone.
That's a useful counterweight to the market's current anxiety. As AI infrastructure stocks wobble and credit spreads widen on leveraged players, the narrative has shifted toward "who will be left standing." Chase's framework — the demand is so large that multiple providers will clear the bar — is essentially a bet on the size of the pie, not the efficiency of individual slices.
If he were an investor at a fund, he'd put money in three areas: inference, low-level infrastructure (the database, orchestration, and API layers that sit underneath agents), and vertical agents. He'd avoid customer support — there are dozens of providers funded at nine figures in the past 18 months, and the incumbents from Salesforce to Intercom won't yield quietly. He's also skeptical of robotics and humanoids, where funding currently exceeds capability by a wide margin. His advice to founders traces back to a line he attributes to Stripe's Patrick Collison, passed through Replit's Amjad Masad: build in unsexy areas with low AI penetration and high domain expertise. Three traits matter: exceptional technical talent, speed of execution, and brand. Being first matters more than ever, but without execution it's worthless — AutoGPT was the fastest-growing GitHub project of all time, and Chase notes he hasn't heard much from it since.
What to Watch
The unresolved tension in Chase's argument is his own history. He's confident that the harness and context layers are durable, yet he admits that model improvements force LangChain to delete product regularly. He passed on a gateway market he now calls enormous. He predicts a future where every product has a memory component, then admits no one knows what the answer for memory is. These are not contradictions — they're the shape of an industry still finding its feet. The frontier isn't the model; it's the layer of code and judgment that decides what to ask, what to remember, and what good looks like. For investors, two markers are worth tracking: whether post-training for the core agent loop becomes standard at companies like Harvey and Ramp, and whether the coding-agent spend ratio at enterprises rises toward salary parity or flattens as org design adapts. Chase's 30% figure is a self-described guesstimate from one company — but it's the kind of number that, multiplied across a market, becomes a valuation model.

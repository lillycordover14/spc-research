What happens when your AI agents start outnumbering your employees 144 to 1? You’ve got a serious identity crisis on your hands, and I’m not talking about the existential kind.

Here’s a stat that should keep IAM architects up at night: 79% of organisations have already shoved AI agents into production without a single written policy governing them, according to a 2025 EMA survey conducted in conjunction with Ory. 

And the machines are multiplying fast: Eighty-three per cent of large enterprises and 70% of mid-sized ones are already running agents in the wild, and up to 98% plan to join the party soon, according to the same source.

Meanwhile, non-human identities have surged to a mind-bending 144:1 ratio over human identities, a 44% increase from the 92:1 ratio observed in H1 2024, based on Entro Labs research. Gartner sees the trajectory clearly: 40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026, rocketing up from under 5% in 2025. That’s not a trend. That’s a hockey stick.

And yet most IAM stacks are hopelessly unprepared. 61.6% can’t handle the security demands. 62.0% buckle under the scale. 48.7% are unprepared for compliance. 59.4% lack the resiliency needed when something goes sideways, according to the same EMA survey.

If you’re running thousands of agents, each one authenticating, grabbing tokens, calling tools, chaining actions, your human-scale IAM isn’t just slow. It’s actively dangerous.

What you need is an IAM built for machine speed. Token issuance that doesn’t blink. Zero-trust enforcement without static secrets sprawled across config files. MCP-native authentication that understands agent-to-tool flows. And the ability to kill a rogue agent’s access in seconds, not hours or days.

To cut through the noise, we evaluated the top 7 enterprise agent IAM platforms using the criteria below.

How We Evaluated: Methodology & Criteria

We’re not just throwing darts at a vendor list. Every platform here was assessed against five criteria that matter when you’re fielding 1,000+ AI agents and your existing IAM is already wheezing.

Machine-Scale Token Issuance
 – Can the platform handle authentication and authorisation at the speed and concurrency thousands of autonomous agents demand? No human-scale bottlenecks allowed.

Fine-Grained Authorisation & MCP Support
 – Out-of-the-box RBAC, ReBAC, Zanzibar-style permissions, plus native Model Context Protocol authentication for agent-to-tool flows. If your agents can’t securely call MCP servers, you’re just building a very expensive chatbot.

Deployment Flexibility
 – Self-hosted, on-premises, private cloud, or fully managed. Data residency and compliance postures aren’t negotiable, and neither should your deployment model.

Zero-Trust Enforcement for Non-Human Identities
 – Just-in-time access, short-lived credentials, cryptographic identity. No static secrets lying around waiting to be exfiltrated. And yes, a kill-switch for agents that go rogue.

Governance, Visibility & Lifecycle Management
 – Agent onboarding, ownership assignment, deep observability for agents connected to or built on Ory, policy enforcement down to the tool-call level, and audit trails that hold up under scrutiny.

These five criteria align directly with what EMA’s research identifies as the top IAM priorities for agentic AI: Security, Scalability, Visibility, and Resiliency. 

Now, let’s get to the ranking.

1.
 
Ory
 
– The API-First Agent IAM Built for Machine Scale

Here’s the thing most IAM vendors won’t tell you: they built their platforms for humans first, then tried to bolt agents on afterwards. Ory didn’t. It’s open-source background, API-first, and architected for machine scale from day one.

The numbers are frankly staggering. Ory’s infrastructure manages 3.25 billion identities and processes 4.4 billion transactions per day across 33,000+ production deployments. It’s crossed 700 million downloads, and Docker pulls. 

And if you’ve ever wondered who handles authentication for OpenAI’s hundreds of millions of weekly active users. It’s Ory. OpenAI adopted the self-hosted Ory Enterprise License for Ory Hydra’s web-scale authorisation, enabling unprecedented logins per second with levels of data transparency and infrastructure flexibility not possible with other vendors. 

ChatGPT reached 1 million users in just five days, surpassed 100 million monthly active users by January 2023, and by February 2026, its user base had grown to approximately 900 million weekly users. That’s machine scale, proven in the most unforgiving production environment imaginable.

What does this mean for your agent fleet?

Machine-scale token issuance:
 Ory Talos is built on the same high-concurrency Go architecture as Ory Hydra, which powers OpenAI’s more than 900 million weekly active users, and is designed to handle the authentication throughput that large-scale agentic workflows demand. Ory Hydra and Ory Talos don’t sweat when your agents hammer it for tokens. It was designed for exactly that.

Fine-grained authorisation & MCP support:
 RBAC, ReBAC, Zanzibar-style permissions via Ory Keto. Native MCP authentication. Full OAuth and OIDC standards compliance. And everything runs headless and API-first; there’s no UI to render for agent identities, because agents don’t need a login page.

Deployment flexibility:
 Self-hosted. On-premises. Private cloud. Fully managed via Ory Network. This directly serves the 72.1% of organisations that prefer self-managed LLM deployment (on-premises or private cloud), which correlates with a stronger preference for self-managed IAM solutions. You choose the model; Ory adapts.

Zero-trust enforcement that actually means something:
 Ory Talos, launched in 2026, transforms traditional static API keys into programmable, hardened identities using Macaroon-based delegation and token derivation. If a child token gets stolen, it expires in 15 minutes (you set the timeframe). You control precisely what agents can access, for how long, and from where. No more “set it and forget it” secrets.

Ground-up observability and true composability:
 Trace-level log correlation, performance intelligence baked in. And because Ory is modular, you can deploy Ory Kratos, Ory Hydra, Ory Keto, and Ory Talos independently; you’re never locked into a monolithic stack you can’t reconfigure.

Ory Agent Security: Ory’s dedicated agent security layer provides a unified control plane for onboarding workflows, ownership assignment, behavioural observability, and an immediate kill-switch for rogue agents. Unlike bolt-on agent monitoring tools, it is native to the Ory stack, meaning policy enforcement and audit trails flow through the same infrastructure already handling billions of daily transactions.

Ory carries a 4.5 out of 5 average rating on G2 in Identity and Access Management Software based on 52 reviews. It is backed by Insight Partners, Balderton Capital, PHX Ventures, and IQT. 

Best for:
 Ory is the top choice for enterprises running thousands of AI agents that demand self-hosted, composable, machine-scale IAM with full API-first control. It’s also the right call if you want to enforce policy where agents actually execute actions. Ory is one of the few vendors that secures agents at the point of execution through Ory Agent Security.

Less ideal for:
 Organisations that require a fully-managed SaaS with minimal engineering overhead, though Ory Network offers a managed path, the tooling remains developer-first, and the configuration learning curve is real.

2.
 
Aembit
 
– The Independent Identity Broker

Aembit
 is an independent identity broker securing access for AI agents, MCP servers, and workloads across clouds, SaaS, and on-premise data centres using policy-based, secretless, just-in-time access with blended human-agent identity and real-time policy enforcement. 

Think of it as the diplomatic layer, authenticating agents, evaluating contextual policies, and brokering credentials across cloud, SaaS, and on-premises environments without ever touching a static secret.

Its MCP Identity Gateway handles agent-to-MCP-server authentication specifically. If you’ve already got your IDPs in place but lack a secure way for agents to talk to tools, Aembit fills that gap neatly.

Core capabilities:
 Secretless, just-in-time access brokering with policy-based contextual evaluation. Blended human-agent identity for workflows where both interact. A dedicated MCP Identity Gateway purpose-built for agent-to-tool authentication.

Strengths:
 Clean separation of concerns. Aembit brokering atop existing IDPs means no rip-and-replace. Its workload IAM pedigree translates directly to agent use cases without reinvention.

Limitations:
 It’s not a directory or identity provider. If you need native identity management, authentication, and full lifecycle governance under one roof, you’ll need complementary platforms.

Best for:
 Ideal for teams already deep in workload IAM who need a broker layer to extend secure access to AI agents without disrupting their current identity stack. 

Less ideal for:
 Organisations starting from scratch who want a unified platform covering identity creation through governance.

3.
 
Teleport
 
– Cryptographic Identity for All Identities

If you’re the sort of organisation where “who accessed what and when” isn’t just a compliance checkbox but an existential requirement, 
Teleport
 speaks your language. It unifies human, machine, and AI identities through cryptographic certificates and ephemeral privileges, claiming a 95% reduction in exposed attack surface with 100% auditable agentic workflows.

There are no shared secrets in Teleport’s model. A hardware root of trust issues cryptographic identities, and every session, agent included, gets recorded. Zero standing privilege means even if an agent is compromised, it has nothing to steal.

Core capabilities:
 Cryptographic identity issuance via hardware root of trust. Ephemeral, zero-standing-privilege access. Full session recording and audit trails for agentic workflows. MCP tooling integration.

Strengths:
 The infrastructure-first security model eliminates credential theft vectors entirely. It’s used by international stock exchanges and fintech leaders who demand immutable audit trails.

Limitations:
 Teleport is infrastructure-native, not SaaS plug-and-play. Organisations wanting out-of-the-box agent discovery dashboards and turnkey lifecycle management may need more engineering effort than anticipated to operationalise.

Best for:
 Best for fintech, stock exchanges, and heavily regulated verticals where infrastructure access boundaries and auditability are paramount. 

Less ideal for:
 Teams prioritising a lightweight SaaS experience with built-in agent discovery and governance tooling.

4.
 
Idira by Palo Alto Networks
 
– Modern PAM Meets Agentic Identity

Idira
 by Palo Alto Networks (formerly CyberArk) launched May 12, 2026, as a next-generation identity security platform built on CyberArk’s PAM heritage, integrating modern PAM, machine identity security, and agentic identity security in one platform. 

Features include zero standing privilege, just-in-time enforcement, AI-driven discovery, agent-to-MCP-server governance, and lifecycle management for human, machine, and agentic identities. The timing matters; this is PAM’s heritage meeting agentic identity head-on.

Idira’s 2026 Identity Security Landscape report, drawing from 2,900+ cybersecurity decision-makers worldwide, found that machine identities now outnumber humans 109:1 and that 9 out of 10 organisations suffered a successful identity-related breach in the past year. 

Those figures underscore why a platform that bakes modern PAM, machine identity security, and agentic identity into one roof matters.

Core capabilities:
 Idira’s Secure AI Agents offering includes agent discovery, MCP server registration, and an identity broker governing access to MCP servers; the platform delivers modern PAM with zero standing privilege and just-in-time enforcement across all identities (human, machine, agentic). An AI-driven discovery engine.

Strengths:
 Deep PAM heritage means least-privilege and just-in-time access aren’t bolt-ons; they’re baked into the DNA. For organisations already running Palo Alto Networks or CyberArk estates, Idira is the natural extension.

Limitations:
 Integrated suite architecture means less composability. You’re buying into the Palo Alto Networks way of doing things. Teams wanting open-source, API-first building blocks with modular swapping will find Idira more prescriptive.

Best for:
 Ideal for large enterprises already invested in the Palo Alto Networks ecosystem that need to extend the mature PAM discipline to thousands of AI agents. 

Less ideal for:
 Teams prioritising lightweight, composable, open-source building blocks over integrated suite cohesion.

5.
 
SailPoint Agentic Fabric
 
– Governance-First Agent Identity

SailPoint
 takes everything it knows about identity governance and extends it to the agentic workforce. The Agentic Fabric provides continuous discovery of AI agents and non-human identities, a unified identity graph, ownership assignment, and least-privilege policy enforcement down to the MCP tool-call level.

This governance-first approach directly addresses a painful reality: 60.5% of organisations still manage agents through a hybrid human/service account model, while 23.6% manage agents purely as service accounts and 10% manage them as human identities, rather than through dedicated agent identity frameworks. That’s duct tape and hope, not a strategy.

Core capabilities:
 Continuous agent and NHI discovery. A unified identity graph with ownership assignment. Access certification and least-privilege enforcement at the MCP tool-call level. Real-time automated response. An MCP Server for Identity Security Cloud customers. Integration with AWS, Azure, GCP, Salesforce, and Microsoft Copilot Studio Agent.

Strengths:
 SailPoint’s governance pedigree is highly regarded in the agent IAM space. Compliance-driven enterprises get access certification, audit trails, and policy enforcement they already trust. The August 2026 launch unified human, non-human, and AI agent identities under one security model.

Limitations:
 The governance-first model adds overhead. Lean engineering teams may find it heavy going. Organisations wanting a lightweight, developer-oriented, entirely self-hosted solution without the governance apparatus may chafe.

Best for:
 The go-to for compliance-driven verticals, financial services, healthcare, insurance, where access certification and auditable policy enforcement are non-negotiable. 

Less ideal for:
 Teams prioritising developer velocity and self-hosted composability over governance machinery.

6.
 
Microsoft Entra Agent ID
 
– The Ecosystem Extender

Microsoft Entra
 Agent ID extends the Entra identity backbone to AI agents through agent identity blueprints with parent-child policy inheritance, OAuth 2.0, MCP, and A2A protocol support. 

It works with both Microsoft and non-Microsoft platforms, AWS Bedrock, n8n, and others, though the deepest integrations naturally live inside Azure.

Available to all Microsoft Entra customers, advanced features require a Microsoft Agent 365 license.

Core capabilities:
 Agent identity blueprints with parent-child policy inheritance. OAuth 2.0, MCP, and A2A protocol support. Conditional access extended to agents. Lifecycle governance with audit trails. Cross-platform reach, including AWS Bedrock and n8n.

Strengths:
 Massive existing install base. Azure-centric enterprises can weave agent identity into their existing Conditional Access and governance fabric with minimal new tooling. Blueprints provide a clean policy-inheritance model.

Limitations:
 Deep Microsoft ecosystem integration means multi-cloud organisations running heterogeneous stacks may encounter friction. Advanced features are gated behind the Microsoft Agent 365 license, adding cost at a time when 51% of organisations already cite rising IAM costs as their biggest challenge.

Best for:
 The path of least resistance for Azure-centric enterprises that want agent identity slotted into existing Conditional Access policies. 

Less ideal for:
 Genuinely multi-cloud, vendor-neutral stacks that chafe at ecosystem gravity.

7.
 
Okta
 
for AI Agents – Identity-First, Vendor-Neutral

Okta
 for AI Agents became generally available on April 30, 2026, delivering agent discovery, onboarding into Universal Directory, short-lived credential enforcement, and a kill-switch for rogue agents, all in a vendor-neutral package.

The Agent Gateway governs MCP server access regardless of the agent’s build platform, and Okta actively contributes to the OpenID-JAG standard. 

That commitment to interoperability matters when you’re dealing with the IAM fragmentation that plagues most enterprises: organisations already use an average of three IAM platforms (including CIAM solutions), with 34% using four or more, and 47% worry about rising or unpredictable costs.

Core capabilities:
 Agent discovery and registration into Universal Directory. Short-lived credential enforcement. Agent Gateway for MCP server governance across build platforms. Lifecycle management with a kill switch. Active ID-JAG standards contribution.

Strengths:
 Market-leading IAM with native agent identity features that don’t feel bolted on. The vendor-neutral posture and open-standards commitment appeal to heterogeneous enterprises. The kill-switch provides a genuine operational safety net.

Limitations:
 Predominantly SaaS-delivered. Organisations requiring on-premises or self-hosted deployment won’t find a native path. And Okta’s platform breadth can mean higher per-agent costs at scale compared to open-source alternatives.

Best for:
 The strongest vendor-neutral SaaS option on this list for organisations comfortable with SaaS delivery and Okta’s pricing model. 

Less ideal for:
 The 72.1% of organisations that prefer self-managed IAM, or for cost-conscious enterprises where per-identity pricing at machine scale gets uncomfortable fast.

The Reality Check: Gaps, Costs, and Fragmentation

Before you pick a platform, let’s get real about the state of enterprise IAM. The stats below are from the same 2025 EMA survey cited earlier.

Fifty-eight per cent of organisations are juggling three or more IAM solutions, and 51% cite rising costs as their biggest challenge with IAM deployments; approximately 68% are looking to bring their IAM deployment in-house instead of relying on SaaS. Adding a separate agent IAM could compound both fragmentation and budget pressure simultaneously.

Approximately 52.8% of organisations want an all-in-one IAM platform, but only 24% have achieved one. Most still stitch together best-of-breed components, and agent IAM adoption will widen that gap unless platforms consolidate human and agent identity effectively.

The self-hosting preference is also striking: 72.1% favor self-managed LLM deployment, which correlates strongly with appetite for self-hosted IAM (68.2%). That preference directly shapes this ranking.

Then there are the operational silos. On average, organisations report three teams own the security and compliance issues around agentic identity, with the most frequently cited teams being IT/Security, Identity/IAM Teams, and Development/Engineering Teams — a multi-team approach that creates potential operational silos.

In financial services specifically, only 29.7% of financial sector organisations have agentic AI running externally at scale in production versus 40.6% in the broader market; 64.8% of financial organisations remain in limited deployments or pilot programs. 

Yet 67.6% of financial sector organisations express a strong preference for an all-in-one SaaS IAM platform, compared to 50.4% across other sectors. — a tension between readiness and appetite that any vendor strategy needs to acknowledge.

The bottom line: no single platform covers every edge case. Evaluate based on your top priority, EMA’s ranked list is Security, Scalability, Visibility, and Resiliency, and your deployment model preference before committing.

Final Take: The Agent IAM Stakes Are Rising

The numbers don’t leave much room for debate. Non-human identities are exploding at a 144:1 ratio over human identities. Gartner predicts 40% of enterprise apps will embed task-specific AI agents by the end of 2026. Purpose-built agent IAM isn’t a nice-to-have; it’s an operational survival requirement.

Anchor your evaluation on clear criteria. The five we applied are a defensible starting point and a realistic assessment of your deployment model, governance burden, and tolerance for engineering overhead. 

The market is moving fast. Keep watching developments in MCP standards, delegate-token hardening like Ory Talos’s Macaroon model, purpose-built control planes like Ory Agent Security, and the emerging OAuth-for-agents specifications. Because the best platform today may not hold the crown in 12 months, and with thousands of agents already running in your infrastructure, you can’t afford to wait.

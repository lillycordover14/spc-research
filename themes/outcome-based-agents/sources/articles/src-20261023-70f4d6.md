  From Machine Identity to Disruption: What Leaders Need to Prepare For | DisrupTV Episode 454
Jeff Jonas, David Bray, and Jeff Cole explored how AI agents are changing identity, security, and the pace of disruption—and what leaders can do to respond.
Organizations are entering a machine-scale world. AI agents are multiplying, making decisions, accessing systems, and acting on behalf of people and businesses. At the same time, new technologies are reshaping industries faster than many established companies can adapt.
In a recent episode of DisrupTV, hosts Vala Afshar and Ray Wang spoke with Jeff Jonas, Founder and CEO of Senzing; David Bray, Distinguished Chair of the Accelerator at the Stimson Center and Principal and CEO of LDA Ventures Inc.; and Jeff Cole, author of Disrupters at the Gate: When Visionaries, Trailblazers, and Two Guys in a Garage Turn the World Upside Down.
Their conversations ranged from governing AI agents to preparing for disruption. One theme connected them all: leaders need to anticipate change—and build systems and organizations that can respond.

AI agents make identity a business-critical issue

Jonas described identity resolution as understanding who is who—and how people, accounts, records, and other entities are related—even when information is incomplete or inconsistent. As AI agents take on more work, that capability becomes essential. Organizations need a reliable way to understand not only their human users, but also the agents operating within their systems.
The challenge extends beyond identifying an agent. Organizations also need to know who authorized it, what it is allowed to do, and how to stop it if it exceeds those permissions. Jonas and Bray discussed the importance of linking agents to accountable people and setting boundaries such as data access and spending limits.
Bray emphasized that permission should be explicit and limited to the relevant systems and information. An agent helping with retirement planning, for example, should not automatically have access to family photos or bank accounts. He also argued that authorization should be enforced separately from the agent itself, rather than relying on an agent to accurately report its own permissions.
Together, these ideas point toward a governance model built around identity, authorization, and the ability to intervene.

Build resilience for a machine-scale environment

The guests described a world in which automated systems can act at great speed—and where defensive capabilities must keep pace. Bray noted that some AI capabilities can run locally, while federated approaches can allow data to remain where it is rather than requiring it to be copied into a central store.
For leaders, the broader lesson is to consider resilience across both digital and physical systems. Bray urged CEOs and boards to identify the systems and resources that would be exceptionally difficult to restore if they failed. Redundancy matters, but it needs to be meaningful: systems located close together may share the same exposure.
That makes resilience a leadership issue, not just a technical one. CIOs and CISOs have important roles, but executives across the organization should understand what must keep running, what could interrupt it, and how recovery would work.
The discussion also connected privacy with trust. Bray argued that people should have a meaningful say in how their data is used. For companies deploying AI, involving customers in those choices can be more than a compliance measure: it can help strengthen long-term relationships.
Disruption is different from innovation
Cole drew a distinction between innovation and disruption. Innovation improves an existing approach. Disruption often comes from unexpected places and changes the underlying business model or the structure of an industry.
Established companies can innovate successfully and still be vulnerable to disruption. Cole’s examples included Kodak, which invented the digital camera but did not build its future around it, and Xerox PARC, whose breakthroughs included technologies that helped shape modern computing.
The lesson is not simply to invest in new technology. It is to examine how a company creates value—and where customers are dissatisfied. Cole pointed to the music industry’s rigid sales model and the poor experiences many people had with taxis as openings that challengers were able to address.
Disruption, in other words, can begin with an unmet customer need. Companies that assume their current success will continue may miss the signals.
A practical starting point for leaders
Cole’s advice was to expect to be disrupted. That does not mean predicting exactly which competitor or technology will change an industry. It means regularly examining the organization’s assumptions, vulnerabilities, and customer experience.
A useful leadership review can ask:

What would be hardest to restore if it failed?
Where are we relying on systems or locations that share the same risk?
Which customer needs are we not meeting well?
How are AI agents identified, authorized, and monitored?
Can people understand and influence how their data is used?

These questions bring together the two conversations: prepare for technological change, and make sure the organization can govern and withstand it.
Prepare for change without losing sight of people
The episode’s discussions of AI agents and business disruption arrived at a shared conclusion: the pace of change is accelerating, but preparation is possible. Organizations can invest in identity and authorization, strengthen resilience, and listen more closely to customers—all while keeping people accountable and involved.
The next wave of disruption may come from an unexpected source. The companies best positioned to respond will be those that are already asking what could change, what must be protected, and how they can adapt before change forces the issue.
Final Takeaways

Treat identity as core infrastructure. Organizations need to understand and govern both human and machine identities as AI agents become more common.
Set clear boundaries for agents. Define what each agent may access, what actions it may take, and who is accountable for it—with a way to stop it when necessary.
Build resilience beyond the IT department. Identify critical systems and make sure backups and recovery plans account for shared geographic and operational risks.
Make privacy and permission part of the design. Give people meaningful control over how their data is used to build trust in AI-enabled services.

Expect disruption—and look for customer pain points. Today’s market leaders should keep examining where customers are underserved and how a new entrant might address those needs.
The central message for leaders: prepare for change before it arrives, while keeping accountability, resilience, and people at the center.

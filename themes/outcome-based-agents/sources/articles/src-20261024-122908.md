How to Price AI Agent Seats With Variable Usage That Actually Holds&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
Per-seat pricing worked for Salesforce because every rep clicked roughly the same number of buttons. An AI agent that drafts, researches, and executes tasks on its own doesn't play by that rule, and copying the old model is quietly costing founders their margin.

Quick Summary - TLDR:

A single heavy user can consume 50 times the compute of a light user on the exact same seat, which per-seat pricing cannot see

Anthropic, OpenAI, and Intercom all ship a hybrid: a seat fee for access plus metered charges for actual agent work

Intercom's Fin charges per resolution, not per seat, because support volume has nothing to do with headcount

A blended seat-plus-usage model needs a usage floor built into the seat price or power users get a free ride on light users' money

Pure consumption pricing scares procurement teams who need a budgetable number, which is why almost nobody ships it alone

Here's the math that breaks every seat-based AI pricing model eventually. Say you charge $40 a seat a month for an AI coding agent. One developer uses it to autocomplete a few functions a week. Another runs it as an autonomous agent that opens fifteen pull requests a day, chews through a context window the size of a small library, and calls out to three different tools per task. Both pay $40. One costs you maybe $2 in inference. The other costs you $100 or more, and you're losing $60 a month to keep them as a customer.

That gap is the whole problem with how to price AI agent seats with variable usage, and it's not a hypothetical. It's the default outcome of pricing an agent like it's a SaaS login.

Per-seat pricing was built on an assumption that held for two decades: usage per seat is roughly flat. A Salesforce rep logs in, updates some records, logs out. A Slack user sends messages at a human pace. The variance between your heaviest user and your lightest user might be 3x, maybe 5x on a bad day. You price for the average and the law of large numbers smooths it out.

An AI agent doesn't behave like a person clicking buttons. It behaves like compute, and compute scales with the task, not the headcount. A support agent handling a product launch spike might trigger ten times the ticket volume of a normal week. A coding agent asked to refactor a legacy module might burn through hundreds of thousands of tokens on one job that a human would've skipped entirely. The seat stays constant. The cost behind it doesn't.
 

How To Price An AI Agent Per Seat Versus Per Task Right Now

A customer whose AI support agent clears a 4,000-ticket backlog in one weekend can rack up API costs exceeding what they paid for the entire quarter on a $49 seat plan. Intercom's Fin charges about $0.99 per resolved conversation instead. -

This is why Intercom doesn't charge per seat for its Fin AI agent. It charges per resolution, a flat fee each time Fin actually resolves a customer conversation without a human stepping in. Intercom made that call because support ticket volume tracks company growth and seasonality, not how many agents are logged into the console. A company with five support reps and a Black Friday spike needs pricing that moves with the spike. A seat count never will.

The hybrid model that's actually winning

What's replacing straight seat pricing isn't straight usage pricing either. It's a hybrid: a base seat fee that buys access and a usage allotment, plus metered charges once that allotment runs out. OpenAI's enterprise ChatGPT tiers work this way, and so does Anthropic's Claude for Work, which bundles a seat with a usage pool and bills overages separately for teams that blow past it.

The logic is straightforward once you see it. The seat fee does what seat fees have always done: it gives finance a predictable line item, gives sales a clean number to quote, and gives the buyer something that feels familiar next to every other software line on their budget. The usage layer does what seats can't: it makes the heavy user actually pay for being heavy, instead of subsidizing them off the backs of everyone else on the plan.

Get the floor wrong and the hybrid model fails in exactly the same way pure seat pricing does. If your bundled usage allotment is generous enough to cover your heaviest users for free, you've just rebuilt the $40 seat with extra steps. The allotment has to be sized against your actual usage distribution, not your average user, and definitely not your lightest one. Pull your own data: what does the 90th percentile user actually consume in a month? Price the included allotment near the median, not the mean, because a handful of outliers drag the mean somewhere no normal customer lives.

Why nobody ships pure usage pricing

If usage is the honest way to charge, why doesn't everyone just meter everything and skip the seat fee entirely? Because procurement doesn't buy honest, it buys predictable. A finance team approving a six-figure AI contract wants a number they can put in a budget line and defend to a CFO next quarter. "It depends on how much your team uses it" is not a number. It's a risk they'll push back on, or worse, cap artificially by rationing usage among their own employees, which kills the exact adoption you're trying to drive.

That's the real argument against pure consumption pricing: it just shifts unpredictability onto procurement's P&L.

What this looks like in practice

Structuring the hybrid well comes down to a few decisions, and none of them are generic.

Pick the metering unit that actually tracks cost, not the one that's easiest to explain in a sales deck. Token counts are the literal cost driver but mean nothing to a buyer. Resolutions, completed tasks, or successful actions map to value the customer can picture, even if you're converting them from token costs behind the scenes. Intercom's per-resolution model works because a support lead understands exactly what a "resolution" is worth to their team. Nobody internally benchmarks token throughput.

Set overage rates that discourage abuse without punishing success. If a customer's usage spikes because their business is growing, that's a signal to upsell them into a bigger seat tier with a larger bundled allotment, not to bleed them dry on overage fees until they churn. Treat the overage meter as a pricing signal you read monthly, not a punishment you set once and forget.

Separate the admin seat from the usage seat. A lot of AI agent tools get sold into a department where three people configure and monitor the agent and two hundred people are affected by what it does downstream. Charging per "seat" in that scenario is a category error. The admin console access is worth a flat fee. The agent's actual work, the thing creating value for two hundred people, should be metered against outcomes, not gated behind two hundred logins nobody will actually use.

Build the usage dashboard before you build the pricing page. Customers on a hybrid model will not tolerate surprise overage bills, and the single fastest way to churn an enterprise account is a finance team opening an invoice they didn't see coming. Give them real-time visibility into where they sit against their bundled allotment, the same way AWS shows a running cost estimate before the bill lands. That transparency is what makes customers accept variable pricing at all.

None of this is solved by a formula you can lift wholesale from another company's pricing page. Intercom's resolution-based model fits support because resolutions are a natural, countable unit of value. A coding agent or a research agent needs its own unit, one that tracks what the agent actually finishes, not how many times someone opened the app. Find that unit first. The pricing structure follows from it, not the other way around.

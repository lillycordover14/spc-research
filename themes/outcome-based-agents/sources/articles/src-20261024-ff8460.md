Pace will present 
KNOTTING, TYING, FOLDING
, an exhibition of sculptures by Lynda Benglis, at its 540 West 25th Street gallery from September 11 through October 24. Alongside glazed ceramic, bronze, pleated, and paper works created by Benglis between 1972 and 2024, the presentation will also spotlight rarely exhibited glass sculptures from 1985.
Since the late 1960s, Benglis has developed her lively, flowing forms across a wide range of materials—from beeswax, latex, and polyurethane foam to later innovations with plaster, gold, vaporized metals, glass, ceramics, and paper. Emerging amid the rise of Postminimalism, she set herself apart from her contemporaries with an enthusiastic embrace of vivid color and sensuous, organic surfaces. She has continuously pushed the boundaries of painting and sculpture over the course of her career, engaging in a radical investigation of sensorial perception.
KNOTTING, TYING, FOLDING
 will feature a robust range of sculptures created by Benglis over more than five decades, illuminating her use of diverse methods and materials. The earliest work in the show is 
Sparkle Knot IV
 (1972), a knotted tube of wire mesh covered in plaster, paints, and sparkles. 
Sparkle Knot IV
 signals the initiation of processes that Benglis would go on to transform and develop across further series also on view in Pace’s presentation, from the vibrant gold-leafed and metallized pleats and glass works of the 1980s, to the painterly, extruded and folded ceramics of the 2010s, all the way to her more recent 
Elephant Necklace
 and paper works, where knotting gives way to open, expansive forms.
“My involvement with surfacing of objects usually swings back and forth between extremes of coloration and texturing,” Benglis has said. “I always go from one pole to the other—it is one way of discovering things about the surface and color’s relationship to form.”
A selection of pleated wall-mounted sculptures from 1982 through 1990, in which “the light, and the change of light, was a goal,” in the artist’s words, will figure prominently in the exhibition. For these works, Benglis first took accordion-pleated wire mesh—a process she has likened to creating paper fans as a child—before knotting, tying, and twisting it into dynamic configurations that were then covered in plaster and gold leaf or, later, sprayed with vaporized metal. The wall-mounted works continue with a selection of paper sculptures from 2016 through 2020, which are composed of chicken wire covered with handmade paper that may be painted or saturated with sparkles. “They’re objects made of wet paper that’s been stretched, like a skin, on wire that I’ve contoured,” Benglis has explained. “I make marks on the paper of various sizes, densities, and characters. I realized that I make drawings the way I do because I see a surface as being spatial.”
A group of lesser-known sand cast glass works from 1985 will also feature in 
KNOTTING, TYING, FOLDING
. For these unique sculptures, Benglis laid colorful glass shards, enamel eyes, and pigment-like powdered glass inside a shape molded in sand, into which she poured molten black or clear glass. Using Kevlar gloves and sticks, she then handled the molten glass in a highly physical process that she has called “a play-dance activity,” bending and tying it into knots. The resulting works are playful and abstract, gesturing toward both living beings—as indicated by titles like 
Desert Pupfish
and 
Gila Top Minnow
—and celestial bodies, with flecks of luminescent color on black ground.
Other free-standing sculptures in the exhibition include large-scale, reflective bronze works from recent years, in which rounded organic shapes seem to undulate and shift as viewers move around them, as well as small ceramic works from the 2010s, with Benglis’s signature folds, loops, and cascades embellished with expressionistic swathes of glaze. “The ceramics, for me,” Benglis said, “were about how to make gestures, an architecture of gestures, and how to make a painting physically—how to present it so that they're coming together and you have a kind of explosion or implosion of forms.”
KNOTTING, TYING, FOLDING
 will also highlight the multi-part sculpture 
Elephant Necklace
 (2016), a glazed ceramic masterwork that comprises 37 abstract elements rendered in black monochrome and arranged in a circle on the floor. Recently exhibited by Pace at the first edition of Art Basel Qatar, the work is part of the artist’s 
Elephant Necklace
 series, in which she explores movement, fluctuation, and organic growth through biomorphic looping forms.
This presentation follows the recent exhibition 
Encounters: Giacometti x Lynda Benglis
 at the Barbican Centre in London—organized as part of a series pairing contemporary artists’ work with Alberto Giacometti’s sculptures. Next year, Benglis will be the subject of a major retrospective at Kunstmuseum Basel (March 13 – August 1, 2027), which will then travel to Tate Modern (September 30, 2027 – March 5, 2028) and Louisiana Museum of Modern Art, Denmark (dates to be announced), as well as a solo presentation, 
Lynda Benglis:
 
Elemental
, at the Newcomb Art Museum of Tulane University (January 20 – May 20, 2027), which will examine the formative role of Louisiana’s Gulf Coast on her artistic development. Her work will also feature in the group exhibition 
Frozen Gestures
 at NSU Art Museum Fort Lauderdale (November 13, 2026 – April 11, 2027).

About the Artist
Since the 1960s, Lynda Benglis has been celebrated for her fearless exploration of forms, which are simultaneously playful and visceral, organic and, abstract. Benglis began her career in the midst of the Postminimal movement, pushing the traditions of painting and sculpture into new territories. She initiated several bodies of work in the late ’60s and early ’70s that set the course for her subsequent practice. Her wax paintings, which began with brushed skin-like layers of pigmented beeswax and dammar resin progressed, in one series, to the use of a blowtorch as a kind of brush, manipulating colors into a marbleized surface that fought against the constraints of the lozenge-shaped Masonite panels. The impulse to see these forms flow beyond the structure of a traditional support led Benglis to embrace pigmented latex, which she began pouring directly onto the floor. The use of gravity and her body in the latex pours invoked Jackson Pollock’s process, a connection immortalized in the February 27, 1970 issue of 
Life
 magazine, which featured Benglis at work.

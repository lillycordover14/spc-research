London flat prices have plummeted at their fastest pace in 17 years, with owners losing £50,000 on average since last summer, new figures show.
Inner London flat prices fell by 8.1pc in the year to August – the steepest drop since 2009 and a five-fold increase on the 1.6pc decline nationally, according to Telegraph analysis of Land Registry data.
The numbers show how a toxic cocktail of high mortgage rates, stamp duty and the 
Government’s buy-to-let crackdown
 have hit the capital’s flat market far harder than any other part of the national property market.
The average sale price of flats across London’s 15 inner boroughs, which stretch from Newham to Wandsworth, has slumped by £48,968 since June 2025 – erasing more than 12 years of house price growth.
The average inner London flat sold in August for £522,200, the lowest price recorded since July 2015.

The figures come a month after Andy Burnham announced plans for 
a new Help to Buy-style scheme
 to assist first-time buyers purchase new-build homes, with details to come in Wednesday’s Budget.
But the numbers show buying flats in the capital can come with substantial financial losses.
Those who purchased apartments at the peak of the market in September 2022 have seen the value of their homes fall by £71,935 in the last four years.
Figures from the Office for National Statistics earlier this week showed average UK house prices rose by 0.9pc to £273,000 in the year to August.
But the headline number masks major variations between regions and property types, with flats performing far worse than houses and values under most pressure in the least affordable parts of the country.

In the City of Westminster, which has some of the highest prices in the country, flat prices are falling twice as fast as they did during the worst of the financial crisis.
Sale prices were down by 15pc year-on-year in August, compared with the 7.4pc drop recorded in 2009.
Higher borrowing costs as a result of the war in Iran, which has sent oil prices, inflation and expectations for interest rate rises soaring since the end of February, have hit buyers hardest in the places where prices were already high in proportion to earnings.
Bank of England figures show the average rate on a new two-year fixed-rate mortgage has soared from 3.98pc in March to 4.69pc in August alone, driving up the annual cost of taking out a £200,000 loan by £960.

These costs matter most for first-time buyers and lower-income buyers who are more likely to be purchasing flats than houses.
The flat market has also suffered from the loss of buy-to-let investors.
Small-scale landlords have been selling up in droves following reductions in tax relief on buy-to-let mortgages and Labour’s renters’ rights bill, which has scrapped so-called no-fault evictions.
A stamp duty surcharge on additional homes – which began as three percentage points in 2016 and was raised to five by Rachel Reeves in 2024 – has also hit demand from new buy-to-let investors in the capital particularly hard.
A combination of higher prices in the capital and higher stamp duty bands introduced in 2014 means that even normal home movers face prohibitively high tax charges to purchase homes in London.
Buying an average inner London flat in August incurred a stamp duty bill of £16,110, nearly 12 times the £1,382 bill on an average UK flat.

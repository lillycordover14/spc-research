Back-office processes were built with people as the default orchestration layer. Salesforce execs debate the next phase.

[page text]
How can agents orchestrate more process while keeping people in the judgment loop? That was the topic debated at last week’s Salesforce World Tour London event, which dove deeper into the question of how agentic AI can re-invent business processes from the ground up while keeping humans in the loop.

The session paired Zahra Bahrololoumi, EVP and CEO of Salesforce UK and Ireland, with Simon Mulcahy, CEO of Agentforce Operations and Chief Business Officer and GM of AI Labs. Mulcahy was a long-time Salesforce staffer who returned to the firm through its acquisition of Regrello, an AI-native process automation firm where he was president. Salesforce acquired the company in October 2025 and its technology now underpins Agentforce Operations, which targets middle- and back-office work across supply chain, procurement and finance.

Mulcahy has been exploring how improved integration, bolstered by agents, is enabling a state shift beyond existing Process. Improvement approaches like Business Process Mapping/Modeling (BPM), Process Mining, process capture, and Robotic Process Automation (RPA). It’s not like these are going away, but rather taking advantage of recent innovations to rethink process improvement and automation from first principles.

The human bottleneck

Mulcahy traces his insight back to COVID supply-chain disruptions. He recalls that a group of experts convened by the UK and US governments concluded that the bottleneck in the back office was not just IT systems but also people:

It’s people doing all the work. It’s people swivel-chairing between systems, re-keying information, printing stuff out, reconciling. All of that work is basically how back offices work, and during COVID, when you send everybody home, it all stopped.

He also saw how de-globalization and shifting labor costs strained supply chains, and came to see them as constraints on the change every business faces. He partnered with Regrello CEO Aman Naimat, a former McKinsey advisor and Stanford lecturer, to develop tools to help mitigate these constraints.

Mulcahy describes how businesses approached this problem before ChatGPT arrived in November 2022 as human-first by design:

The whole mental model was that it’s humans who are effectively the organizational structure of a business. At the heart of any company you’ve got your core master data, perhaps your ERP system, you’ve got a CRM, and you’ve got other core systems. But around that is just work, and that’s humans doing all of that work. How that work happens is all designed human first, and it’s predicated on individuals project managing a process from beginning to end.

In a world where it’s AI first, that’s no longer true. The human doesn’t orchestrate the end-to-end process. The human is an important key decision maker, approver, designer in that, maybe on the loop or occasionally in the loop, but isn’t the driver of the loop. That’s a really big architectural shift, and it’s not just swapping some technology out in the same process. You actually rethink how things work.

For her part, Bahrololoumi says customer conversations about the agentic enterprise are not about the technology. They center on four things: getting data in order, process, bringing people along through change management, and governance. That last one takes in security, auditability, observability and traceability. Bahrololoumi unpacks process in more detail to highlight the human bottlenecks today:

What we are discovering and exposing with Agentforce Operations and Regrello is that processes today have all been geared and designed around human capacity. So we have to change our thinking, because by exposing it, we are able to see that it doesn’t need to operate that way anymore. We are no longer restricted by human capacity.

In the middle office, she points to know-your-customer (KYC) checks and client onboarding at banks. If they take more than a day, something is fundamentally wrong, and root cause analysis can show they should take minutes. In the back office, cheap labor arbitrage meant they met every problem or surge by adding more people. Bahrololoumi says:

We have a hypothesis: no-one should be paying for bums on seats to do Business Process Outsourcing, because a customer has already codified a process, and they’re probably buying an outcome, and they’re using labor arbitrage to get that at the cheapest possible point.

Balancing people and agents

Listening to them made me wonder how companies might strike the right balance, given the industry's cultural bias toward running people and machines at maximum utilization. My thesis is that this underpins the trend of cutting people for AI, reduces the free time needed to see even bigger opportunities to rethink processes, and constrains the ability to pivot in response to unforeseen events. For example, some routine work that looks inefficient might help people form a greater understanding of how a process behaves. Also, a day spent making hard judgment calls seems likely to exhaust a different kind of mental effort than more routine work.

Bahrololoumi responds that nobody knows the right balance yet. She recalls the offshoring era, when companies measured themselves by ratios such as 10% onshore and 90% offshore, and says the industry isn’t there with agents. Salesforce is looking at the question function by function. Bahrololoumi says:

I can say with confidence we can expose all of that now, but I can’t tell you the punchline, to say that in finance operations it should be 30% human and 70% agents. I think it’s a case-by-case basis, and we will learn a bit more as we delve deeper.

Mulcahy meanwhile makes the case for a measured approach to find the right set point for continuous improvement rather than a one-time fix:

People have this reductive view of the world that it’s broken and it needs fixing. It’s not. It’s just, bearing in mind the tools we have available to us today, how can we make it better? And how do we never stop? The question isn’t how do we fix it and make it correct. The question is how do we continuously improve. In the manufacturing universe, that’s Kaizen, Six Sigma. We should be applying that type of thing with the tools available.

Half of the processes that we are still making do with were designed so long ago by people, mere mortals, who at the time had the best tools and the best training at their fingertips. They made something up, and for some reason we just said, that’ll do.

Capturing what people know

One critical opportunity today is to find better ways to capture tribal wisdom that traditional process mapping and discovery approaches have so far missed. Mulcahy gives the example of employees still running a process from a spreadsheet built by a colleague named Mary who left years ago, without fully realizing why it was organized that way or whether it could be improved without breaking anything. He argues that much of how companies work is tribal knowledge and folklore, treated like a wing nut that only ever needs two more cranks to the right, when the better question is what the process is really trying to achieve.

Mulcahy argues that companies underestimate what they don’t capture:

Your data and your processes are your crown jewels. That is true for every company. It’s not just the data about the customer, it’s the metadata and how that data is used, and that is just as important. Think of it in the context of the back office as well. How you work is data, and yet most organizations cannot tell you how Mary actually does her job. How much of your business is actually folklore, unwritten processes? These are incredibly important because they define your output as a company.

Many of these processes are in spreadsheets and emails and phone call logs. They’re not even in data. They are informal. How much informal data is in the company as a percentage? North of two thirds. It’s your data, but you can’t do anything with it because you’ve got no access to it.

He says Salesforce has spent years building a single view of each customer across every front-office touchpoint. The middle and back office, by contrast, hold what he calls a fortune of data that the business can't access.

Re-deploying people

Bahrololoumi doesn’t directly take up the part of my question about the deluge of companies citing AI as a reason for layoffs. In fairness, it’s a nuanced topic, with AI layoffs sounding better on earnings calls than overhiring or HR-retraining limitations. Bahrololoumi says that in her conversations with customers, none of them have asked her how to reduce their workforce. They ask how to grow, and how to improve the employee experience as well as the customer experience. Mulcahy adds:

In many cases, just by dint of human first processes, there is inefficiency in the system. Humans are just used in inefficient ways, but they can be redeployed into areas where they can deliver vastly more capability. There will be mechanical benefits of that: speed, efficiency, accuracy. But what’s really interesting is as you do that, your business starts to accelerate. Nobody cuts their way to success, but you do absolutely accelerate your way to success.

Bahrololoumi points to Simplyhealth, where Agentforce took on administrative tasks so customer service agents could focus on more involved medical cases. She says those staff are now paid more. Simplyhealth says its human agents now serve 50 members a day, up from 25 two years ago, and email handling time has dropped from 12 minutes to one.

She also cites Bobbi, the agent Thames Valley Police and Hampshire & Isle of Wight Constabulary use for non-emergency contacts, which 
diginomica covered at the six-month mark
. Bahrololoumi says:

It’s resolving 75% of non-emergency conversations completely autonomously. What I didn’t say in the keynote is that, because of the trust layer and the filtering, it’s also detecting through language one high-risk case a day, which in some cases is leading to arrests. And it’s releasing 3,000 hours of policing time for them to redeploy into frontline policing. That equation in action is all about releasing capacity for higher-value tasks and being able to deliver better outcomes.

Process owners and deterministic engines

Mulcahy says the interface to enterprise software is shifting from screens of boxes to conversation. With Agentforce Operations, a process owner can ask to build supplier onboarding, answer the system’s questions about documentation and rules, and end up with a working process without writing code. IT then handles governance and putting it into production.

Behind that conversational front end sits a deterministic engine, and Mulcahy explains why AI alone isn’t enough:

[AI] is a probabilistic engine, and in many cases in the business world, a probabilistic engine is not good enough. If you have 10 steps sequentially, and they’re each 99% accurate, that’s 0.99 to the power of 10, which means one in 10 instances is wrong. You don’t want that in certain calculations: tax, audit, finance.

So there are places where AI on its own, leveraging tokens, is very powerful, but there are places where that’s either too expensive or not accurate enough, not explainable enough, not auditable enough. That’s where you need the power of a highly deterministic system, which is where Salesforce has come from. The marriage of the two, blended, is basically the value proposition of Salesforce today and moving forward.

My take

The chat interface aspects of AI and agentic tools tend to get the most play. But it’s also important to remember that the transformer models underpinning LLMs started as an experiment to build a better translator between human languages. Framed that way, it suggests an opportunity to think about how they might be applied to translating a lot of other things that have previously been loosely coupled. A key one is the process knowledge and tribal knowledge that has thus far been hard to capture, encode, and improve upon.

One common trope these days is that companies frequently associate massive layoff announcements with AI. While this might make sense for optimizing finances for the short term, it seems likely to undermine the foundation required for the future.

The open question is how and where to expose humans, especially new hires, to the mundane things that might be simple to automate so they can form better mental models on how things work and what are the opportunities to rethink process or pivot quickly when supply chains or market conditions change. This speaks to the age-old question in process improvement circles of the tension between making existing processes go faster versus redesigning a better process from the ground up. Finding the latter seems like it will require more people, and especially experienced ones who will cost more than new hires or business process outsourcing firms.

The journey toward finding the right set point between better agentic tools, data integration and rookie and veteran employees is the harder problem ahead.

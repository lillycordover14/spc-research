Choosing an AI chatbot for customer service comes down to three variables: which tickets you want resolved without a human, how the vendor bills for a resolution, and whether your helpdesk-native agent can reach the systems that hold the answer. Tools differ most in write access to order, refund and account APIs, and in how they count a resolved conversation.

A chatbot is the wrong investment when ticket volume is low or the knowledge base is unstructured. The right pick depends on volume, stack and risk tolerance.

AI chatbots for customer service: The short answer

An AI agent that reads your knowledge base and calls order and refund APIs fits teams with high volumes of repetitive tickets. A rule-based chatbot only suits a few scripted flows. Under per-resolution pricing, the automated resolution rate largely sets your bill.

We recommend starting with a helpdesk-native agent such as Intercom Fin or Zendesk AI Agents when your tickets already live there, because handoff history and permissions come built in.

Netguru designs and builds custom support agents that integrate with helpdesks, order systems, and messaging channels. Go custom only when write actions or data rules outgrow the vendor. Route legal questions, billing disputes, and upset customers to humans.

How do AI chatbots work?

An AI chatbot passes each customer message to a large language model, which drafts a reply from retrieved knowledge base content and calls your APIs when the customer needs something done. How these components fit together, from the retrieval layer to the API connections, is a question of 
AI chatbot system architecture
, and the right design depends on your integration needs.

The request path, left to right:

Channel and helpdesk integration: the message arrives from chat, email or in-app, with ticket and customer context attached.

Retrieval: retrieval augmented generation embeds the question and pulls the closest knowledge base passages.

Generation: the large language model writes a reply grounded in those passages.

Tool calling: for refunds, order lookups or plan changes, the model emits a structured call, and your backend checks permissions before executing it.

Handoff: low retrieval confidence or a failed tool call routes the conversation to a human with the transcript.

Two failure modes sit in this path. Retrieval returns whatever the index holds, so a stale pricing article or an unstructured PDF produces a confident wrong answer. We recommend re-indexing on every article edit, chunking by heading, and giving each article an owner. At chatbot volume, one outdated article is repeated in every conversation that retrieves it.

A read-only bot can explain a refund policy but cannot issue the refund. Without tool calling, those conversations end in handoff, so containment can look healthy while resolution stays flat.

Three rules narrow the list:

Helpdesk first. An agent inside your current helpdesk inherits ticket history, routing and handoff with no sync layer to maintain. Switching helpdesks to get a better bot rarely pays back.

Write actions second. If most contacts are "where is my order", a read-only agent can resolve them. If they are refunds or plan changes, choose a tool with action support and scope its permissions tightly.

Volume third. Per-resolution pricing favors spiky or modest volume. At sustained high volume, model it against a per-seat or flat-fee alternative.

We recommend a custom build only when no native agent can reach your backend actions, or when data residency or PII rules rule out a vendor-hosted model. Otherwise the build and evaluation-set maintenance cost exceeds a subscription.

No chatbot is warranted when volume is low (our rough threshold is a few hundred tickets a month, an illustrative figure, not a benchmark). The same goes for when policies change weekly or the knowledge base is stale.

Unmaintained content also carries legal cost: in 2024 a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to pay $650.88 in damages after its chatbot gave a customer wrong bereavement fare information (more on that case below).

How much do AI chatbots for customer service cost?

At Intercom's $0.99 per-resolution price, an AI chatbot for customer service costs about $2,300 per 1,000 resolutions once knowledge base upkeep, integration work, and QA review are counted. The vendor fee is under half of that.

Billing units set the shape of the invoice. Per-seat pricing charges for human agents, per-conversation pricing charges for every chat including the ones the bot fails, and per-resolution pricing charges only for conversations counted as resolved.

What goes wrong with AI customer service chatbots?

AI customer service chatbots fail in four ways: a stale knowledge base, hallucinated policy, handoffs that drop context, and loops with no exit. Each has an early sign you can monitor. Most of these failures can be reduced by choosing the right techniques to 
improve a chatbot with AI
 and machine learning.

Stale knowledge base: retrieval augmented generation answers only as well as the documents it retrieves. If a refund rule changes in the helpdesk but the old article stays indexed, the bot quotes the old rule with full confidence. Early sign: escalations tagged "wrong policy". Give every indexed article an owner and an expiry date.

Hallucinated policy: when retrieval returns nothing relevant, a large language model fills the gap. Restrict answers to retrieved passages, make declining the default, and test with evaluation questions that have no answer in the knowledge base. Early sign: answers with no source attached.

Dropped handoff history: human handoff and escalation triggers, such as two failed turns, negative sentiment, or a refund or legal keyword, are worthless if the agent receives a blank ticket. Pass the transcript, retrieved sources and customer ID into the ticket.

Loops with no exit: a bot that repeats "I didn't understand" traps the customer. Cap failed turns at two, then route to a person, and keep a visible "talk to a human" option throughout.

In 
Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149)
, a British Columbia tribunal held the airline liable for wrong bereavement-fare advice from its chatbot and rejected the argument that the bot was a separate entity. Treat every policy answer as a published company statement.

Disclosure is a second duty. 
Article 50 of the EU AI Act
 requires that people be told they are interacting with an AI system unless that is obvious. We recommend labeling the bot in its first message. Obligations vary by jurisdiction and timeline, so have counsel review your deployment.

Yes, for the work the bot is allowed to finish without a person. Everything else is queued with full context for the morning shift. A three-test rule, which is our own judgement call to tune against your risk tolerance, sets that line.

The bot completes a request overnight only if all three tests pass:

Grounded: the answer comes from a retrieved help article or a system lookup, and confidence is above your threshold.

Reversible or capped: the action can be undone in the morning, or sits under a set limit.

Low-risk identity: it changes no security settings and discloses no personal data beyond what the customer has already verified.

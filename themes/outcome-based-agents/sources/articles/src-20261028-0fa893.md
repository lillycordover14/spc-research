‘Synchronized Swimming’ Must Wait, But FDA And EMA Ready To Collaborate On Botanical Drug Regulation&nbsp;&nbsp;insights.citeline.com

[page text]
Collaboration between the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency on herbal and botanical drug products could start with two topics: how to set standards for batch-to-batch quality variation, and interactions between herbal products and prescription medicines.
These topics emerged from the agencies’ first joint workshop on the subject, ‘Regulatory perspectives on herbal medicinal/botanical drug product development,’ held at the EMA in Amsterdam on Sept. 25.
In a Q&A published by FDA on Oct. 27, Marta Sokolowska, a senior policy advisor at the agency who co-chaired the workshop, said that “despite our different regulatory frameworks, we share the same goal: supporting botanical drug development while acknowledging existing data gaps.” The EMA’s chief medical officer, Steffen Thirstrup, said in the same Q&A that the agencies had “jump[ed] out into the deep end of the swimming pool together without knowing whether we could swim together. I think we figured out we could swim, or at least float, together, but we couldn’t do synchronized swimming yet.”
After the public part of the workshop, the agencies worked through “a very long list of potential topics” for collaboration, Thirstrup said, and settled on two in particular: “One being batch-to-batch quality variation and how to set the right standards. The other topic is related to drug and herbal interactions, knowing that many patients may be taking medications and supplementing with herbal or botanical products. There is the potential for these to create interactions with prescription medicines, and sometimes life-threatening ones.”
The two agencies work within very different frameworks. In the EU, herbal medicines can be authorized through a full application, as with any other medicine, or through two additional routes: well-established use, which requires 10 years of use in the EU, and traditional use, which requires 30 years, 15 of them in the EU. The EMA’s herbal medicinal products committee (HMPC) has produced more than 170 monographs.
In the US there is no herbal-specific route. Botanical drugs go through the same new drug application (NDA) or biologics license application (BLA) as other drugs, and “there’s no specific pathway or program,” Sokolowska said, although botanical experts are available to FDA’s review divisions.
Batch-To-Batch Quality
Despite those differences, the first of the two topics, batch-to-batch quality, is a problem both agencies describe in similar terms. Sokolowska pointed out that, unlike single-molecule drugs, botanical drugs contain a whole range of molecules, and that growing conditions, complex constituents and multi-herb manufacturing “can contribute to batch-to-batch variabilities.”
“At the end of the day, we want to know exactly what patients and consumers are getting,” she said. Thirstrup noted the aim is that “the product that patients and the public buy today is the same as the one tomorrow and next year.”
Both regulators already use similar tools to tackle this issue. At the workshop, FDA botanical reviewer Charles Wu said that for a birch-bark gel approved in December 2023, “we like to see the fingerprinting profile also identical,” and the agency “required multiple geographical diverse cultivation sites to minimize the nature of variability.” Angela Müller of the Association of the European Self-Care Industry (AESGP) said EU rules also show “consistency not only through identity and content, but also in the fingerprints.” Susan Winckler, CEO of the Reagan-Udall Foundation for the FDA, asked whether there is “a quality framework that helps us with batch to batch consistency that could be applied in both regulatory constructs.”
Christelle Anquez-Traxler, senior regulatory science and strategy lead at AESGP – who was among the speakers at the workshop – told HBW Insight that a first step could be to agree a standard way of describing herbal substances and preparations in publications, databases and repositories, covering regulatory classification, Latin name, part used, formulation and, for extracts, the drug-extract ratio and solvent, “so that the identification of what was studied/collected is always crystal clear.”
Herb-Drug Interactions
On the topic of herb-drug interactions, Thirstrup said pharmacists at the workshop “see properly regulated herbal medicines as useful options for helping people manage some mild conditions, but they also reminded us how important professional advice is, particularly when patients are taking other medicines.” Patients, he said, had told the workshop that “reliable information is not always easy to find or understand, especially information about possible drug-drug interactions.” More broadly, he noted that “if something has a pharmacological action and can do something with your body, it will most likely also have some safety concerns.”
During the workshop, Jorge Batista of the Pharmaceutical Group of the European Union said patients often do not disclose use of herbal medicines to their general practitioner, which makes it “harder to pinpoint in relation to contraindications and adverse reactions.” Pharmacists at the counter are increasingly asked: “Can I take this medicine? Is this a medicine? What are the possible interactions?” Batista said, and “it’s so important that pharmacists also have access to monitoring pharmacovigilance systems,” including the ability to record this data in electronic health records.
Patient expert Dominique Hamerlijnck told the workshop it is “not too easy to find any warning or information on the combinations between different herbal products, different additional products and medications, especially if you take a lot of them.” She added that when she tells healthcare professionals about the herbal medications she takes, “they are not in my medical file because they’re not being accepted. So the interaction is never seen and never found.” Carmen Purdel, chair of the HMPC, told the workshop that its monographs include “all the safety data, contraindication, warnings, side effects.”
Real-World Evidence
Real-world evidence (RWE) is not among the topics the agencies have publicly identified for collaboration, but industry representatives see it as the next place to look for common ground. During the workshop, Anquez-Traxler said that RWE could be “an area of convergence between the two, each side of the big pond.” She told HBW Insight that real-world data and evidence “present unique opportunities that should be recognized and taken into account in regulatory assessment,” with “mutual interest” on both sides.
Müller said real-world studies could “help to reduce, not replace, the amount of clinical trials, which would help the funding issues that companies face.”
In her workshop wrap-up, FDA’s Sokolowska said there was “a great opportunity to figure out how can we use confirmatory real world evidence as part of a confirmatory evidence.” Jacqueline Wiesner of Germany’s Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices (BfArM), a member of the HMPC, said real-world data had turned up in the literature review for the committee’s ginkgo monograph, which is being revised, but that the data are “not assessed by now.”
Product Development Challenges
Product developers also face questions of cost and incentive, not just evidence. Sokolowska said: “We heard that the incentive for drug development might not be adequate to offset the unique challenges of botanical development programs.” The agency has held a roundtable with the Reagan-Udall Foundation and opened a request for information on the challenges of developing botanical products, with comments accepted through the docket until Nov. 3. Its findings will be shared with regulatory colleagues “to identify any similar themes they encounter and further our efforts to be consistent as regulators,” she said.
The agency is “trying to accelerate progress in bringing more FDA-approved botanical products to the market, while maintaining our high standards for safety, efficacy, and quality,” Sokolowska added. The FDA told HBW Insight that it considers botanical drug products “a critical area where greater scientific development can unlock new therapeutic opportunities for American patients while maintaining the highest safety standards.”
Holly E. Johnson, chief science officer at the American Herbal Products Association – and a speaker at the workshop – told HBW Insight that as demand for botanical drugs grows, “it is also critical that FDA has the necessary funding and personnel expertise to keep pace and effectively support this work.” She said collaboration to advance botanical drug development “could include data sharing with agencies like EMA” and building on the Botanical Safety Consortium, a public-private partnership that is “advancing development of preclinical non-animal testing models for assessing safety in complex botanical materials.”
US-EU Differences
The two markets look very different in practice. Anquez-Traxler told HBW Insight that four botanical products have been approved in the US as prescription drugs under the NDA and BLA routes – with a few botanical active ingredients permitted for marketing under certain OTC monographs – while “a majority of HMPs [herbal medicinal products] in Europe are available as non-prescription medicines.”
Stefan Gafner, chief science officer at the American Botanical Council – who presented at the workshop – told HBW Insight the regulatory hurdles are “very high” and that for many companies “the return on investment may not be high enough” to justify a new drug application in the US or a well-established use application in Europe. He said the US botanical drug system “seems to be geared towards the prescription drug market, with most applications targeting neglected diseases with a limited market potential,” while in Europe herbal medicines “are sold as OTC drugs, and marketed for conditions that have a much wider consumer appeal.” He wants a less costly route to botanical drug status and a system in which herbal medicines legal in Europe can be sold in the US “and vice versa,” but said that while harmonization is “certainly possible,” it “will require a lot of compromising from both agencies” and he is “not very optimistic that we will see a harmonization of the two regulatory systems anytime soon.”
The agencies themselves spoke about alignment between the US and EU in more general terms. The EMA told HBW Insight that “continued exchanges may help identify areas where greater consistency or alignment could be beneficial.” Anquez-Traxler said it is “[too] soon to talk about harmonization from a pure regulatory point of view.”
Sokolowska highlighted the shared problem facing regulators on both sides of the Atlantic: “Ultimately, we rely on similar data, so when data are lacking, neither of us can adequately address patients’ and clinicians’ questions about the safe use of these products.”

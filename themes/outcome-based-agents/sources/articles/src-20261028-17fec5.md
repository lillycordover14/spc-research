Princess Kako sparks orders for earrings created by Japan indigo artisan&nbsp;&nbsp;毎日新聞

[page text]
Mako Kobayashi crouches as she stirs indigo dye liquid at Shoaizome Koharaya in Gotemba, Shizuoka Prefecture, on Oct. 15, 2026. Her arms and hands have turned blue from indigo dyeing. (Mainichi/Hiroko Michishita)


    GOTEMBA, Shizuoka -- Orders poured in from across Japan for earrings made by a 27-year-old aspiring indigo dye artisan after Princess Kako wore them at a high school equestrian event in this central Japan city. 


    Squatting beside a vat of steaming liquid heated to 90 degrees Celsius, Mako Koboyashi repeatedly stirs a traditional dye mixture used to color thread and cloth, made from wood-ash lye, lime and sake.


    Kobayashi is an apprentice at Shoaizome Koharaya, a traditional indigo-dye workshop in Gotemba, Shizuoka Prefecture. She joined the workshop in March 2024 with the goal of becoming an indigo-dye artisan.


    "At first, it was hard because I did not know how to apply the right amount of force, but I learned how to stir without relying on strength," she said with a smile. 


    Interested in manufacturing, Kobayashi studied electrical engineering at university and went on to work as an electrical engineer after graduation. Although she admired Japan's traditional crafts, she believed entering that world would be impossible for someone without family ties to a craft tradition.


    Yet while working as a company employee, she traveled around Japan to see traditional crafts firsthand. She also felt a sense of crisis that the traditional craftwork she loved were in decline due to a lack of successors.


    For the first year after becoming an apprentice, Kobayashi worked as a company employee on weekdays and trained on her days off. She later quit her job and now spends three to four days a week training while supporting herself through part-time work. "Money is tight," she said with a laugh.


    Still, she said, "It makes me happy when people ask me to dye something for them. It's even more satisfying when I'm able to meet their requests."


    Her mentor, Hiroyasu Kohara, said, "I was surprised when she said she wanted to become an apprentice. What impressed me was her interest in the unique characteristics of our indigo dyeing and her willingness to challenge herself in many different ways."

Earrings worn by Princess Kako bring unexpected attention

Earrings, right, of the same shape as those worn by Princess Kako, are seen at Shoaizome Koharaya in Gotemba, Shizuoka Prefecture, on Oct. 15, 2026. (Mainichi/Hiroko Michishita)


    In July, Princess Kako, the second daughter of Crown Prince Akishino (Fumihito) and Crown Princess Kiko, attended the opening ceremony of the 60th All-Japan High School Equestrian Competition in Gotemba wearing earrings made by Kobayashi. Orders then came in from around the country.


    "I only learned that she had worn them after receiving inquiries. I was surprised, but I was also very happy that it led so many people to place orders," Kobayashi said.


    Looking ahead, Kobayashi hopes to open an experiential lodging facility that combines an indigo-dyeing workshop and a guesthouse. "There are many challenges, but I want to continue working as an indigo artisan for a long time," she said.


    Profile:


    Mako Kobayashi was born in 1999 in the city of Gifu. After graduating from Chiba University's Faculty of Engineering, she worked for a packaging machinery manufacturer before becoming an indigo-dyeing artisan. Her favorite motto is, "Live each day as if it's your last."


    (Japanese original by Hiroko Michishita, Shizuoka Bureau)

Rising market volatility and artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure investment are driving renewed demand for documentary letters of credit (LCs).

Latin America is benefiting from shifting global trade relationships, while Middle East conflict is increasing LC values despite a decline in transaction volumes.

AI is streamlining LC workflows, but human expertise remains essential for oversight and training.

Once thought of as archaic, drifting into commercial history, the documentary 
letter of credit (LC) 
market has rebounded this year. It reached $4.81 billion in 2026 at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 5.3%.

To put that growth into perspective, LC export volumes in the US dropped by as much as 
65%
 between 2013 and 2024, according to figures from the US Federal Reserve. 

This year’s Sibos 2026, held in Miami, Florida, focused on the role of 
digital finance
 in economies driven by 
artificial intelligence (AI)
. While traditional LCs can feel a little outdated compared with the soaring demands of an increasingly automated industry, the link between the two might not be as estranged as it initially seems. 

Reporting from Miami, Mahika Ravi Shankar, Editor-in-Chief at Trade Finance Global (TFG), sat down with Frederick Rugginz, Global Head of Financial Institutions Trade Sales, at J.P. Morgan. The conversation uncovered the surprising technological innovation driving the boom in LCs, discussing which regions benefit and how AI can help streamline deployment. 

Why are LCs growing so quickly?

The conflict in the Middle East has inflated the value of LCs, which often contain credit escalation clauses tied to fluctuating benchmark oil and gas pricing. Rising oil prices raise the value of issued LCs. 

The larger impact, however, is that market volatility has lowered the risk-tolerance threshold for major 
commodity 
buyers and sellers. Less willing to take on exposure to other companies, banks are stepping in to underwrite the transactions using LCs.

The mechanism behind LCs is simple. First, two companies agree to sell a commodity shipment at a fixed price. The buyer asks the bank to underwrite their payment, guaranteeing that the seller will receive it. The bank agrees to pay only if the shipment arrives and meets an agreed-upon quality, ensuring the buyer doesn’t have to pay before receiving the shipment.

Rugginz explained that companies are using this to spread default risk, making LCs an increasingly attractive option. 

Perhaps surprisingly, AI capital expenditure (CapEx) launches frequently deploy LCs to the same effect. “Most corporates will have limits that they’ll take on each other. There’s only a certain amount of exposure that they want to take on each other,” said  Rugginz. This boom is particularly apparent in data centre financing, with innovative “power LCs” helping navigate the energy gap for emerging data centres. 

Nvidia and OpenAI, for instance, are known for being in a circular 
$100 billion
 investment loop, benefiting their balance sheets each time. But the surge in LC demand exposes an industry unwilling to take on more risk, pushing banks to step in.

Different regional experience

Latin America (LatAm) has benefited from the tariff tensions between China and the US. Goods from Brazil and Argentina are undercutting the tariff-inflated prices of goods from other areas, allowing them to capitalise on their export advantage to the US and China. 

“This is a new relationship with Europe, with Africa, with China, and the US, and a lot of LatAm’s exports have been using documentary trade,” said Rugginz. 

With LatAm’s exports – valued at 
$1.39 trillion
 in 2024 – increasingly reaching new markets, particularly 
driven
 by its abundant critical mineral deposits and newly competitive export prices, LCs are helping buyers and suppliers gain confidence when entering new markets.

Rugginz explained that the war in Iran has also driven up the value of LCs in the Middle East. “Since the Iran conflict started, there’s been a 7% drop in LCs to the Middle East, but a 15% increase in value.” Despite volatility, which is threatening, postponing, or reducing a significant percentage of oil projects, overall revenues remain high. 

In regions with ongoing conflict, LCs play another critical role. “What transactional LCs and trade finance do is keep energy and goods flowing both ways, supporting these key commodities,” Rugginz explained. Even in a default scenario, when conflict and volatility threaten payments, banks prioritise food and energy, derisking investments and enabling global banks to stay committed to a region. 

AI innovation could streamline LC workflows

The problem with surging demand for a cumbersome, outdated system is that bottlenecks quickly emerge. To that end, Rugginz explained, AI has arrived at exactly the right time. 

AI has sped up workflows in several key areas: 

Large language models (LLMs) can check documentation and LCs, including any wording a client provides, against internal policies

AI can suggest wording for LCs, including in response to contractual terms or information clients provide

AI can check a new contract against any previous contracts, including across a range of documentary areas from LCs to bills of lading (BoL), for continuity and terms 

LCs are often a standard form, which makes drafting and preparing these documents time-consuming but straightforward work, a process that is seamlessly integrated into AI capabilities. 

Rugginz emphasised that an experienced industry expert checks each piece of AI-assisted work for content and against internal policies. But even here, there are savings. 

One challenge facing major banks is a generational gap: individuals with operational and process knowledge are increasingly reaching retirement age, without a similar level of expertise filtering through to the next generation. 

“AI can help fill that gap. It can help the next generation because they’ll have just a little more breathing space to do that proper check,” said Rugginz. The advantage of adopting technology for laborious processes is the extra time it unlocks, enabling more time for training and caution in each proofing period.

—

One lesson is apparent: there’s no need to throw away the old when bringing in with the new. Financial instruments have a habit of reinventing themselves for deployment, and for now, LCs are standing the test of time.

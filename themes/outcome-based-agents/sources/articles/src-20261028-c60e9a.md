Suncoast Waterkeeper keeps tabs on high bacteria levels

The boat ramp on Linley Street, one of the areas the nonprofit monitors, is having consistent water quality issues.

By

                        
                         
                            S.T. Cardinal 
                        
                         
                            

| 1:25 p.m. October 28, 2026

                                    Suncoast Waterkeeper Water Quality and Pollution Specialist Pia Ronquillo-See collects a water sample at the boat ramp on Linley Street on Monday.

With forest green fishing waders on, Pia Ronquillo-See stoops down in thigh-high water near mangrove roots at the Longboat Key boat ramp on Linley Street and collects a water sample.
“I wade in until above my knees because that is the vulnerable area that people who are entering the water will be exposed to,” said Ronquillo-See, Suncoast Waterkeeper water quality and pollution specialist. “I do the salinity meter first, I uncap the bottle, collect the water sample and then I do the pH and temperature reading.”

She then pens in the results of her measurements and places the sample into a cooler in the trunk of the organization's Ford F-150 Lightning electric pickup truck. That’s one stop on a typical Monday for Ronquillo-See, who visits at least 10 testing locations from Terra Ceia Bay to the north to Sarasota Sailing Squadron to the south every Monday morning.

Brunch at the Bay is Suncoast Waterkeeper’s annual fundraiser. This year, it is at the St. Regis Longboat Key Resort for the first time on Sunday, Nov. 1.
The annual fundraiser is a chance for Suncoast Waterkeeper to share its mission with locals looking to make a difference, and Tyrna said she hopes the event’s success will allow the nonprofit to shift from a grant-heavy budget to a donor-heavy revenue split.
As the nonprofit looks to increase its donor base through its $25-per-month Tide Turners membership program, Tyrna has ambitions to increase the organization’s staff — and impact.
“We have a staff of four. I see us growing to a staff of six,” Tyrna said. “Our next hires would be a lawyer who can help with advocacy and policy and Clean Water Act citizen suits, and I’d like to hire someone who helps steward our members and be a volunteer organizer.”

Water samples are taken to a lab, mixed with nutrients, placed into what is essentially a small ice cube tray and cultured for 24 hours. The number of pods that illuminate under fluorescent light determine the prevalence of enterococci bacteria in the water. Environmental factors such as wind speed, tide level, recent rainfall and temperature can change the prevalence of bacteria.
At the boat ramp off Linley Street, enterococci levels have exceeded 70 per 100mL several times, including over the past two weeks when a 122mL level and 9,208mL were measured. When enterococci levels exceed 70/100mL, water quality is considered unsafe, for studies have shown a heightened risk of contracting illness.
The Florida Department of Health does the same testing at local beaches and issues advisories when high bacteria levels are measured.
“They do the beaches. They do it really well. We do the bay,” Suncoast Waterkeeper Executive Director Abbey Tyrna said. “Because of FDH’s limited resources, many of the bays are not being monitored. So we go to where people recreate and we know people get in the water and we monitor in the same way the Department of Health does.”
Suncoast Waterkeeper shares water quality results with government staff, elected leaders an  the media each Friday and publishes an in-depth report annually. The organization also sues businesses and governments when they do not follow federal guidelines and state pollution permits.
The boat ramp on Linley Street received an F+ grade on Suncoast Waterkeeper’s 2025 Safe to Swim annual report card, which showed 59% of weeks measured at that location were below 71 enterococci per 100mL. The year before, the boat ramp earned an 84% grade. The boat ramp was originally picked as a location when monitoring began years ago because of the number of 
moored boats in the water
 in the area.
“In 2025, our results at the boat ramp were way above the safe to swim conditions for the first half of the year,” Ronquillo-See said. “That’s why we were prompted to keep sampling here and also explore microbial source tracking to see if we could find out the sources of bacteria.”
Pia Ronquillo-See writes down her findings from the boat ramp on Linley Street on Monday.
Photo by S.T. Cardinal
Microbial source tracking determined sewage from moored boats is likely not the culprit of high bacteria levels.
“What we’re finding is that the moored vessels don’t seem to be making any impact, at least not the ones that have been there for the last six months. So we do not believe the initial reason we started to monitor there is actually creating an issue,” Tyrna said. “What is probably the issue is that stormwater pipe and the unabated stormwater moving into the area.”
Suncoast Waterkeeper and Executive Director Abbey Tyrna in her office Oct. 21.
Photo by S.T. Cardinal
In some good news for Longboat Key boat ramp visitors, the town has its eye on future 
stormwater improvements for the neighborhood
, although the project is still in the planning phase. There are multiple avenues the town could take when making improvements to the system that would improve the quality of the water that flows into Sarasota Bay.
“Stormwater ponds are the way that the state and developers have decided to clean up stormwater. It’s an approved method,” Tyrna said. “In Sarasota at The Bay, they created a denitrification trench to allow for more removal of nitrogen before hitting the bay. There could be a similar situation on Longboat to incorporate some sort of retrofit that would treat the water before it’s discharged through that pipe.”

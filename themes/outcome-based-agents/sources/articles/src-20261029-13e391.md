Ask five AI vendors to define 'Forward Deployed Engineer' (FDE), and you can expect five different answers.

Let's refer to the source material. The term was coined by Palantir in the mid-2000s, when its earliest customers (mostly intelligence and defense agencies) could not fully explain their problems, share their data, or spend months on a traditional software rollout.

So rather than wait, Palantir sent its engineers directly to its customers — embedded inside those organizations, building against real conditions, and feeding what they learned back into the product.

The AI era has brought the model roaring back, with OpenAI, Anthropic, Databricks, and a wave of startups and a growing number of established SaaS vendors all standing up FDE teams.

But as the label spreads, its meaning thins out. Across the industry, many of the people now called FDEs more resemble solutions architects or sales engineers.

That matters, because the problem FDEs are meant to solve is real. Most enterprises already have AI. The challenge is engineering it into how the business actually works.

In our experience at Atlassian, across more than 100 enterprise customers, AI rarely stalls because the technology can't do the job. It stalls because everything around it isn't ready — the wrong use case, missing governance, tools that sit outside the workflow, and costs nobody has modeled.

So what separates a real FDE program from services work with a new name? I'd put it simply — if the customer stays dependent on the vendor after the engagement, it's consulting.

Why this isn't consulting

A company typically hires a consultant for a very specific reason. That reason is what they focus on, not on making the company productive enough that it's still in good shape after they leave.

My colleague Paavany Jayanty, who leads AI forward deployed engineering for Atlassian in the US, puts it this way: 

We're not consultants. We deploy a highly qualified, full-stack AI engineer with a very specific skillset into the customer's environment to go solve that problem.

At Atlassian, we are insistent on the engineer part. Our FDEs have gone through the same hiring process as any other Atlassian engineer, and they've written production code. The expectation is that anything they build for a customer meets the same quality bar as the software that goes into Atlassian's own systems. And sometimes, what our FDEs build ends up becoming part of the product for all our customers.

To that end, our goal isn't to find workarounds. It's to support the business use case properly, because that's the only way the solution stays durable in the long term. When an FDE hits a gap in the platform, they're empowered to fix the blocker at the source rather than patch around it.

One of our FDEs built a missing path to move Rovo agents promptly from sandbox to production. That single fix unblocked more than 15 enterprise accounts at once, including Cisco.

Built to be handed over

Our core design principle puts transparency first — FDEs never deploy changes to a customer's production systems without their explicit awareness and oversight. Everything we build is open. We build in a sandbox, demonstrate the work, and then the customer reviews it, approves it, learns from it and moves it into production themselves.

At one financial institution, that meant going from uncertainty to a working agent in a secure sandbox in under three weeks, giving the organization something concrete to evaluate and scale.

Teaching customers how the solution is built, what it's made of and how to maintain it is a critical part of each engagement, not an add-on. Engagements typically run around 12 weeks, and the goal at the end is a fully self-sufficient team.

Getting there usually requires two pieces of groundwork that customers don't always anticipate.

The first is data readiness. One healthcare organization had personally identifiable information in its source systems that it didn't want exposed to AI, so our FDEs built a way to strip it out before any agent touched it.

The second is showing people what's possible. Many customers don't realize what the platform they already own is capable of until they see it working on their own problem.

How we judge success

The signal I watch is simple — once the engagement ends, is the number of agents on the customer's side growing? That's the clearest indication they've started building on their own.

Expedia is a good example. Our FDEs co-built production agents with Expedia's teams, including one that has archived around 600,000 Confluence pages. Himanshu Singh, an Engineering Manager at Expedia, said Atlassian's FDEs helped teams: 

turn an ambitious idea into real business impact (and) build lasting confidence in how AI can improve the way we work.

Expedia has since built several agents of its own, in addition to the ones we built together.

The work also compounds across customers.

At one technology company, FDEs deployed AI that triages and resolves tier-1 and tier-2 service issues end-to-end, cutting mean time to resolution from 18 days to 10 minutes and saving more than $20 million.

And after building a bug-triage agent for several customers, we turned it into a Rovo Studio template.

Any customer can now take it, adjust the prompt and run it themselves, creating a multiplier effect in which every customer benefits.

What customers get wrong

The most common misconception is that an FDE is a free engineering resource you get for a set number of days. It's not — it's a two-way partnership. The customer brings real business requirements, and has to be willing to take the FDE's guidance.

Another fallacy we've heard is that vendors use FDEs to build agents that drive up consumption under usage-based pricing. In practice, it's the opposite.

We help customers think through their use cases and build them in the most efficient way, including by reducing token usage. That means matching the model to the task and reusing organizational context instead of rebuilding it for every agent.

In Atlassian's testing, grounding agents in that shared context produced answers 44% higher in quality while using 48% fewer tokens.

Why not just hire your own engineers?

We want customers to build their own agents. Your engineers can learn the platform from documentation and forums and get something up and running.

What an FDE engagement offers is a head start on the enterprise AI journey, and the chance to learn from engineers who work with the vendor's product teams every day and understand what will hold up over time. FDEs also bring insights from other customer engagements, which helps organizations think more clearly about their own AI strategy.

The goal isn't long-term dependency. Success is when the customer stops needing us. A good FDE engagement is designed to end, and the best ones do.

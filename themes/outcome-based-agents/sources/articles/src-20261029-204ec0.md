Serval's Jake Stauch Says Code Agents Will Beat ServiceNow, and the Guardrails Are the Business
Add preferred source
Serval co-founder and CEO Jake Stauch is betting that AI coding agents, not drag-and-drop workflow builders, are the right way to automate enterprise IT — and that the durable product isn't the model but the permission layer around it. In an interview on the No Priors podcast, Stauch describes a company that set out at two founders to become "the largest enterprise software company of all time," circled ServiceNow on a list of targets, and went straight at large enterprise instead of taking the safer SMB route. Serval has grown from 20 employees in January to 200, counts Fox Corporation as a company-wide customer, and sells a guardrail-first model in which agents request specific powers that humans approve with scoped access and audit trails. The central tension: autonomy makes agents useful, and autonomy makes them dangerous, so Stauch argues the winner owns privilege escalation rather than raw intelligence. The article examines the code-vs-workflow thesis, the deployment model, the SaaS-consolidation debate, and why Stauch thinks enterprise AI spending keeps rising even as buyers demand ROI proof. It also weighs the risk that ServiceNow and OpenAI, now deepening their own partnership, close the gap Serval is counting on.

Enterprise IT has a dirty secret that anyone who has worked inside a big company already knows: ServiceNow is both the biggest line item on the IT budget and the biggest source of complaints. Jake Stauch spent years selling security products to IT teams and heard the same thing over and over. So when he and a co-founder started Serval roughly two and a half years ago, they did something almost no startup does — they wrote down a list of every enterprise software company in the world, circled ServiceNow, and declared they would beat it.

"We set out from the beginning to be the largest enterprise software company of all time," Stauch said on the No Priors podcast. "We actually made a list of all the companies. We circled ServiceNow, and we said we're going to beat them."

That was a two-person company at the time. Today Serval has about 200 employees, up from 20 in January, and Fox Corporation as a company-wide customer. The ambition is the story here, because it explains every product and go-to-market decision Serval has made — and it frames the central question investors should be asking about the entire enterprise AI software category: if coding agents really can replace the workflow layer of corporate IT, who captures the value, the model makers, the incumbents, or the guardrail vendors?

Code beats drag-and-drop, and AI is what makes code practical

Stauch's founding thesis is deceptively simple. Workflow automation should be written in code, not assembled in a drag-and-drop builder. The reason nobody did it before is that code required programmers, and programmers are expensive and slow relative to the volume of automation a large company needs.

"Code is a better way of building automation," he said. "The problem, obviously, is not many people can write code and do it quickly enough for it to make sense for automation."

The incumbent's model is fundamentally a workflow platform, and Stauch's critique is about velocity rather than capability. Every new automation requires implementation, often by outside consultants, so real deployments run months behind where the business wants them. Drag-and-drop builders, in his telling, fail at both ends of the skill spectrum: too complex for non-technical users drawn in by the promise of no-code, and too abstracted for engineers who would rather just write the script.

Serval's bet was that coding agents would get good enough to close that gap, and specifically that they would get good at the boring, well-defined tasks that make up most enterprise automation — reset a password, route an approval, check a policy. Those tasks are verifiable, which means a model can be scored on whether it succeeded, which means it can improve steadily. In the early days the models weren't there, so Serval seeded its demos with a library of example workflows and kept growing it. Eventually, Stauch said, "it became hard for you to give it a workflow that it couldn't automate if the APIs existed."

The strategic choice that follows is the one worth studying. Most startups attacking an incumbent pick one of two paths: build a thin layer on top of the giant and hope to ride its distribution, or go down-market to small and mid-sized businesses where the incumbent is weak and sales cycles are short. The first path almost never works — host Elad Gil noted it has essentially never produced a durable company, with Veeva as the rare exception that backward-integrated into its own platform. The second path is what challengers to Workday and Salesforce have mostly done.

Serval rejected both. It went straight at the largest enterprises from day one, accepting brutal sales cycles and demanding deployment requirements in exchange for a much bigger prize.

The product is the boundary, not the model

The most counterintuitive thing about Serval is what it does not build. It doesn't train language models. It builds the constraints around them.

"We are not building the LLMs ourselves," Stauch said. "We are building the guardrails. We are building the boundaries. And the boundaries are the product for us."

This is a bet that as models get smarter, the value migrates away from raw intelligence and toward governance. The thinking: every incremental capability an agent gains raises the damage it could do if it goes wrong, which makes the approval workflow, the audit trail, and the permission scope more valuable, not less.

That logic leads Stauch to a prediction that runs against the prevailing narrative of ever-broadening access. He thinks individual employee permissions in large enterprises will shrink, not grow, and shrink sharply.

The argument is about the ceiling on damage. A single person with broad access is a manageable risk because a human is slow and self-interested. That same person with a fleet of agents acting on their behalf is a much bigger problem. So enterprises will push toward genuine least privilege — and then discover that everyone needs exceptions, all the time. Serval wants to own the exception layer.

"By owning how privileges get escalated in the enterprise, we actually become more powerful and more essential the more you clamp down on end-user permissions," he said.

This is also why he is skeptical that consumer-style personal assistants translate into the enterprise. An individual's permission set is so narrow that a personal agent "can barely even read your emails." Serval instead comes in through the IT department, uses service-account credentials and admin-level access, and wraps that elevated power in deterministic approval steps. Underneath, it is also a ticketing system, so every agent action generates access requests, approvals, and logs.

The design vocabulary Stauch keeps reaching for is the software development lifecycle — development, staging, production, branches, gates, user acceptance testing, compliance review. He argues most new AI products skip it entirely and ship agents straight into production, which a large bank or insurer will never accept.

Selling a replacement means selling a career, not a gamble

The hardest obstacle to displacing deeply embedded software is not technical. It is personal. The buyer who chose the incumbent — and whose reputation is tied to it — has to stick their neck out to rip it out.

Stauch's answer is that Serval must be positioned as the path to a promotion rather than a risk to be managed.

"People built their career on ServiceNow," he said. "You're going to build your career on this next generation."

He offers Fox Corporation as the proof. The person who led the company-wide migration on the ground was nominated as MVP of the entire company and received an award for the work. Stauch uses that story deliberately: it converts the pitch from a compliance exercise into a career-defining move.

Deployment follows a matching model. Serval sends a "deployment strategist" and a "deployed software engineer" on site to map workflows and drive the rollout, then sells ongoing services through what it calls ProServal, where automation engineers keep supporting customers after go-live. Stauch says there is "unlimited demand for work" and a long tail of requests that never quite ends.

Two automations have already crossed the threshold into native product features: detecting knowledge-base articles that conflict or are stale, and reclaiming unused software licenses by canceling subscriptions automatically. Stauch notes that the same nominal workflow varies enormously across companies — a password reset at one firm is, in his word, shockingly different from another's — but that variation lives in the platform's building blocks rather than in custom code. Serval is, in his phrase, "the IDE for the IT department."

The internal organization mirrors the sales motion. Serval has no product function in the traditional sense. Product development is decentralized to engineers, who sit in customer Slack channels and join at least one customer call a week — many, one a day. Stauch compares it to immersion language learning. The trade-off he acknowledges is that the product now ships faster than customers, or he, can absorb, which forces decisions down to the edge and requires killing features the team was once committed to.

The SaaS shakeout will hit point solutions, not systems of record

Stauch partly agrees with the loudest debate in software right now — that AI lets companies build their own tools, hollowing out SaaS vendors. But he draws a firm line at systems of record.

Companies that tried to vibe-code their core systems, he said, "have discovered that it's quite expensive to build and maintain enterprise software." The failure mode is not the first version. It is the edge cases, the maintenance staff, and the perennial truth that "no one really wants to work on the internal tools team."

His forecast is consolidation rather than elimination. A large enterprise with hundreds of SaaS applications probably needs a couple of dozen; point solutions and middleware get absorbed into core platforms. But ERP, HRIS, IT service management, and CRM persist — possibly transformed, and possibly with a new generation of incumbents. That last clause is the slot Serval is aiming at.

On spending, Stauch sees the current token pullback as a phase rather than a ceiling. CIOs are trying to rein in the initial era of maximum token consumption, but most companies are still adopting AI for the first time, so aggregate spend keeps climbing. What buyers actually want is proof of return, and that is where he is bluntest.

"I don't know that anyone's really figured out how to measure what you're getting from the tokens that you're buying," he said.

Serval prices on workflows run rather than tokens consumed, on the theory that token counts measure verbosity and inefficiency rather than work accomplished.

Background agents are the moonshot, and slow escalation is the only safe road

The most ambitious version of this technology is what Stauch calls background agents — software that goes off on its own, discovers problems, and fixes them without being asked.

"Background agents are the ultimate moonshot in enterprise software — agents that go off and do work for you and magically discover all your problems and solve all your problems," he said. "No one's really figured out how to do this well."

The tension is structural. You want autonomy precisely so you don't have to babysit the agent, but autonomy is exactly what makes an enterprise security team break out in hives. Gil raised the recent incidents at OpenAI and Hugging Face as examples of agents doing something unexpected in pursuit of a goal.

Serval's answer is incremental escalation. The agent comes back and asks for specific powers — it wants to call a particular tool or run a specific script. A human approves each request with scoped permissions and guardrails. Over time the agent accumulates a broad enough toolkit to handle most situations on its own.

"It's an incremental escalation of capabilities: the agent comes back and asks to call this tool or run this script, you approve with permissions and guardrails, and over time it becomes competent because it has a big enough toolkit," Stauch said.

He concedes the babysitting is annoying. But he argues the alternative is an agent that "could theoretically just start writing code that could take action on all your systems" — an "unbounded security risk that I don't think anyone's gonna be able to take anytime soon."

Two gaps still need closing, and both are partly model problems rather than harness problems. Memory must improve so an agent retains the relevant thread of a relationship going back to the first conversation; today it fragments across chats. Context windows must grow large enough to hold that history. But Stauch keeps returning to the same binding constraint: "The permissions problem will always remain the biggest thing — even if the agent is very valuable and smart, how much do I trust you to just run wild and not have to babysit your actions?"

Catalyst turns the platform's complexity into a conversation

Serval's first generation of AI tooling created an unexpected problem. Customers could build powerful systems, but they had to be taught every product primitive first, which reintroduced the training burden the product was supposed to remove.

Catalyst is the fix. It is an agent whose job is managing the platform itself — building automations, configuring approval rules and permission checks, writing policies, updating systems, and understanding the entire deployment. It is now the primary interface into the product.

The point is to eliminate the blank canvas that has plagued automation tools for two decades. Rather than staring at an empty workflow builder, a user can ask where to start, get suggestions, ask the agent to explain a concept, and iterate. Sarah Guo observed that this effectively raises the technical competence of a large population of users and turns more of them into engineers. Stauch agreed: the agent becomes a build partner that walks users through the process and surfaces opportunities they would never have thought of.

The persona design matters more than it sounds. Serval's users range from an IT engineer who writes code and wants to see every line, to a marketing or people-team user who never wants to see code but needs code-generation flexibility for a genuinely complex workflow. Stauch's criticism of earlier enterprise products is that they were, in his word, pedantic toward IT — assuming users needed a stripped-down, opinionated version of the tool. He thinks the opposite is true: IT respects the complexity and wants the power.

Headcount grows because each employee does more, not less

Serval went from 20 people in January to 200, and Stauch expects hiring to accelerate. That inverts the usual AI-efficiency argument, in which automation lets companies do more with fewer people. His version: because each employee now accomplishes so much more than a year ago, the company sets bigger ambitions for what they could collectively do, which creates demand for more people.

The addressable market, in his telling, is bounded only by ambition. "We are automating essentially all kinds of work for every single employee in the world," he said.

That open-ended market is a mixed blessing. Stauch calls it "not strictly better," because the company must constantly choose what to focus on rather than being constrained by what the market will bear. But it removes the ceiling that usually disciplines a software company's roadmap.

The backdrop for all of this is a capital cycle that dwarfs the software boom of the last decade. The five largest hyperscale cloud operators raised $326 billion in fresh equity and debt in 2026, more than triple the previous record pace, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence, while AI data-center capital spending is projected at roughly $725 billion to $800 billion this year. Goldman Sachs chief economist Jan Hatzius expects the year-over-year contribution of hyperscaler AI investment to U.S. GDP growth to peak in the fourth quarter of 2026 at about 1.5%. That spending is what keeps model capability improving — which is exactly the assumption Serval's business rests on, and exactly the force that could erode its edge.

The unresolved question is whether a guardrail-first company survives the very model improvements its founder is counting on. If coding agents keep getting better, value shifts further toward governance and away from generation — good for Serval's thesis, but also an invitation for ServiceNow to bolt on its own code-generation layer and for model providers to move up the stack. ServiceNow has already moved in that direction, expanding its alliance with OpenAI to embed models and agents deeper into its workflow engine and open its tasks inside ChatGPT and Codex, with a Codex integration planned for its AI Workflow Factory. Stauch's counter is that owning privilege escalation makes Serval more essential as permissions tighten, but that claim depends on enterprises actually shrinking individual access — something he predicts rather than demonstrates. Two other threads bear watching: whether the Fox Corporation deployment becomes a repeatable reference for career-advancement selling, and whether pricing on workflows run holds up once buyers demand the return-on-investment measurement that even Stauch admits nobody has solved. For investors, the lesson is less about any single startup than about where the profit pool lands in enterprise AI: not with whoever has the smartest model, but with whoever controls the permission, the audit trail, and the approval that lets a cautious bank say yes.

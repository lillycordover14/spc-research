From Static Capacity to Adaptive Networks: Building Resilience into Biologics Supply
 

		Biopharma companies are looking beyond available capacity to CDMO networks that can adapt across sites, scales and changing supply needs.	

Save

Biologics supply resilience increasingly depends on whether CDMO capacity can adapt across sites, scales and changing product requirements. Photo: stock.adobe.com/littlewolf1989 

Editor’s Take:
 For biologics sponsors, true CDMO capacity depends on more than open slots—it requires the right technical capabilities, locations and ability to adapt as a program evolves.

Historically, biopharmaceutical supply chain disruption was treated as an isolated logistical or procurement problem, a matter of managing delayed shipments, or fluctuating raw material costs. 

Today, given the global nature of logistics and the complexity of modern biologics, supply chain disruptions present systemic risks that permeate the entire product lifecycle. Operational bottlenecks can directly impact clinical development timelines, compromise commercial launch readiness, and ultimately threaten the continuous supply of life-saving therapies to patients.

As a result, biopharmaceutical companies are now gauging the supply chain resilience of the contract development and manufacturing organizations (CDMOs) when selecting their potential biopharmaceutical partners. 

Identifying this resilience requires a holistic view of supply networks, one not solely concerned with the amount of capacity available at a CDMO but also with where that capacity is located, whether it fits the product’s technical needs, and whether it can support key markets.

Mapping vulnerabilities before they impact supply

A resilient CDMO network begins with a clear understanding of where supply could become constrained. Biopharma companies should consider four areas when assessing whether a manufacturing partner has identified and prepared for these vulnerabilities:

Geographic concentration:
 If critical production stages depend on a limited number of regions, disruption affecting a manufacturing hub or transport corridor can restrict downstream supply. Biopharma companies should examine where the CDMO’s facilities and suppliers are located, as well as whether its network provides credible alternatives when one region is affected.

Material and supplier dependencies:
 The availability of a manufacturing suite offers little protection if a shortage of chromatography resin, cell culture media, filters, or single-use components prevents production from proceeding. Long lead times and reliance on sole-source suppliers may further limit the CDMO’s ability to respond when forecasts change or supplies are interrupted.

Future manufacturing capacity:
 Clinical manufacturing generally requires flexible access to smaller batches, whereas commercial production depends on repeatable performance at greater volumes and reliable long-term scheduling. Unless the CDMO has considered this transition early, a biopharma company may reach a pivotal milestone without a clear route to the capacity required for launch.

Technical capability:
 Available bioreactor volume is not necessarily usable capacity. A CDMO’s facility must also possess the containment, analytical, downstream processing capabilities, and flexibility required by the product and process. As biologics become more complex, the number of technically suitable manufacturing sites may narrow.

Assessing these areas across the CDMO’s supply network provides a more meaningful indication of resilience than available capacity alone. A partner that identifies potential constraints early can give a biopharma company more options for managing these bottlenecks and pitfalls before they affect the program.

Onshoring as regional reinforcement

Facility location can influence supply resilience, but onshoring and regionalization represent different approaches. Biopharma companies should understand what each model can offer and whether the CDMO’s wider supply network allows those benefits to be realized.

Onshoring
: Involves locating or returning manufacturing activities to the biopharma company’s domestic market. Its appeal lies in shortening some supply routes, improving proximity to local teams, and reducing dependence on overseas production corridors. It may also provide access to established domestic biotechnology clusters and support national efforts to strengthen medicine security. However, onshoring does not create a self-sufficient supply chain. Domestic facilities may continue to rely on internationally sourced equipment, raw materials, and specialist services. It may also be impractical where the required expertise or infrastructure is unavailable locally, while higher operating costs can make domestic production unsuitable for some programs. Companies should therefore assess whether a CDMO’s onshore capacity is supported by a sufficiently diverse and well-managed supply base.

Regionalization
: Distributes manufacturing capabilities across several major geographic markets rather than concentrating them within one country or global hub. For biopharma companies serving multiple markets, this approach can place production closer to regional demand and provide alternative supply routes if another part of the network is disrupted. It may also improve access to regional talent and make collaboration across development, manufacturing, and quality functions easier. However, operating across multiple regions introduces additional complexity. Differences between facilities, regulatory requirements, and supply bases may make production difficult to transfer, while qualifying additional sites requires time and investment. Regionalization is therefore most effective when the CDMO’s facilities operate as a connected network, with sufficient alignment in processes and quality systems to make alternative manufacturing routes credible.

Neither approach guarantees resilience through geography alone. Their value depends on whether the CDMO has built the supplier diversity and transfer readiness needed to sustain manufacturing when normal supply arrangements are disrupted.

Capacity must follow the product lifecycle

Capacity planning is particularly challenging for biopharma companies because demand for biologics is hard to predict. Early clinical programs require limited volumes, and their future needs remain uncertain. Positive clinical data, accelerated approval pathways, or stronger-than-expected market uptake can then increase demand within a compressed timeframe. Conversely, trial delays or clinical attrition may release production slots that had been reserved.

Planning for multiple outcomes

Because biologics demand can change rapidly, biopharma companies need a CDMO that plans for more than a single forecast and that can remain flexible when things change. For example, a clinical program may progress faster than expected following positive data or an accelerated approval, creating an earlier need for commercial capacity. Trial delays or attrition can have the opposite effect, reducing or postponing demand.

A resilient CDMO prepares for these possibilities through scenario-based capacity planning. Early in development, the CDMO assesses how the process may need to scale and identifies which facilities could support later clinical or commercial production. The plan can then set decision points linked to meaningful program milestones and can course-correct as needed. This determines when the biopharma company may need to reserve additional capacity, begin technology transfer, or invest in new infrastructure.

The scenarios should be proactively updated as the program develops. Biopharma companies contribute current information about clinical progress, regulatory timelines, and anticipated demand, while the CDMO translates those assumptions into capacity requirements. This structures the response to uncertainty without requiring the biopharma company to commit prematurely to capacity it may not use or need.

However, planning alone does not create resilience. A scenario has limited value if the CDMO cannot adapt its manufacturing operations when that scenario materializes.

Designing networks that can adapt

A flexible CDMO can convert capacity plans into practical action. If demand increases, the network may need to accommodate larger batches or free up production slots. If the primary site is disrupted, manufacturing may need to move elsewhere. Without suitable alternative capacity, the biopharma company remains dependent on the original arrangement even if the risk has already been anticipated.

This adaptability must be established before circumstances change. The CDMO should identify which facilities could support the product at different stages and determine what would be required to move between them. Alignment in equipment, operating practices, and quality systems can make technology transfer more predictable, although facilities do not need to be identical. What matters is that their differences are understood and that the analytical and regulatory requirements of a transfer have been assessed.

For programs with sufficient demand or supply risk, qualifying a second site may provide an alternative manufacturing route if the primary facility becomes unavailable. In other cases, identifying a technically suitable backup site and preparing a transfer strategy may offer an appropriate level of protection without immediately duplicating manufacturing and keeping the initial investment to a level that fits the client’s risk tolerance.

Biopharma companies should therefore assess whether a CDMO’s capacity is genuinely accessible as requirements change. A network may contain multiple facilities, but it is only adaptable if the product can move between appropriate scales or sites within a timeframe that protects development progress and patient supply.

Technical capability determines whether CDMO capacity is truly available

As biologics pipelines diversify, biopharma companies cannot assess CDMO capacity solely by looking at available production slots or bioreactor volume. Capacity is only usable when the facility also has the infrastructure, expertise, and analytical capabilities required to manufacture the product safely and in accordance with its control strategy.

This distinction becomes more important for complex modalities. An antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) may require antibody production alongside high-potency containment, conjugation, and specialized analytical methods. Multispecific antibodies can introduce product-specific challenges during expression and characterization. High-concentration formulations for subcutaneous delivery may also place different demands on processing and fill-finish operations than conventional intravenous products.

The more specialized the requirements become, the fewer facilities may be able to support the program. As a result, a spare slot in the CDMO’s network may offer little protection if that site lacks the necessary technical capabilities. Companies should therefore establish where the relevant expertise and infrastructure are located, whether they can support the product at later-stage volumes and how supply would be maintained if the primary facility became unavailable.

A resilient CDMO must understand where technical dependencies could restrict supply and prepare realistic ways to manage them. This may involve connecting specialist centers through established transfer processes, qualifying an additional site where the risk justifies it, or maintaining contingency plans for critical activities that cannot readily be relocated.

The future of biologics supply will depend on adaptable CDMO networks

As biopharma companies rely more on external manufacturing, their supply resilience will depend on the adaptability of their CDMO partners. CDMOs must respond to this dependence with coordination, syncing their technical capacity to critical materials across their connected networks.

This flexibility must be established before circumstances change. Capacity planning should evolve with clinical and commercial forecasts, while technology transfer and contingency strategies should prepare programs for changes in scale, location, or demand.

CDMO selection will become an increasingly important supply-chain decision. Biopharma companies must look beyond capacity available today and choose partners whose networks can adapt as their programs and the wider operating environment evolve.

Abstract
In addition to hamstring and patellar tendon (PT) autografts, the quadriceps tendon (QT) has gained increasing popularity for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), partly due to lower reported graft re-rupture rates. However, postoperative changes of the residual quadriceps tendon and the relationship between tendon mechanical properties and clinical outcomes remain poorly understood. The purpose of this study was to evaluate in vivo QT mechanical properties following graft harvest for ACLR and to quantify tendon mechanical properties using shear wave elastography (SWE). Twenty patients (11 males, 9 females; mean age 29.8 ± 7.2 years; mean BMI 26.8 ± 3.8 kg/m²) with anterior cruciate ligament rupture who underwent ACLR using an ipsilateral QT autograft were prospectively enrolled. In addition to standardized clinical outcome measures, participants underwent a standardized multimodal ultrasound protocol of the bilateral QT consisting of B-mode-ultrasound (B-US), Color Doppler-ultrasound (CD-US) and a SWE examination. Measurements were performed preoperatively (t0) and at 3 (t1), 6 (t2), and 12 months (t3) postoperatively. For SWE values a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was performed to evaluate differences over time and between sides. The within-subject factors were time (preoperative, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months) and side (operated vs. contralateral control). For ROM measurements and scores a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was performed to evaluate differences over time. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared (ηₚ²). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Relationships between variables were assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r). A significant main effect of time (F(3,57) = 2.86; 
p
 = 0.045; partial η² = 0.131) and a significant time-by-side interaction (F(3,57) = 3.98; 
p
 = 0.012; partial η² = 0.173) were observed. On the operated limb, a significant time effect was observed (F(3,57) = 5.14; 
p
 = 0.003), characterized by a transient increase in mechanical properties from the preoperative baseline to 3 months (Δ = −0.421; 
p
 = 0.045), followed by a significant decline from 3 to 6 months (Δ = +0.447; 
p
 = 0.003) and from 3 to 12 months (Δ = +0.368; 
p
 = 0.030). No significant difference was found between the preoperative baseline and 12 months. On the control limb, QT mechanical properties remained stable throughout the entire follow-up period (F(3,57) = 0.38; 
p
 = 0.769); none of the pairwise comparisons reached statistical significance. Over the 12-month follow-up period, significant improvements were observed in knee flexion as well as in the IKDC and Lysholm clinical scores, with the greatest gains occurring between the preoperative baseline and 6 months postoperatively. The Öhberg score exhibited a biphasic course with an initial increase at 3 months and subsequent normalisation by 12 months. Extension showed only a non-significant trend toward improvement. No statistically significant correlations were found between QT mechanical properties and clinical scores, as well as between QT mechanical properties and range of motion. p

p
 = 0.231), without reaching significance. No significant correlations were observed between QT mechanical properties and knee flexion (

The findings indicate a high adaptation capacity of the QT, with transient postoperative changes in stiffness that normalize over time. Future studies incorporating longitudinal histological evaluation are warranted to further elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying QT alterations after graft harvest.

Anterior Cruciate Ligament Injury Management and Reconstruction Techniques

Introduction
With an incidence of 68.6 per 100.000 person-years
1
, ruptures of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) are among the most common ligament injuries worldwide
1
,
2
 and may represent a career-ending event for athletes
3
. Owing to the limited healing capacity of the native ACL, anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is commonly performed to restore knee joint stability and mitigate the risk of early degenerative changes
4
. With over 200,000 surgeries annually in the United States alone, ACRL is a frequently performed operation, reflecting both the high incidence of injury and the clinical demand for surgical stabilization
5
. Predominantly affected are young and physically active individuals, typically between 15 and 35 years of age, with a peak incidence in adolescents and young adults engaged in pivoting sports
1
,
6
. Graft selection in ACLR involves several considerations, including autograft versus allograft, and soft tissue grafts versus grafts with bone blocks. In Europe, tendon autografts are generally preferred for primary ACLR
2
, whereas allograft tendons are typically reserved for revision procedures or for primary ACLR in patients older than 35 years
7
. Commonly used grafts for ACLR include the bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) graft, hamstring tendon (HT) graft, and quadriceps tendon (QT) graft. The patellar tendon (PT) graft is associated with faster graft incorporation and superior knee flexion, whereas HT grafts are associated with less patellofemoral knee pain, better knee extension, and reduced donor-site morbidity
8
. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have been published supporting the use of the QT grafts for ACLR. Recent studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated lower graft re-rupture rates and reduced donor site morbidity compared with HT and BPTB grafts
9
,
10
,
11
. In case of ACLR revisions, a recent meta-analysis reported no significant differences in patient reported outcomes, knee laxity, or failure rates between QT and HT grafts
12
.
To date, graft choice remains a highly investigated topic in ACLR; however, the optimal autograft remains controversial. Despite the extensive body of literature addressing clinical outcomes and failure rates, donor-site morbidity is still insufficiently understood. Apart from a limited number of studies comparing sensory deficits, cosmetic scaring, and functional outcome
13
, only few investigations have applied ultrasound or elastosonography to assess structural and mechanical properties of the PT
14
,
15
,
16
, HT
17
 and associated musculatures
18
,
19
 following ACLR. To date, there are two studies that investigated healing rates of QT after ACLR using ultrasound
20
,
21
. High healing rates could be detected after only 6 months
21
, but echogenicity was significantly lower in the harvested QT even after 3.4 years, suggesting potential changes in tendon quality even after this time
20
.
Shear wave elastography (SWE) has emerged as a valuable imaging modality for in vivo measurements of tissue stiffness, enabling quantitative evaluation of SWE-derived mechanical properties
22
. Although previous studies have demonstrated good to excellent intra- and interrater reliability
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
 for SWE assessments of ligaments, muscles and tendons, the technique remains operator dependent, requiring adequate experience, technical proficiency, and standardized measurement protocols SWE to achieve high reliability
28
. Mifsud et al. validated the relationship between shear wave speed and in vivo tendon stiffness under small-strain conditions by comparing SWE against with a gold standard method for measuring the Achilles tendon stiffness, combining conventional ultrasound imaging with dynamometry
29
. In addition, SWE has been shown to be reliable tool for the longitudinal assessment of PT viscoelastic properties
30
. Several studies have investigated in vivo stiffness changes of the PT
14
,
15
,
16
, the semitendinosus tendon (ST)
17
 and musculature
18
,
19
, following ACLR. However, to date, no study has evaluated QT properties using SWE after ACLR with a QT autograft. Tendon stiffness reflects the biomechanical integrity and load-bearing capacity of the musculotendinous unit and plays a key role in force transmission and dynamic joint stability. Alterations in muscle–tendon stiffness after ACLR have been shown to be directly associated with reduced quadriceps strength and impaired knee function
18
. Persistent quadriceps weakness is a well-established clinical problem after ACL reconstruction and is associated with inferior outcomes and increased reinjury risk
31
. Altered tendon stiffness may cause reduced strength, limited physical performance, and delayed return to sport. Therefore, assessing tendon stiffness of QT provides clinically meaningful information that complements patient-reported outcomes scores by linking structural tissue properties to functional recovery. Accordingly, this study aimed to characterize the structural and mechanical changes of the QT following graft harvest for ACLR, using SWE as an in vivo quantitative assessment tool. We hypothesized that QT mechanical properties undergoe distinct, time-dependent changes after ACLR with a QT autograft, reflecting the tendon’s biological alterations during the healing process.
Materials and methods
This study was designed as a prospective, exploratory, single-arm longitudinal study with repeated within-subject measurements. Assessments were performed preoperatively and at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, with the contralateral limb serving as an internal control.
Ethics approval
The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (Ref. No. 22-454-B; Friedrich-Alexander University Erlangen-Nuremberg, Germany). All participants were informed about the purpose, potential benefits, and risks of the study and provided written informed consent prior to participation, in accordance to the principles found in the Declaration of Helsinki.
Experimental approach
The present study was designed to evaluate in vivo QT mechanical properties bilaterally following unilateral ACLR using QT autografts, with the aim of identifying postoperative differences in shear wave velocity (SWV). Study procedures for each participant comprised a standardized ultrasound examination and a comprehensive clinical assessment.
The primary outcome measures of the study included: (1) tendon stiffness properties, assessed by the absolute values of shear wave elastography (SWE) – derived parameters, including tissue specific shear wave velocity (SWV); (2) modified Ohberg score
32
 measured with Color Doppler ultrasound (CD-US); and (3) clinical outcome measures, comprising range of motion (ROM), Lysholm Score
33
,
34
,
35
, the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) Score
36
,
37
, and the Cincinnati sports activity scale (CSAS)
38
,
39
,
40
.
All measurements were performed under resting conditions with participants positioned supine for QT measurements. The knee was placed at 0 degrees of knee flexion during data acquisition. Measurements were obtained preoperatively (t0) and at 3 months (t1), 6 months (t2), and 12 months (t3) postoperatively.
Study population
Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling strategy. All patients undergoing ACLR at our institution during the study period were screened for eligibility. Individuals who met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and provided informed consent were enrolled sequentially until the required sample size was achieved. Twenty-eight patients were recruited as participants from the orthopedic department. Eight patients were excluded because they did not meet inclusion criteria (

The inclusion criteria included an ACL tear with following ACLR using QT autograft. The participants were advised to renounce sport activities for 48 h prior to the data collection

Surgical technique
Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals were established, followed by a diagnostic arthroscopy. Concomitant meniscal injuries were addressed first using arthroscopic meniscal repair where indicated. With the knee flexed to 90° and supported using an adjustable leg holder, a 25-mm longitudinal skin incision was made beginning at the proximal pole of the patella. After careful dissection of the quadriceps tendon (QT) from the overlying fascia, an 8–10 mm wide graft was outlined using a sterile ruler and scalpel. A stay suture was placed in the distal portion of the graft to facilitate handling (Fig. 
2
). Gentle distal traction was then applied, and a graft length of 80–100 mm was measured before completing the proximal incision and harvesting the graft. Graft dimensions were adjusted according to patient height. To preserve the integrity of the suprapatellar pouch and joint capsule, a partial-thickness QT graft was harvested (Fig. 
2
). The donor-site defect was closed with absorbable sutures (Vicryl; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) (Fig. 
2
). Finally, the proximal end of the QT graft was prepared using a FiberTag TightRope implant (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA).

Postoperative rehabilitation protocol
The postoperative protocol involved immobilization in a rigid brace in full extension for two weeks, with the affected limb kept non-weight-bearing. Subsequently, the range of motion was permitted from 0 to 90 degrees (extension-flexion) over the next four weeks, with mobilization involving partial weight-bearing at half body weight. In cases involving an additional meniscus repair, the range of motion was initially limited to 30 degrees of flexion after two weeks and subsequently increased by 30 degrees every two weeks. After six weeks, range of motion and weight-bearing were permitted for every patient.

Ultrasound examination and data acquisition
Data were collected under standardized laboratory conditions, with ambient room temperature maintained at 21 °C. Participants were positioned supine with the knee placed in neutral position during all measurements. A neutral knee position has previously been shown to yield the highest inter- and intraobserver reliability for PT assessment

All examinations were conducted by a single ultrasound certified examiner with more than seven years of experience in musculoskeletal ultrasound and elastography. Tendon evaluation by B-US
Conventional B-mode ultrasound was first performed using a 9-MHz linear transducer for anatomical orientation, exclusion of structural abnormalities, and identification of standardized anatomical landmarks for mid-portion measurements, in accordance with previously published protocols

Tendon evaluation by Color Doppler Ultrasound (CD-US)
CD-US was used to detect hypervascularized areas in the QT by systematically scanning the three tendons using CD-US. Any neovascularization, regardless of its strength, was considered as a positive finding

Statistical analysis
Ultrasound images for all B-mode and SWE measurements were digitized and exported as uncompressed TIFF files. For SWE values a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was performed to evaluate differences over time and between sides. The within-subject factors were time (preoperative, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months) and side (operated vs. contralateral control). For ROM measurements and scores a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was performed to evaluate differences over time.
Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared (ηₚ²). Statistical significance was set at 
p
 < 0.05. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Relationships between variables were assessed using the Pearson correlation coefficient (r).
The analysis was carried out with the SPSS statistics program (IBM Deutschland GmbH, Ehningen, Germany) for Windows (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, USA).
An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power (version 3.1, Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany)

,
15
,
16

with changes in shear wave velocity (SWV) over time defined as the primary outcome. Assuming a large standardized effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.40), a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05, a statistical power of 95%, and a repeated-measures ANOVA with four measurement time points, a minimum sample size of 20 patients was required.

Discussion
We hypothesized that QT mechanical properties undergo time-dependent changes following ACLR using a QT autograft. The hypothesis was supported by our findings, which demonstrated a significant time-by-side interaction, reflecting a transient increase in SWV of the operated limb at 3 months, followed by a decrease in stiffness at 6 months that nearly resolved by the 12-month follow-up. Over the 12-month follow-up period, significant improvements were observed in knee flexion as well as in the IKDC and Lysholm clinical scores, with the greatest gains occurring between the preoperative baseline and 6 months postoperatively. The Ohberg score exhibited a biphasic course with an initial increase at 3 months and subsequent normalization by 12 months. Extension showed only a non-significant trend toward improvement. No statistically significant correlations were found between QT mechanical properties and clinical scores, as well as between QT stiffness and range of motion.
Svensson et al. reported a progressive reduction of the donor-site gap in the PT between 6 weeks and 6 years following PT harvest for ACLR
57
. In most patients, thinning of the central portion of the patellar tendon was observed, whereas the peripheral tendon thickness was significantly increased compared with the contralateral healthy side for up to 2 years postoperatively
57
.
Consistent with these findings, Kartus et al. reported radiographic and histological abnormalities of the PT following harvest of its central third, with a mean follow of 26 months
58
. Although the donor-site gap progressively decreased over time, PT thickness was significantly increased compared with the intact contralateral patellar tendon, as demonstrated by both MRI and ultrasonography
58
.
Histological evaluation demonstrated a significant increase in cellularity and vascularity compared with normal control tendons. Notably, these changes were observed not only within the repair tissue of the central portion of the tendon, but also in the peripheral regions of the patellar tendon at the donor site
58
. Furthermore, a histological and ultrastructural analysis performed 10 years after harvest of the central third of the patellar tendon continued to reveal persistent alterations in tendon histology and ultrastructural, indicating long-term remodeling processes
59
.
In contrast, the ST appears to regenerate following harvest for ACLR, regaining a histological appearance similar to that of the non-harvested contralateral tendon
60
. Apart from residual alterations in fiber organization observed in a subset of tendons, no significant differences were identified between the regenerated and non-harvested ST with respect to fiber structure, cellularity, vascularity, or glycosaminoglycan content at a minimum of 6 years post-harvest
60
.
Shear wave elastography (SWE) has emerged as a valuable imaging modality for in vivo assessment of periarticular and tendon structure, using SWV (m/s) and Young’s modulus (kPa) to quantify mechanical properties
22
,
61
,
62
. Establishing reference values for medial collateral ligament or anterior talofibular ligament mechanical properties using SWE supports the objective assessment of ligament properties
23
,
61
. Burulday et al. highlight its value in assessing Achilles tendon stiffness in psoriatic arthritis, potentially detecting subclinical biomechanical changes beyond conventional ultrasound
62
.
In addition to radiological and histological evaluations, several studies have investigated sonographic patellar tendon (PT) stiffness following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) using a PT autograft. Akkaya et al. assessed PT strain ratios in 18 patients after bone–patellar tendon–bone (BPTB) ACLR, with a mean follow-up of 2 years. The PT on the operated side demonstrated increased thickness and reduced length, accompanied by decreased strain ratios at the donor site compared with the contralateral healthy tendon
14
. Gulledge et al. evaluated PT SWE in 30 patients who had undergone ACLR with a PT autograft within the preceding 40 months. Interestingly, SWV increased over time in the lateral region of the operated knee, while SWV decreased over time in the central region of the contralateral knee
15
. Ito et al. investigated PT structural properties at 1 month, 3–4 months, and 6–9 months following ACLR using a BPTB autograft, assessing medial, lateral, and central regions of the tendon
63
. In line with our findings on QT stiffness, shear modulus was elevated in all regions at 1 month postoperatively and progressively decreased over time, particularly in the central tendon region
63
. Suydam et al. evaluated 13 patients who underwent ACLR using a semitendinosus–gracilis (STG) autograft. Regrowth of the semitendinosus tendon was confirmed by B-mode ultrasound between 6 and 24 months postoperatively in 12 of 13 patients. The authors reported a higher shear modulus in the intact contralateral tendon compared with the regenerated tendon, along with a progressive increase in shear modulus of the regenerated tendon over time, indicating ongoing structural maturation
17
.
There is evidence that muscle weakness commonly occurs following knee joint injury, attributable in part to presynaptic reflex inhibition of the periarticular musculature after trauma or surgical intervention
64
. This arthrogenic muscle inhibition persists after ACL injury and during rehabilitation after ACLR
64
. Because muscle force generation and muscle stiffness influence the mechanical properties of associated tendons
65
, changes in tendon stiffness measurements may not be exclusively attributable to tendon harvest alone.
To date, there are two studies that investigated healing rates of QT after ACLR using ultrasound
20
,
21
. With measurements of intraoperative graft size volume and postoperative defect size volume of QT, a healing rate of 93% ± 9% of the donor site at 6.5 ± 0.7 months was observed in a study from Cognault et al.
21
. High healing rates could be detected after only 6 months.
Our study demonstrated a significant time-by-side interaction, reflecting a transient increase in mechanical properties of the operated quadriceps tendon at 3 months, followed by a decrease at 6 months and partial recovery by the 12-month follow-up. Although tendon mechanical properties progressively approached that of the contralateral side, residual side-to-side differences remained at 12 months. These findings are consistent with a dynamic tendon remodeling process after graft harvest, characterized by early postoperative alterations in mechanical properties and gradual recovery over time. Nevertheless, the persistence of side-to-side differences suggests that restoration of tendon mechanical properties may remain incomplete within the first postoperative year. A recent study from Inoue et al. evaluated QT morphology using ultrasound at a mean of 3.4 ± 3.5 years after ACLR
20
. While anterior-to-posterior thickness, was significantly greater in the operated knee, no significant differences were found in medial-to-lateral width or cross-sectional area. However, echogenicity was significantly lower in the harvested QT at all levels (
p
 < 0.01), suggesting potential changes in tendon quality even after 3.4 years
20
.
As histologic evaluation was not included in this study, it remains unclear if ultrastructural tendon morphology normalizes over time. Previous studies, that included MRI examinations and histologic PT evaluation after ACLR, reported radiographic and histologic abnormalities of PT even after 6 years
57
,
58
.
The ipsilateral quadriceps tendon (QT) demonstrated lower SWV compared with the contralateral side at all time points, except at 3 months postoperatively (t1). These differences may be attributable, at least in part, to alterations in gait mechanics and weight-bearing patterns following ACL rupture and subsequent reconstruction. Interestingly, the ipsilateral quadriceps tendon (QT) exhibited numerically lower SWV values than the contralateral tendon already at the preoperative assessment. This observation suggests that tendon mechanical properties may have been altered before surgery, potentially as a consequence of ACL injury–related unloading, neuromuscular adaptations, or pre-existing inter-limb differences, including limb dominance. Therefore, postoperative side-to-side differences should be interpreted with caution and cannot be attributed solely to graft harvest. Although SWV values approached baseline levels at 12 months, this finding does not necessarily indicate histological normalization but rather suggests some sort of functional recovery of mechanical properties.
From a clinical perspective, patients frequently express concern regarding the extent to which tendon harvest affects the structural integrity of the donor tendon. The present study provides insight not only into functional recovery, but also into the mechanical properties and in vivo quality of the QT following ACLR. Nevertheless, to further substantiate these findings, additional longitudinal investigations incorporating histological evaluation of the donor tendon are warranted. Such studies would provide valuable complementary information and represent an important direction for future research.
This study has several limitations. First, postoperative rehabilitation protocols were not fully standardized across all participants, which may have influenced shear wave elastography (SWE) measurements. While some patients underwent isolated ACLR, others required concomitant meniscal repair, necessitating a more restrictive postoperative rehabilitation regimen. Furthermore, individual variability in rehabilitation adherence and motivation, particularly with respect to regaining full range of motion and returning to sports, may have contributed to differences in tendon loading and stiffness during recovery. Subgroup analyses based on surgical and rehabilitation differences as well as age and sex might have provided more precise insights, but was underpowered due to the limited sample size. This represents an important potential confounder and should be addressed in future studies. In addition, patient-related factors that may affect SWE measurements were not systematically controlled. For example, smoking has been reported to negatively influence strain ratios of the donor-site patellar tendon
14
; however, smoking status was not included in the present analysis. Although the contralateral limb was not surgically treated, it may still be affected by reduced physical activity, unloading, rehabilitation, and changes in neuromuscular function after surgery. Therefore, the contralateral limb cannot necessarily be regarded as a completely healthy control. SWE measurements were limited to the central portion of the quadriceps tendon, corresponding to the graft harvest site. Regional assessment of the medial and lateral tendon portions may have yielded additional information regarding spatial patterns of tendon healing and remodeling. We did not include the mean time from ACL injury to ACLR. This may have given us valuable information regarding the influence of timing of ACRL. A post hoc power analysis indicated that with a sample size of 20 subjects, the study was sufficiently powered (power = 0.95, α = 0.05) to detect large effect sizes (Cohen’s f ≈ 0.42). However, smaller effect sizes may therefore not have been detected. In our cohort, the mean age was 29.84 ± 7.21years (range: 19–42), which is slightly higher than the typical population undergoing anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, often reported to be between 15 and 35 years of age. This difference is likely attributable to our recruitment strategy within a general orthopaedic clinical setting, which includes not only high-performance athletes but also recreationally active and older patients. We acknowledge that this may limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly to younger, high-level athletic populations. However, the inclusion of a broader age range may also enhance the applicability of our results to real-world clinical practice, where ACLR is increasingly performed in older and less active individuals. We did not include tendon thickness measurements or donor-site defect characteristics. This would have given us more detailed information about tendon healing and adaptions over time.
Conclusion
To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate in vivo quadriceps tendon (QT) mechanical properties following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) using SWE. Our findings indicate time-dependent changes in QT mechanical properties following ACLR using a QT autograft, as well as distinct differences between the operated and contralateral limbs after ACLR using QT autograft.

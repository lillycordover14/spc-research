Anterior Uveitis Basics: Cells/Flare Grading and When to Refer

A patient presents with a red, painful eye and sensitivity to light. Is it anterior uveitis—and how urgently does the patient need additional care?

For optometrists and optometry students, evaluating suspected anterior uveitis requires careful examination, consistent documentation, and timely clinical decisions. 
Anterior chamber cells and flare grading
 helps describe inflammation and track changes, but referral decisions also depend on vision, intraocular pressure, associated findings, and treatment response.

What Is Anterior Uveitis?

Anterior uveitis is inflammation primarily affecting the front portion of the uvea, including the iris and anterior ciliary body. Often called 
iritis
, it may occur without an identifiable cause or in association with infection, injury, or systemic inflammatory disease.

Acute anterior uveitis commonly causes eye pain, redness, photophobia, and blurred vision. It can affect one or both eyes and may recur. These symptoms warrant prompt evaluation because untreated uveitis can threaten vision.

What Are Cells and Flare?

During a slit-lamp examination, clinicians assess two related findings in the anterior chamber:

Cells:
 Inflammatory cells suspended in the aqueous humor, appearing as small illuminated particles.

Flare:
 Light scattering from protein in the aqueous humor following disruption of the blood–aqueous barrier.

Cells and flare should be evaluated and documented separately. Together, they help characterize inflammation, but they do not identify its cause. A complete examination remains essential.

Anterior Chamber Cells Grading: The SUN Scale

The 
Standardization of Uveitis Nomenclature (SUN)
 Working Group established a widely used scale for grading anterior chamber cells. The examiner counts cells within a 
1 mm × 1 mm slit beam
.

SUN grade

Cells in the field

0

Fewer than 1

0.5+

1–5

1+

6–15

2+

16–25

3+

26–50

4+

More than 50

Use the same examination technique at follow-up visits to support meaningful comparisons. Record a 
hypopyon
, or layering of inflammatory cells in the anterior chamber, separately rather than treating it as another cell grade.

SUN grades are ordered categories, not a linear measurement: 2+ does not mean twice as much inflammation as 1+.

Anterior Chamber Flare Grading

The SUN flare scale describes the intensity of light scattering and its effect on the visibility of iris and lens details.

SUN grade

Flare appearance

0

No flare

1+

Faint flare

2+

Moderate flare; iris and lens details remain clear

3+

Marked flare; iris and lens details appear hazy

4+

Intense flare with fibrin or “plastic aqueous”

Unlike the cell scale, the SUN flare scale does not include a 0.5+ category. Document the findings individually—for example, 
“anterior chamber: 2+ cells, 1+ flare”
—so subsequent examiners can follow each component.

Look Beyond the Anterior Chamber

Cells and flare are part of the assessment, not the entire diagnosis. Other causes of a painful red eye, including keratitis and scleritis, may produce overlapping symptoms.

The initial examination should assess visual acuity, intraocular pressure, the cornea, pupil, and other anterior segment structures. Look for keratic precipitates, fibrin, and posterior synechiae—adhesions between the iris and lens. A dilated fundus examination helps determine whether inflammation also involves the posterior segment, which can change the diagnosis and management plan.

When Should Anterior Uveitis Be Referred?

A cell grade alone does not determine referral urgency.
 Consider the complete clinical picture and whether the clinician can safely diagnose, treat, and monitor the condition within their training and applicable scope of practice.

Arrange Same-Day Ophthalmic Assessment for Concerning Findings

Severe pain, substantial visual impairment, elevated intraocular pressure, or inflammation beyond the anterior segment should prompt urgent escalation. A hypopyon or concern for an infectious process also warrants immediate clinical discussion to determine the appropriate emergency pathway.

Patients with suspected acute anterior uveitis need prompt eye care. If the diagnosis is uncertain or appropriate treatment and follow-up cannot be provided, arrange urgent ophthalmic assessment rather than delaying care.

Refer for Persistent, Recurrent, or Complicated Disease

Uveitis specialist consultation is appropriate when inflammation:

Persists or worsens despite treatment.

Recurs frequently or returns during medication tapering.

Requires prolonged corticosteroid treatment.

Is accompanied by steroid-related complications, such as ocular hypertension or cataract.

Requires consideration of systemic or steroid-sparing therapy.

The timing depends on severity and trajectory. Worsening findings require earlier reassessment; patients should not wait for a scheduled follow-up if vision declines or pain increases.

Treatment and Follow-Up Basics

For appropriate cases of noninfectious anterior uveitis, treatment commonly includes topical corticosteroids and a cycloplegic medication to reduce pain and help prevent posterior synechiae. Suspected infectious causes require a different treatment strategy and may require specialist testing.

Medication selection, dosing, and tapering must be individualized. Follow-up should assess inflammation, visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and complications. Symptom relief alone does not establish that inflammation has resolved. Regular monitoring is also necessary because corticosteroids can increase the risk of glaucoma and cataracts.

Frequently Asked Questions About Anterior Uveitis

What does 1+ anterior chamber cells mean?

On the SUN scale, 1+ means the examiner observes 
6–15 cells within a 1 mm × 1 mm slit-beam field
. It describes one examination finding; it does not independently determine treatment or referral urgency.

Can anterior uveitis come back?

Yes. Anterior uveitis can recur. Repeated episodes may prompt investigation for associated systemic inflammatory disease and reconsideration of the long-term management plan.

What should patients do if they develop symptoms?

Patients with eye pain, redness, light sensitivity, or blurred vision should contact an eye care professional promptly. Sudden or worsening vision changes require urgent assessment.

For future optometrists, anterior uveitis connects careful observation with clinical judgment. Consistent cells and flare grading supports clear communication, while a complete examination and timely referral help protect vision.

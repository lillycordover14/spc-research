Serval, a startup that uses AI agents to automate corporate IT operations, announced on October 29 that it raised $150 million in a Series C round. The valuation is $3 billion (approximately ¥470 billion), and the round was led by Sequoia Capital. It is the company's third raise in 12 months, bringing cumulative funding to $277 million.

Serval raised $75 million in a Series B round in December 2024 at a valuation of $1 billion at the time. The latest round included existing investors Elad Gil, Evantic, First Round, General Catalyst and Redpoint, with Conviction and Headline joining as new investors. Headcount has surged from about 20 employees in January to roughly 200 now, and CEO Jake Stauffer has outlined plans to reach at least 700 by the end of next year.

The core product is Catalyst, an automation agent. Staff in IT, security, HR, finance and legal can build and run code-based business workflows without any engineering experience. AI agents convert business requirements into standardized processes, while administrators retain control over permission checks and approvals. Co-founder and CTO Alex McLeod described a system in which built automations are tested in a separate environment with branching, access controls and audit logs before being released to employee-facing agents.

Serval is explicitly positioning itself as a replacement for IT service management giant ServiceNow. According to the company, one automaker completed its migration from ServiceNow in 45 days — an enterprise-wide move spanning IT service management, the configuration management database, and hardware and software asset management. Annual recurring revenue has doubled every quarter since the Series B, and two Fortune 20 companies adopted the platform in the past six months. Customers include Fox Corporation, WPP, Live Nation and Ramp.

Stauffer said the company had "years of runway" in the bank and framed the raise as seizing an opportunity at a moment when large enterprises are deciding right now whether to stick with incumbent vendors. He also noted that ServiceNow's market capitalization stood at roughly $150 billion ahead of this week's earnings release, down from a peak of about $230 billion, and said the ambition to reach comparable scale in the future is becoming more realistic.

The proceeds will be directed toward hiring. Serval plans to add more than 500 people over the next 12 months, including more than 100 automation engineers for ProServal, a newly created professional services unit. Their role will be to work alongside customer staff to identify automatable tasks and rebuild existing processes; about half will be new graduates, who will be stationed at client sites after four weeks of training.

Sequoia's Anas Biad said Serval is "on track to become the fastest-growing top-down enterprise system of record company in history," and expressed the view that the company holds proprietary intellectual property in both its platform and its system of record.

ServiceNow, for its part, is pushing back. Sean Regan, head of the Flow by ServiceNow division, said in a statement that "every AI agent needs trusted data, workflows and governance to act — and that isn't easily replicated," arguing that the difficulty of real-world operations is precisely where incumbents have the advantage. ServiceNow launched Flow, a standalone AI service desk, on October 1, priced at $2 per conversation on a usage basis and available by credit card even to companies without a ServiceNow contract.

ServiceNow's third-quarter results, announced October 28, showed revenue of $4.11 billion, slightly beating market expectations, but earnings per share of $1.00 fell short of the $1.02 consensus. Its fourth-quarter revenue guidance of $4.25 billion to $4.26 billion came in below the $4.36 billion market forecast. The stock fell more than 17% in after-hours trading and dropped more than 26% in the following session on October 29.

On its earnings call, ServiceNow said agentic AI use in production environments grew more than 13-fold year on year, renewal rates stood at 98%, and the number of AI-related ITSM deals rose about 80%. It raised its 2026 target for AI-related annual contract value to $1.7 billion from $1.5 billion.

Serval's strategy of taking direct aim at ServiceNow by name is consistent in how it pitches investors. Stauffer recalled that when he ranked large enterprise software companies by size and picked candidates that AI could disrupt, ServiceNow came out near the top. His logic: the essence of workflow automation lies in drag-and-drop and custom configuration, and code written by AI sharply lowers that cost. He remains skeptical of incumbents' efforts to bolt AI onto existing products, saying he wants to watch actual utilization rates.

The battle for control of IT operations management is beginning to change the very nature of renewal contracts. As switching becomes easier, large contracts that were previously assumed to renew automatically may be exposed to competitive bidding. ServiceNow's stock plunge reflects market concern that AI-driven business automation will shake the profit structure of the existing license-based business. Serval's ARR figures remain undisclosed, leaving outsiders with limited evidence to verify its rapid growth. Even so, the fact that it has raised three times in 12 months shows that money is gathering behind the bid to change the guard in enterprise software.

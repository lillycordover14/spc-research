The sky suddenly darkens and heavy rain pours down with the rumble of thunder. As you hurry along under an open umbrella, you may worry whether it is safe to use it when lightning is striking. After the rain stops, the air may feel cleaner, but for people with asthma this kind of weather can actually be more dangerous.

Thunderstorms do more than just dump rain and light up the sky. They affect respiratory health in ways we rarely consider and can turn everyday actions into dangerous ones. What should we be especially careful about when thunder and lightning are in the forecast?

● On thunderstorm days, asthma ER visits jump sixfold

Rain accompanied by thunder and lightning is called a “thunderstorm.” When a thunderstorm develops, people with asthma need to pay closer attention to their breathing than usual. According to research presented last November at the annual meeting of the American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (ACAAI), emergency room visits due to asthma were found to be six times higher during thunderstorms.

A research team led by Diala Merheb, an internal medicine resident at the University of Kansas Medical Center, compared 4,439 asthma-related emergency room visits with weather records from the same periods. They found a clear association between thunderstorm conditions and an increase in ER visits by asthma patients.

The reason asthma worsens on stormy, lightning-filled days is hidden in the rain. Pollen and mold spores floating in the air are among the main allergens that trigger asthma. When a thunderstorm forms, strong winds and high humidity can cause these particles to break into smaller fragments or release allergenic substances from inside them.

Once fragmented, the particles can penetrate deeper into the lungs when inhaled. Substances that would normally be trapped in the nose or larger airways are broken down into finer particles that irritate the airways of people with asthma. This is why, contrary to the expectation that rain will wash pollen away and clean the air, asthma symptoms can actually worsen on days with thunder and lightning.

This sudden worsening of asthma symptoms associated with thunderstorms is known as “thunderstorm asthma.” People with pollen allergies or poorly controlled asthma need to be particularly cautious. The research team emphasized that on days when thunderstorms are forecast, people should limit outdoor activities, and asthma patients should use their prescribed medications appropriately and prepare for possible symptom flare-ups.

● Does using an umbrella to avoid the rain raise your lightning risk?

On thunderstorm days, you need to be careful not only about your respiratory health but also about lightning strike accidents. When rain pours down, we instinctively open umbrellas, but outdoors in open areas without safe shelter nearby, umbrellas can increase the risk of being struck by lightning.

Lightning occurs when ice particles in clouds collide, causing electrical charges to separate and creating a large electrical difference between the cloud and the ground. When lightning travels from the cloud down to the ground, it is called a “cloud-to-ground strike.”

Most umbrellas have metal ribs that conduct electricity well. Holding an umbrella high above your head can influence where lightning strikes, depending on the surrounding environment, and if lightning current flows through the umbrella into the body, it can cause severe damage to the heart or nervous system.

A mannequin holding an umbrella is struck by artificial lightning in a laboratory experiment. Korea Electrotechnology Research Institute

In 2022, the Korea Electrotechnology Research Institute carried out artificial lightning experiments on a mannequin holding an umbrella and another mannequin holding nothing. In the experiments, the mannequin with the umbrella was exposed to lightning strikes more often than the one without.

However, using an umbrella does not mean you will inevitably be hit by lightning. The risk of a strike depends on various factors, including the surrounding terrain, the height of nearby objects, and the position of the thunderstorm. The safest approach is to avoid staying outdoors under an umbrella once thunder and lightning start, and instead seek shelter inside a sturdy building or a vehicle with a fully enclosed roof.

● A single lightning bolt can stretch 829 km—longer than the Seoul–Busan distance

Lightning appears as a brief flash of light, but in reality it can extend over distances that are hard to imagine.

 

In July last year, the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) officially recognized a lightning event that occurred on 22 October 2017 over the Great Plains region as the longest lightning bolt ever recorded. The bolt stretched an astonishing 829 km—more than twice the straight-line distance between Seoul and Busan.

Lightning striking across the night sky. Larry Vink·Pexels

This lightning bolt extended from eastern Texas to near Kansas City, Missouri. It surpassed the previous record of 768 km set in 2020. The fact that it was only recognized as a record almost eight years after it occurred is thanks to advances in satellite observation technology. In the past, ground-based instruments alone could not accurately capture the full length of lightning, but geostationary satellites that monitor lightning over wide areas have made it possible to measure the scale of ultra-long lightning events.

Lightning has set astonishing records not only in length but also in duration. A lightning event that occurred in June 2020 over northern Argentina and Uruguay lasted 17.1 seconds, earning recognition for the longest duration.

These records show that lightning can occur over much wider areas than the small region we see flash before our eyes. The WMO advises that whenever thunderstorms develop, people should avoid outdoor activities and seek safe shelter, even if the visible lightning seems far away.

Thunderstorms are more than just heavy rain or bright flashes in the sky. They can alter airborne allergens and affect respiratory health, and they can extend lightning strike risks over distances far greater than expected. Once you hear thunder, it is wise not only to think about staying dry, but also to look first for ways to keep your body safe.

A plant frost jacket is such a simple, straightforward way to keep plants alive and well over winter, and modern designs with drawstring bases and ventilating zips make it easier than ever to use them. When winterizing a garden, zippered plant jackets don't only protect tender beauties like banana plants and tree ferns but can help to stop sensitive souls like olive trees from dropping their leaves in a cold snap, shield citrus trees from the occasional frost if you garden in zone 9, and prevent palms from rotting.
Fleece 
plant covers
 are also incredibly useful to cocoon early-blooming shrubs like camellia and magnolia if a frost hits just as they're setting their buds, so you can save the flower show. Young fruit trees, too, can benefit from this extra layer to stop their thin bark splitting in freezing winter conditions, and a plant cover can shield all sorts of other plants from damage caused by snow, sleet and strong winds.
Credit: Getty Images
So, if you're getting ready to 
winterize plants
 as you 
prepare your garden for the onset of cold weather
, plant jackets should be part of your plan. Not all fleece plant covers are created equal, however, so if you want them to actually 
work
, you need to take a close look at their weight rating. The material used to make frost jackets comes in different weights and, just like comforters offer various levels of warmth, that directly impacts how well it will 
protect plants in winter
, but also the more sunshine it'll block.
How are Plant Frost Jackets Rated?
Horticultural fleece
 is a non-woven material typically made from polypropylene, and its weight determines how much protection it offers your plant, measured in grams per square meter (gsm) or ounces per square yard (oz/sq yd). Check the label or online description to find the rating. Any good-quality frost jacket will state the rating and the higher the number, the more insulation it will provide but also block more sunlight.

30-35gsm or 0.9-1.2oz/sq yd indicates a medium-weight fleece, offering around 4°F of protection and letting in about 65% of sunlight.
50-60gsm or 1.5-1.7oz/sq yd is a heavy-weight fleece, giving about 7°F of protection but only allowing around 50% of sunlight through.
70-100gsm or 2-2.8oz/sq yd is an ultra heavy-weight fleece, providing around 8-10°F of protection and letting in around 20-30% of sunlight.
You'll also see horticultural fleece rated 17gsm or 0.5oz/sq yd and this is designed not for long-term winterizing but for throwing over 
late-season veggie crops
 to protect them from frost. This ultra-lightweight fleece lets in around 85% of sunlight but only offers about 2°F of thermal protection.
Armed with this knowledge, you can choose the exact right level of protection for whatever plant you're winterizing in 
your USDA zone
. While you need to ensure your plant is getting the right level of cold protection, you also need to cater for its sunlight needs.

Winterizing Early Spring-Flowering Shrubs
Hardy shrubs that flower early in the spring
 such as 
camellias
 and 
magnolias
 are typically tough enough to survive most winters without a jacket, but their flowerbuds are quickly killed by a late frost or cold snap. Throw on a medium- or heavy-weight plant jacket overnight to save those new buds from the frost.
Winterizing Evergreen Plants
Evergreen plants
 such as conifers still need sunlight and fresh air to survive through winter, so a medium-weight fleece is your best bet for general winterizing if your aim is to shield a hardy plant from snow, hail and wind damage. If you're planning on leaving it on permanently through winter, use a medium-duty fleece for more tender evergreen olive and citrus trees and certain palms, too.

Most medium-duty fleeces are designed without a zipper but that's not a problem: because the thinner material lets more air and sunlight through, they can safely be left in place.

How to Use a Plant Frost Jacket Effectively
No fleece jacket can generate heat, it can only insulate from the cold, so how you use it has a huge impact on how effectively it protects your plant in winter. Choose a design with both a drawstring and a zip as this not only makes the jacket quick and easy to pop on, but allows you to fit it closely and avoids the wind ripping it off.

Wherever possible, peg the plant jacket to the ground to catch any heat rising upwards. Soil holds onto considerable warmth from the sun, so make use of this natural radiator. Use bricks or stones to weigh the jacket base down, or peg it to the ground with landscape fabric pegs, choosing a product with gaskets to prevent the fabric tearing like these 
Galvanized Landscape Pins from Amazon
.
Similarly, if you're 
winterizing a plant in a container
, use a frost jacket that's big enough to fit around the pot, too, as this will protect the roots as well as trap any heat from the soil. Roots in planters are very vulnerable to freezing temperatures so consider wrapping the pot with bubble wrap first.
To prevent moisture building up under the cover and causing mold, make sure you buy a jacket marked as breathable, and open the zip on mild days to allow air to circulate.
While you don't want to squash a plant into a cover, it's a wise idea to fit the jacket around the plant to prevent it flapping in the wind and

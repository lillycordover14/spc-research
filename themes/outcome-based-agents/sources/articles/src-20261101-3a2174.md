Portland Book Festival: Violinist Anthea Kreston’s ‘Crescendo’ is a looping, crazed capriccio of a memoir

The Corvallis resident will appear in a pop-up at the Nov. 7 festival, as well as at three “Cover to Cover” events during festival week.

Anthea Kreston has been playing violin since she was 2
½
 years old and a student at the Suzuki Institute. She has written a memoir about the career that took her to Carnegie Hall, Lincoln Center, and all over Europe. Photo courtesy: Anthea Kreston

In the mid-’70s, a little girl travels with her mother and sisters from their Chicago-area home to the Suzuki Institute at the University of Wisconsin. She totes a minuscule 1/64 violin. She is 2
½
 years old.

The child who will become 
Anthea Kreston
 starts playing on that tiny fiddle with scores of other children in the famed Suzuki program. She never stops.

Crescendo
 is Kreston’s immensely readable memoir of how a Midwestern girl becomes a concert violinist, starting with that tiny violin. Mostly with chamber groups, sometimes as a soloist, sometimes as concertmistress, she’s played most every major stage: Carnegie Hall, Lincoln Center, the Portland Opera, and all over Europe.

It’s a varied career. Off the stage, she performs as a strolling violinist at an Italian restaurant while in college. She gets out of a traffic ticket by playing 
Danny Boy
 for an Irish cop.

Her propulsive writing draws the reader from sentence to paragraph, from thought to action to meditation about what music is, what art is, how to create and keep creating. How to play and keep playing, practicing for hours every single day, even when the pressure of touring threatens your family. Even when your violin, constant companion since you were 14 years old, is stolen on a train in Germany.

Kreston is on her way to a concert when the instrument is taken, and a cluster of other professionals immediately steps up to offer their own violins for her to play that night. She choses one, discovering only after the concert that it is an Amati.

Did she ever recover her lost violin? Read the book to find out; it’s short and breezy and fun.

Sponsor

Crescendo
 is no staid symphony, no organized delineation of a life. It’s more of a crazed capriccio, full of trills and thrills and fireworks. The narrative loops back and forth in time, with auditions for the prestigious Artemis Quartet (spoiler: she gets the job), interspersed with her time in a Portland commune, touring Asia with Yo-Yo Ma, or setting up a summer music camp in Wisconsin with her husband and collaborator, the cellist Jason Duckles.

“Life doesn’t go in a smooth line, or follow the same tempo beginning to end, like a perpetual motion,” she writes. “It’s more like a mosaic” — and that is how the book is constructed, moving forward with an enticing story, then going back in time to fit in another tile or two.

In this buzzsaw of a book, Kreston, who will make 
several appearances in Portland
 this week as part of the Portland Book Festival, writes with a voice that is fresh and unspoiled. She bares her beating heart for the reader, recounting a childhood of such random violence that after she leaves home at 16, alone and with just a few dollars saved from babysitting, she changes her first name to Anthea.

And everywhere, everywhere, she carries classical music.

Asked in an interview whether she fears change, Kreston laughs. “No, no. Certainly not. I love change. I love it. I don’t fear anything. I have a lack of fear at all times. I look forward to a fearful situation because I love to find solutions.”

And on top of that, as a teacher and mentor, “I love helping other people through change or fear or doubt.”

She is a fearless writer. The challenges of her life and career are tough: extreme competition, childhood trauma, a struggle with infertility, trying to keep a family together while touring and playing concerts.

Sponsor

Now, at age 54, after a career that’s taken her all over the world, she has settled in Corvallis with her husband and their two teenage daughters.

“I love Corvallis!” she says. “I like the variety of people, how oddball and kooky they are. I like the size of the town. The downtown is totally charming.”

She is still free to play internationally when opportunities arise. “I wouldn’t say that any of my career is location based.”

During the Portland Book Festival, Kreston will appear in a pop-up at noon Nov. 7 in the Portland Art Museum — that event is part of the main festival, which is sold out. But she will also be at three “
Cover to Cover
” events before and after:

6 p.m. Monday, Nov. 2, at Broadway Books in conversation with Portland-based violinist Ling Ling Huang, free;

10:30 a.m. Sunday, Nov. 8, at ChatterPDX with poet Tina Chang, 
tickets
 $5-$20;

3 p.m. Sunday, Nov. 8 in concert with the Jewish Community Orchestra at Mittleman Jewish Community Center, 
tickets
 $8-$20.

Kreston teaches privately and is involved in chamber groups, including the 
Delgani String Quartet
, which presents a series of five programs this year, playing in Corvallis, Salem, Portland, and Eugene, with a virtual option.

She is the 2026-27 Artist in Residence at All Classical Radio. She writes a 
blog
.

Her new podcast, 
Velvet Metronome
, debuted this month with an interview with veteran Portland cellist Nancy Ives.

Sponsor

Kreston is working on a new book, 
Unstoppable,
 which she describes as stories about people who have persevered and survived and thrived in the face of insurmountable odds. It will be about her own story as well, about loss, both immediate and slow and inevitable.

“I love writing,” she says. And it shows.

Anthea Kreston is a virtuoso violinist, with a career studded with accolades and promise.

And, as is evident in this book, she is also a virtuoso writer.

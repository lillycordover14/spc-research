A New Twist on Collagen: Upcycled Jojoba Fermentation for Adaptive Skin Care
 

		This paper describes skin care technology made from fermented, upcycled jojoba cake, and a new non-invasive spectroscopic technique for directly measuring collagen synthesis in vivo.

For decades, 
collagen
 has been one of beauty’s most recognizable anti-aging references, a shorthand for firmness, elasticity and youthful-looking skin. Today, however, the industry is approaching collagen with greater scientific depth. Rather than treating it as a single ingredient or a surface-level claim, researchers and product developers increasingly view collagen as part of a dynamic extracellular matrix whose production, organization and degradation influence skin’s long-term resilience. Type I collagen decline is closely associated with dermal thinning, loss of elasticity, wrinkles and sagging, making collagen preservation and renewal central to the emerging concept of 
skin longevity
.
1,2

This evolution reflects a broader shift from correcting visible signs of aging toward extending skin overall health. Consumers are connecting skin appearance with wellness, nutrition, recovery and preventive care.
3
 Collagen now sits at the intersection of topical skincare, beauty supplements, protein intake, professional procedures and healthy-aging routines. The opportunity for beauty brands is therefore expanding beyond products that simply contain collagen toward solutions designed to protect the existing matrix, support fibroblast activity and stimulate the skin’s own regenerative processes.

At the same time, peptides are becoming increasingly prominent in longevity-focused skincare.
4,5
 Their appeal reflects a growing appetite for targeted biological solutions: ingredients positioned not only by what they are, but by how they interact with pathways involved in collagen synthesis, fibroblast activity, inflammation and barrier function. Scientific reviews describe peptides as a diverse class that may support collagen and elastin synthesis, cellular proliferation and matrix integrity, although efficacy depends on peptide identity, concentration, delivery and supporting evidence. With the advent of 
GLP-1
 antagonists to help individuals lose weight, rapidly, there comes a somewhat unwanted condition, now often referred to as Ozempic skin, where the weight loss causes unwanted wrinkling and sagging of the skin, appearing to accelerate aging.
6
 

Together, these trends point toward the need for a more sophisticated collagen strategy. Applying intact collagen to the skin may provide hydration and film-forming benefits, but it does not necessarily rebuild the dermal matrix. The more compelling opportunity is to help skin support its own collagen-production mechanisms.

It is within this context that Stratasync Jojoba becomes particularly relevant. This fermented, jojoba-derived active was developed to connect the industry’s interest in collagen, peptides and longevity with measurable biological and clinical performance, including increased collagen-related activity and improvements in firmness, elasticity and visible skin quality. This paper will examine the development of a new, innovative skin care technology based on the fermentation of upcycled jojoba cake created at Vantage’s own operations where jojoba oil is produced. 

This new work will delve more deeply into the clinical work done to examine the efficacy of the ferment to improve skin of the face, neck and decolletage. In addition, the paper will expand initial discussion on the use of a new non-invasive spectroscopic technique called diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) that allowed for the direct in vivo examination of collagen synthesis in the skin
7
.

Background

The methods to prepare the 
Lactobacillus plantarum
 jojoba cake ferment and various in vitro and in situ assays done to understand the composition and skin efficacy targets of the ferment were described in detail in an earlier publication.
7
 

A 56-day clinical study was done on 46 individuals experiencing décolletage aging consistent with a rating of 2-3 in the décolletage aging parameters established by Kerscher et al.
8
 Twenty-three individuals were randomly selected to use a placebo cream, and the other 23 individuals used the active formulation. The only difference between the two creams was that the active cream contained 1.0% of the 
Lactobacillus plantarum
 jojoba cake ferment and the placebo cream removed the ferment and replaced it with 1.0% more water. The participants applied prescribed amounts of the cream to their entire face, neck and décolletage twice a day for 56 days. The individuals were brought into the testing lab at day-0, day-28 and day-56, and their skin was allowed to acclimate to a controlled temperature and humidity for at least ½ hour before any analysis was conducted. 

The participants were analyzed for the following non-invasive skin parameters:

Skin Hydration [Corneometer, CM 825 – Courage + Khazaka electronic GmbH]

Skin Texture [Digital Image Analysis, Camera Nikon D7500]

Skin Firmness and Elasticity [Cutometer]

Skin Wrinkle Intensity [Digital Image Analysis, Camera Nikon D7500] 

Figure 1. Facial sites where diffuse reflectance spectroscopic analysis images were taken.

The individuals were also analyzed for their collagen fluorescent signal, from the 10 facial sites shown in Figure 1, on day-0 (baseline), day-28, and day-56 to determine their changes in collagen fluorescence signaling.
7
 Fourteen randomly selected individuals who used the active formulation were further analyzed using DRS at day-0, day-28 and day-56. The combined average collagen fluorescence signals from the same 14 individuals at each time point were used to create a composite single facial image showing the changes in collagen fluorescence normalized against the tryptophane fluorescence as described above. Spectral acquisition was done on only one side of the face, and the data acquired were mirrored to the contralateral side to provide a visual reconstruction of a complete facial map.

At the end of the 56-day treatment period, individuals using either the placebo or active formulation were asked to complete a questionnaire assessing their perception of the treatment’s efficacy across several criteria

Skin Hydration

The skin hydration was measured using a corneometer at 30 minutes after initial application of the test formulations and then on days 28 and 56. The results of the skin hydration measurements are shown in Figure 2.

Figure 2. Results of skin hydration at time 30 minutes, and at days 28 and 56.  

The hydration data shows that the ferment was able to increase skin moisture quickly and then maintain skin hydration at statistically significant levels at days 28 and 56 against both the baseline measurement (single asterisk) and against the placebo formulation (double asterisk). The placebo formulation was able to improve skin hydration at all three times as well against the baseline.

Skin Texture
 

Skin texture can be measured using digital imaging analysis using the Nikon D7500. Images of the types of measurements that can be made on the face, neck and decolletage are shown in Figure 3. 

Figure 3. Typical images of the face (left), neck (middle) and decolletage (right) used to calculate skin texture data.

The data collected from the image analysis demonstrates that the application of the formulation containing the ferment was able to improve the skin texture at all three tested areas showing statistically significant improvements against baseline and placebo as shown in Figure 4. The placebo (gray bars) did not show statistically significant response in skin texture at either time point.

Figure 4. Results of skin texture analysis of the face (left), neck (middle) and decolletage (right) at days 28 and 56 vs baseline and placebo.  

Skin Firmness and Elasticity

The measurement of the skin firmness and elasticity was only done on the face as the placement of the cutometer on the neck and decolletage was problematic. The results of the skin firmness and elasticity of the face at days 28 and 56 are shown in Figure 5.

Figure 5. Measurement of facial skin firmness and elasticity on days 28 and 56 showing results against placebo and baseline.

It can be seen from the skin firmness and elasticity data that the formulation containing the active ferment was able to statistically increase both firmness and elasticity at days 28 and 56 when compared against the placebo (gray bars) and baseline measurement. The placebo formulation showed statistically significant improvements against the baseline measurement at both time points, too.

Skin Wrinkle Intensity

The skin wrinkle intensity can be measured using digital imaging analysis. An example of a typical digital image of the eye crow’s feet wrinkles and the neck wrinkles is shown in Figure 6.

Figure 6. Typical digital images and wrinkle intensity analysis of image from the crow’s feet around the eyes (left) and the neck (right). The blue colors indicate deeper wrinkle intensity.

It was noted that somewhat surprisingly, measuring wrinkles on the decolletage proved difficult. However, using digital imaging analysis it was possible to measure the wrinkle intensity on both the face around the eyes and on the neck to one side of the chin. The results of the analysis of the wrinkle intensity at days 28 and 56 are shown in Figure 7.

Figure 7. Results of measurement of wrinkle intensity at days 28 and 56 for the face (right) and neck (left).

The ferment active formulation was able to statistically improve wrinkle intensity in both the face and neck as measured against both baseline and placebo. The placebo formulation did not statistically improve the winkle intensity when measured against the baseline at either time point.

In Vivo Collagen Synthesis Fluorescence Intensity by Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy

Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy is an emerging spectroscopic technique that allows for the visual examination of the collagen signal in the skin.
7,9
 A typical image of a person’s face analyzed at baseline (left), day 28 (middle) and day 56 (right) using DRS is shown in Figure 8. Using this technique, data from all the participants in both the placebo formulation group and the active ferment formulation group are shown in Figure 9. 

Figure 8. Typical image of a volunteer’s face analyzed for collagen synthesis using diffuse reflectance spectroscopy.

Figure 9. Results of collagen synthesis fluorescent measurements run on a Fluoromax Spectrofluometer 4C-TCSPC (Horiba Scientific) on the face (left), neck (middle) and decolletage (right) at days 28 and 56.

The measurements demonstrate that the jojoba cake ferment was able to statistically improve collagen fluorescence signaling at days 28 and 56 when measured against the baseline and the placebo. The placebo formulation did not show statistically significant improvements in collagen signaling at either time against baseline. 

To gain an appreciation for the power of this technique to non-invasively measure collagen expression in the skin, a composite image taken from 14 individuals who employed the formulation containing the jojoba cake ferment is shown in Figure 10. 

Figure 10. Composite full facial image of 14 individuals analyzed by diffuse reflectance spectroscopy for their collagen signal intensity shown at baseline (left), day 28 (middle) and day 56 (right). The small scale indicates the color changes anticipated for greater collagen synthesis.

Conclusions

The development of this new jojoba cake ferment illustrates how sustainability, biotechnology and skin science can converge to create opportunities for cosmetic ingredient innovation. Jojoba cake, a residual by-product of jojoba oil production, has been upcycled through fermentation with 
Lactobacillus plantarum
 to transform naturally occurring proteins, polysaccharides and other components into lower-molecular-weight biomolecules with greater potential bioavailability to the skin. In addition to the studies reported on here, Vantage has recently completed sophisticated peptide analysis of the 
Lactobacillus
 jojoba cake ferment and is beginning to better understand the peptide compositional makeup of this new fermentation technology. These peptide investigative results will be reported on later.

Figure 12. Conversion of residual jojoba cake, an upcycled by-product of jojoba oil manufacturing, by fermentation using  Lactobacillus plantarum helps to reduce high molecular weight jojoba cake components, like proteins and polysaccharides to lower molecular weight biomolecules like peptides and oligosaccharides.

The results of a placebo-controlled clinical study demonstrate that the fermented jojoba cake ingredient, used at 1.0%, delivers measurable benefits across several consumer-relevant signs of skin aging. After 28 and 56 days of use, the active formulation produced statistically significant improvements in skin hydration, texture, firmness and elasticity, and wrinkle intensity compared with baseline and placebo, with benefits demonstrated across multiple areas including not only the face, but also the neck and décolletage. These improvements were complemented by an increase in collagen fluorescence signaling, supporting earlier in vitro findings showing increased type I collagen expression in human fibroblasts.
7
 Diffuse reflectance spectroscopy provided a novel, non-invasive means of visualizing this response in vivo and helped connect the observed clinical improvements with changes occurring within the skin.

This work demonstrates the broader potential of circular ingredient design in personal care. By combining an upcycled botanical resource with fermentation biotechnology and rigorous clinical evaluation, it is possible to create ingredients that address two increasingly important expectations of the beauty industry: meaningful skin performance and a more responsible use of natural resources. 

As the beauty industry continues to seek technologies that support healthier-looking skin while making better use of existing resources, fermentation of upcycled botanical materials represents a promising platform for future innovation. In this context, jojoba cake is no longer simply a residue of jojoba oil production, but a renewable resource from which a new generation of high-value, scientifically supported skin care ingredients can be developed.

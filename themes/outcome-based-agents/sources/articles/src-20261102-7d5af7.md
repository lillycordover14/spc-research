Druid AI co-founder and COO Andreea Pleșea wrote her 2011 PhD on software agents that negotiated over bricks described differently by various buyers and sellers. She explains the role of ontology in getting agentic enterprise systems to understand each other.

[page text]
Andreea Pleșea

All major enterprise software vendors are now building their own agentic platforms. These platforms are getting better at connecting processes that are built on their platforms. But then these agents all struggle with the ontology problem, or what things mean on different systems. Solving this problem is becoming the salient edge of multi-agent systems integration.

Druid AI started solving this problem at its founding in 2018, sorting out issues in building conversational assistants. It was essentially the front end of the integration problem that robotic process automation was trying to smooth out on the back end.

Andreea Pleșea, Druid AI’s co-founder and Chief Operating Officer, started working on this problem 15 years ago when software agents were a research topic. The hard part was getting two programs to agree on what a word meant. She wrote her PhD thesis on business ontologies, formal maps of the concepts used within and between businesses and how they relate to each other. She explains the hard part was trying to get agents buying and selling things to find common ground, using bricks as a simple example:

It was not only the type of brick, but the way one was advertising its offer in different ways, using synonyms, while the other one had a particular request. So, if we extrapolate the primitive way of agents communicating with each other to what happens today, it’s like matching users’ intent versus the agents that are performing a certain job.

The vocabulary problem

Pleșea spent the years after her PhD at TotalSoft, a Romanian business software company, leading development of self-service applications that sat on top of its ERP system, because employees struggled to find their way around information scattered across different systems. She argues that the agents vendors are now adding to those systems of record are rebuilding the same maze:

Every ERP now, every CRM tool that companies are using, comes with its own prebuilt agents, which puts companies back in the same picture as they were in 2011 or earlier, where everyone had systems, but they didn’t know where the information came from, and they didn’t have a single point of truth. Whom should I talk to if I want to get information from Salesforce? I go to the Salesforce agent. Now I’m going to the other CRM agent. So, there is this need for unifying, and for having a secure and governed environment or framework where all these agents are orchestrated in a single place.

She adds that agents running on employees’ desktops multiply the problem. At enterprise scale, it becomes hard to control what each agent is doing, or to measure and audit its activity. That is the gap Druid is aiming at, with a platform that orchestrates agents running on its own platform and on others, and an evaluation framework on top that checks whether the job actually gets done.

Following maps versus setting goals

Druid wasn’t the only Romanian company working on enterprise automation. UiPath, founded there in 2005, built its business on robotic process automation (RPA). These software robots click and type through applications the way a person would, usually to shuttle data between systems that lack good APIs. UiPath focused on the back end. Druid started on the front end, with conversations. In 2018, Druid connected its chatbots to UiPath’s robots, so that a conversation could start a long-running back-office job and hear back when it finished or hit an exception. The two partnered for several years. Today, Pleșea says, they are competitors. She explains the gap they set out to fill:

RPA performs back-office tasks when you don’t have APIs or database access. They can click through apps. As I said, if everyone had had APIs, RPA wouldn’t have been necessary. But for an enterprise, RPA was doing back-office jobs. What about their customers? The customers of the enterprise didn’t have access to the automation.

Joe Kim, Druid AI’s CEO and President, who joined in September 2025 after running Sumo Logic, argues that agents provide a different kind of integration than was previously possible with automation. He characterizes the way you write integrations as having traditionally been about work as designed, versus focusing on the intention of work as done:

The work as done, in my example, would be a salesperson going out there and doing whatever he or she can to be able to close that deal. Some of it might be following that process that I have. But ultimately, all they’re trying to do is to close that deal. I want to close the deal. I want to hit my target. I want to get my bonus. And when you’re building your AI agents, they’re also going to optimize for this as well.

Kim argues agents have to be designed for behavior and trust from the start, and monitored the whole way through, rather than mapped step by step.

Scaling ontologies with LLMs

Kim says Pleșea’s thesis planted the seeds that are easier to scale using LLMs (Large Language Models). When Druid started, conversational platforms required matching what people said to predefined intents and filling in slots. LLMs have made it far easier to interpret what people mean.

Kim clarifies it’s important to frame your use case to manage expectations and enforce judgment accordingly. Use cases for using generative AI to produce summaries for someone who knows the field might be fine if the accuracy is lower and the user has the competence to push back. But the bar is far higher when these are used to make decisions and take actions in business systems. They need to get close to 100% accuracy when they deal with customers and employees and handle personal information.

Kim argues that the reasoning can come from LLMs, but when an agent needs to commit something back into a business system, that write should go through the structured, deterministic systems an enterprise already runs:

So instead of just using LLMs, now I have all of these other pieces of technology that I already have within my environment to do those commits. I can now log all the things that are going back into my systems and control those things, really fine-tune it piece by piece, instead of ‘well, the LLM is going to kind of figure that out.’

He observes that the most popular pattern is using deterministic systems for routing rather than orchestration:

They have something in the front, and when you’re asking a question or asking it to do something, the router looks at it and says, ‘Hey, this is something that I want to use a workflow for,’ so then you use a deterministic path. Or, ‘This is okay, safety and all that stuff isn’t really that much of a concern,’ and they take a non-deterministic path.

When you do routing, you run into two problems. One is that although you’re taking a path for structured and deterministic data and a path for non-deterministic, unstructured data, you don’t get to create something on top of it that takes advantage of both of those things. From a business value perspective, it ends up becoming one or the other path. It does not become one and the other path. Problem number two is that even if you were to route it to a new model, if you’re not careful, the agents doing work on that side, because you’re not controlling them, end up having containment issues.”

My take

The first time I wrote about RPA, around 2016, I was a little surprised that the robots were essentially cutting and pasting between two terminal windows to make things go faster. It seemed that if the big enterprise vendors just made their APIs a little easier to use, nobody would need it. But then ten years on, RPA has blossomed into a thriving market, whether because vendors want to build a competitive moat or because nobody had the vision for what enterprises really needed to get the job done across systems. People are still cutting and pasting from one cloud app to another, and using RPA to do it.

Now the big RPA vendors like UiPath, Automation Anywhere and Blue Prism are all pushing into the adjacent territory of getting agents to talk to each other. Meanwhile, ERP and CRM vendors, integration players and model providers are all approaching the agentic question from their own direction, each trying to connect the dots between the multiple systems enterprises are already running.

It’s a work in progress, and everyone is competing for the agentic layer. There is probably room for many different players to approach this problem from different directions. It will be interesting to see how far this more conversational, ontology-minded approach can go.

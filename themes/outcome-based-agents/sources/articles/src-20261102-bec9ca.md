    Harper’s Critique Falls Flat: Addressing the ‘Atlantic’ Article as a Black First-Year

Awa Sangare
, 
Contributing Writer
 

November 1, 2026

 

Caroline Schouten
 

Let me start off by saying that I am a native New Yorker, born and raised, and I strangely relate to Harper. We’re both Black, come from first-generation college backgrounds, and I also can’t say that I’ve ever experienced overt racism aside from a dirty look or two. I assume this is the case for Harper, as he makes it a point to disapprove of his colleague “Ava” having a seemingly paranoid fear of white locals, as he grew up in a town similar to Lewiston.

However, my experience stems from growing up somewhat sheltered in a historically Black neighborhood, going to a majority Black school for most of my life, and the fact that I am currently listening to “
Sexy Soulaan” 
as I write this line. So naturally, the first thing to tick me off about the article is Harper’s reference to New York City as a place of “money lust and voguish pop-ups and puffy-vested financiers and pandemic chaos,” because the city that shaped my love of directionless walks, churros on the beach, and block parties is so much more than that. I believe this sentiment encapsulates the primary issue that I have with the article: Harper’s tendency to oversimplify complex issues and reduce them to personal experiences.

It feels like Harper constantly dismisses or misinterprets the feelings of his colleagues, specifically “Ava,” and completely misses his own mark on what is to blame for the tokenization and problematic practices he describes. Frankly, regardless of the controversial nature of the word “cracker,” Harper presents as an untrustworthy character in my eyes. Ava’s drastic concerns of being hated, or even killed, for simply being Black are not unfounded in this day and age, especially as a woman in 
a year when racially motivated crimes were considerably highlighted
.

It reminds me of my own family’s warnings to be prudent because that is just the reality we live in. I question why he didn’t just share his perspective, as it certainly would have made for an interesting conversation between two reasonable adults. He displays this attitude of quiet objection again when a colleague mentions avoiding a restaurant because of a student’s experience there. Instead of expressing his reproach and perhaps learning more about why his Black colleagues feel this way, he dismisses them as classist and paranoid. The worst part is that he does this while leading them to believe he’s a safe space to be given the benefit of the doubt. It feels two-faced and exploitative to disclose these private conversations.

This may have been an important conversation, but it was certainly not a productive one. If this article were to come out mid-year, let’s say April, one could argue that he’s giving students a genuine consideration to think about when choosing colleges. But this was released in August, when students are literally gearing up to move in. It feels like he just wanted to shake things up without caring who’s affected.

All this article does is leave Harper’s colleagues to the wolves and plants seeds of doubt in students of color like myself. Because now, I’m wondering who else is thinking like this? When I enter the workforce, will there be colleagues who feel I was hired because of my race? Was I hired because of my race? Logically, I know I shouldn’t be doubting myself because all of these competitive opportunities come from audacity and the ability to walk into a room confidently. Self-doubt won’t solve a hard equation, get me an internship, or raise my hand in class, but it is hard to ignore and not return to this spiral when it is highlighted. Frankly, these questions are distractions.

Harper is not here to clean up whatever he created, and that feels cowardly, because things like this break trust in the community. People stop being vulnerable because there’s people like Harper who go out and put them on blast later on. It is terrible because minorities, especially Black people, have historically been silenced and gone unbelieved. Harper himself is using race as a shield. It is so hard to shut down Harper because we have built up a society that holds these personal anecdotes with race to a certain significance and value. Who wants to be the hypocrite that discredits yet another Black man? Irony lines this story like glue, it’s ridiculous.

His reference to an incident where a white student was accused of attempting to “lynch” his roommate by calling the police on him seems…incomplete. I genuinely do not feel comfortable commenting on this because details are clearly missing. But, it is worth saying a white person’s participation in “progressive politics” does not make it impossible for them to act in a prejudiced manner. Regardless of whether the claims were true, Harper’s apparent belief that having class privilege as a Black student somehow bars you from racism and his immediate reflex to defend the white students involved is troubling, and consistent with his judgement towards his coworkers.

Now, I’m not saying that we should believe and rally behind every Black person who makes an accusation, or that Harper should have believed the allegations, but I find his reasoning for doubt to be deeply flawed and dangerous. Because, again, have you seen the state of this country? In the same way that Harper was shocked and offended by the issues that he only saw once he was hired, why would it be impossible for a “woke” white student to be covertly racist? He should know better than anyone that not everything is what it seems.

Harper’s concerning positions are extremely frustrating because they distract from the validity of the main point he makes: many elite institutions praise inclusive policies whilst harming minorities. I truly feel for him as I get further into the article. It must have been jarring to see the institution he worked for try to undermine the people who run it and for his work to be overshadowed by close-minded assumptions. The several microaggressions Harper experiences are extremely upsetting, and he is right to be angry. In particular, the “oops” and “ouch” solution that Professor “Sandra” suggests in response to Harper’s concerns about unqualified teachers teaching about race, not only trivializes the topic of race, but also puts too much attention and responsibility on students of color.

This misguided proposal not only assumes all students of color hold the same opinions about race, a flaw that Harper also points out, but it could also pit two valid expressions and experiences against one another by relying on personal validation as academic merit. Also, students of color may have an opinion about a comment, but not be able to articulate their rationale on the spot, which could discredit genuine concerns or put them in an uncomfortable spotlight that they’d rather avoid. Long story short, Harper is valid in his outrage at rampant tokenization because he’s right. It’s everywhere. It’s the disposable Black girlfriend trope that you won’t stop noticing once you pick it up. It’s using a Black model to misleadingly promote makeup products that don’t actually work on darker skin tones (looking at you YSL). It’s being placed on a MLK Day planning committee even though your work doesn’t substantially deal with race.

I’m not going to dismiss his feelings around being a “DEI hire,” because that is his experience and what right do I have to call it wrong? But I am not going to validate them either, because a lot of Harper’s evidence feels like a misdirected blame of progressive and inclusive values, rather than the paradoxically racist mechanisms elite institutions use to try to employ them. Harper ironically falls into the trap of using DEI, and the very idea of inclusion, as a scapegoat for the actions of the privileged. His disdain towards Bates’ incoming Race, Power Privilege, and Colonialism (RPPC) requirement also does not land well. Why would it be insane to take required classes on race in a country basically built on it? This proposed initiative makes sense considering Bates’s history as one of the first colleges to admit Black students. The concept of race is embedded in our society and influences everything, from the way neighborhoods are built to the food we eat.

Nobody raises eyebrows at Columbia requiring swim classes or the numerous Catholic colleges that require theology. During my first few weeks here, I’ve met people from all over the country, out the Deep South to as far west as California, as well as international students who live around the world. I seriously doubt that everyone was taught about US race relations and the lasting influences of American colonialism beyond a few pages in a textbook.

In fact, I know this is true because 
several states made laws limiting the instruction of Critical Race Theory (CRT
), the idea that racism is systemic rather than individual or isolated, in the past few years. As a first-year student who’s had to sit through at least two hours of the school’s new 
Perspective dialogues
 and pre-orientation workshops, I sincerely doubt any racist will suddenly change their mindset or that teenagers will stop making stupid decisions because of these lessons. However, the effort to move towards a more equitable and safe space is necessary. It is quite literally the school’s responsibility to take claims of racism seriously, so the idea of a racism self-audit seems completely reasonable.

My issue with Harper is not that he calls out Bates. It’s that he misdirects his anger towards progressive values and I can’t help but ask what the point of writing this was or, at the very least, why he wrote it in this way. He ironically gives himself over to conservative voices as apparent proof that schools should not have more inclusive policies, becoming a token for discriminatory causes that harm marginalized groups even more.

But don’t let my views become evidence for the “right” position. I don’t want to be the “last Black person talked to.” In an attempt to answer my own question, I suppose a good thing to come out of this article is that it prompts students to ask questions about higher education that they are often too scared to face. Yes, academia is deeply racist at times. Now, are we going to continue ignoring it or are we actually going to do something about it?

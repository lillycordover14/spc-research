Structural basis for N-glycopeptide preference in an infant-derived Bifidobacterium bifidum PNGaseA&nbsp;&nbsp;Nature

Abstract
N-glycans are abundant nutrient sources for human gut bacteria, which deploy peptide: N-glycosidases to release them from glycoproteins. Breast milk is comprised of a high proportion of N-glycosylated bioactive proteins that can be accessed by infant colonic microbes. Here, we biochemically and structurally characterize a PNGaseA-family enzyme from infant-associated 
Bifidobacterium bifidum
 LMG13195. This enzyme acts on a broad range of N-glycan structures but shows a marked preference for N-glycans attached to glycopeptides rather than to denatured or native proteins, highlighting a specialized route for N-glycan acquisition in infant-associated 
Bifidobacterium
 species. Structures of the apo enzyme, an unliganded E236Q mutant, and a D107N–E236Q complex with a G2-peptide reveal a canonical two-β-sandwich PNGase core plus an additional ten-strand β-sheet cradle and are the first structures of this superfamily. Comparison of these states, combined with molecular dynamics simulations, indicates that glycopeptide binding is coupled to β-sheet cradle-loop movements that close against β-sandwich 1 over the peptide C terminus. The complex defines recognition of the two core GlcNAc residues and the central mannose, reveals that peptide contacts are concentrated around the +2 Thr residue of the NXT sequon, and positions the scissile amide at the active site. These findings, together with mutational and computational analyses, provide a structural basis for N-glycopeptide preference by linking glycopeptide binding to cradle closure and catalysis, thereby expanding the host-glycan nutrient landscape accessible to infant-gut 
Bifidobacterium
 species.

Introduction
N-glycans are posttranslational modifications on proteins, particularly common on proteins secreted extracellularly, such as antibodies
1
. In the context of the human colonic microbiota, the prevalence of this N-glycosylation means that they are ubiquitously available for these microbes to use as a nutrient source. N-glycans are both host-produced and diet-derived (from both animal and plant sources). Furthermore, fungal yeasts produce large mannan N-glycan structures as a part of their cell wall, which are available to colonic microbes derived from yeast-fermented foods and mycobiota residing in the colon
2
.
There are now several studies characterising the enzymology of N-glycan degradation by human-associated microbes: 
Bacteroides thetaiotomicron
 and mammalian complex N-glycans
3
, 
Bacteroides thetaiotomicron
 and yeast mannan
2
, 
Streptococcus pneumoniae
 and both high-mannose and complex N-glycans
4
,
5
, 
Phocaeicola massiliensis
 and plant/insect N-glycans
6
, and 
Bifidobacterium
 species and high-mannose N-glycans
7
. There are three families of enzymes that remove the N-glycan from the peptide component and one of these is the Peptide:N-glycosidases (PNGases), which hydrolyse between the protein and the core GlcNAc
1
. The two other families are glycoside hydrolase (GH) families 18 and 85, whose members hydrolyse between the two core GlcNAc sugars to separate the glycan from the protein
1
.
Among PNGases and related glycoamidases, catalysis is not uniform, and several different mechanisms have been proposed. The cytoplasmic PNGases Png1/NGLY1, which act on misfolded glycoproteins during ER-associated degradation, belong to the transglutaminase-like superfamily and use a cysteine protease-type mechanism, with a Cys-His-Asp catalytic triad in which cysteine acts as the nucleophile
8
. By contrast, secreted PNGaseF uses an acidic-residue-based catalytic centre. Structural understanding of this class has been dominated by PNGaseF, whose crystal structures revealed a catalytic core formed by two β-sandwich domains and, in a complex with chitobiose, identified Asp60, Glu118 and Glu206 as key residues for catalysis and glycan recognition
9
,
10
. For comparison, the related glycosylasparaginases use a mechanistically distinct route in which a processed N-terminal threonine acts as both the base and the nucleophile, a hallmark of the N-terminal nucleophile (Ntn) hydrolase family
11
. This mechanism was previously argued to differ from that of PNGaseF
10
. However, because these PNGaseF studies did not capture a full N-glycopeptide substrate, it remained unclear how secreted PNGases recognize the peptide portion of the substrate. Critical aspects that remain unresolved are the position the glycosylated Asn in the catalytic site and how PNGases engage features of the canonical N-glycosylation sequon, N-X-S/T (where X is any residue except Pro). Notably, this sequon is also recognized by oligosaccharyltransferases
12
 and by soluble bacterial N-glycosyltransferases such as 
Aggregatibacter aphrophilus
 NGT
13
.
The infant colonic microbiota is dominated by species of 
Bifidobacterium
, especially in breast-fed infants, at least partly due to their ability to directly or indirectly metabolise human milk oligosaccharides as their main carbohydrate and energy source
14
. However, N-glycans are also present in human milk and some species of 
Bifidobacterium
 have characterised GH18 and GH85 enzymes that are active against N-glycoproteins
15
. The presence of these enzymes in infant-associated species of 
Bifidobacterium
 indicates that N-glycans are also a source of nutrition. One notable observation showed growth of 
B. longum
 subsp. 
infantis
 ATCC 15697 on a mixture of complex N-glycans released from whey, including both sialylated and non-sialylated structures, of which the majority were degraded during growth
16
. In terms of high-mannose N-glycan breakdown, the full enzymatic machinery from a 
Bifidobacterium longum
 strain has been biochemically characterised
17
. Finally, N-glycan fragments have been observed in the faeces of breastfed infants, and the dominant bacteria were species of 
Bifidobacterium
18
.
The ability of 
Bifidobacterium
 species to breakdown N-glycans as a nutrient source is thus evident from available literature, yet the initiating steps and their structural basis remain poorly defined. To address this, we searched genomes of infant-associated 
Bifidobacterium
 species and identified a single putative PNGaseA-family enzyme in two 
B. bifidum
 strains, including 
B. bifidum
 LMG13195 (PNGaseA
Bif
). Biochemical analyses showed that PNGaseA
Bif
 accommodates a broad range of N-glycan structures but preferentially processes N-glycans on short glycopeptides rather than on denatured or native glycoproteins. To understand the basis of this preference, we combined biochemical characterization with structures of the apo enzyme and substrate-relevant mutant states, including a complex with a G2-peptide. Structurally, PNGaseA
Bif
 retains the canonical PNGase catalytic core seen in PNGaseF, comprising two β-sandwich domains, here designated β-sandwich 1 and β-sandwich 2, but also contains an additional ten-strand β-sheet cradle. These analyses reveal that glycopeptide binding is coupled to closure of the β-sheet cradle over the peptide region of the substrate through movements in β-sheet cradle loops that approach β-sandwich 1. Molecular dynamics simulations further support that this region is intrinsically mobile in the apo state, consistent with the open-to-closed transition observed upon substrate binding. Together, these findings establish a structural basis for the preference of PNGaseA
Bif
 for N-glycopeptide substrates, with β-sheet cradle closure creating a more shielded active-site environment than the comparatively solvent-exposed catalytic cleft of PNGaseF.

The β-sheet cradle creates a C-shaped pocket and drives specificity for the substrate niche
Analysis of the area around the active site provides insights into why PNGaseA
Bif
 prefers N-glycans on a peptide. Either side of where the reducing end of the core GlcNAc would be positioned are two long pockets that together form a “C” shape (Fig. 
3
). In comparison to this, the PNGaseF structure is very open around the catalytic site, likely allowing it to accommodate N-glycans attached to larger protein components (Supplementary Fig. 
12
). An electrostatic overlay was applied to the PNGaseA
Bif
 apo structure to visualise the charge of the surface (Supplementary Fig. 
12
). Interestingly, one of the pockets is highly negatively charged and the entrance to the other is highly positively charged. We predicted that a structure with these features would support the accommodation of the charged amino- and carboxy- termini of a peptide and support the overall proposal that the function of the C-shaped pocket is to accommodate a peptide. We were able to confirm and explore the details of this through subsequent structures with ligand and simulations.

The chitobiose core and central mannose are the key glycan components recognised
Analysis of the substrate-enzyme complex reveals that recognition of the glycan is dominated by the chitobiose core and the central mannose (Fig. 
4
). The innermost GlcNAc (A
G2
) is anchored by contacts from both backbone and side-chain atoms of the enzyme. In particular, the backbone of residue Asn107 contacts the acetamide nitrogen of A
G2
, whereas the side chain of Lys230 contacts the acetamide oxygen and the side chain of Arg108 contacts OH4 of the same sugar. A
G2
 is further stabilized by a CH–π interaction with the side chain aromatic ring of Tyr106. MD simulations of the complex between PNGaseA
Bif
 and the G2-peptide, after reverting D107N and E236Q mutations, reveal that Asp107 also forms stable interactions with A
G2
 acetamide nitrogen (Supplementary Fig. 
16
). The second GlcNAc (B
G2
) is recognized mainly through the side chain of Arg108, which contacts its acetamide group, and by an additional CH–π interaction with the side chain aromatic ring of Tyr161. Interactions between B
G2
 and Arg108 are also maintained in MD simulations, while interactions with Tyr161 are substituted by Tyr106 (Supplementary Fig. 
16
). Tyr161 also packs against the central mannose (C
G2
), further stabilizing the conserved glycan core. By contrast, the more distal sugar moieties do not interact with the enzyme and are solvent-exposed (Fig. 
4
). Additionally, OH3 and OH6 groups of A
G2
 remain accessible to solvent, indicating that substitutions at these positions, including core α1,6-fucose, can likely be accommodated without major steric clash. Consistent with this, modelling of core α1,6-fucose and core α1,3-fucose, the latter a hallmark of plant N-glycans and also common in insect N-glycans
32
, places both fucose moieties in a solvent-exposed position without obvious interference with the protein (Supplementary Fig. 
17
). Together, these observations indicate that glycan recognition by PNGaseA
Bif
 is concentrated on the conserved chitobiose core and central mannose, while the surrounding region remains structurally permissive to substitution.
Recognition of the peptide is predominantly driven by the asparagine and + 2 threonine
Recognition of the peptide is concentrated around the glycosylated Asn and, especially, the +2 Thr of the NXT sequon present in the N-glycopeptide (Fig. 
4
 and Supplementary Fig. 
15
). In the captured complex, the side chain of Gln236 forms hydrogen bonds with both the side-chain amide nitrogen and side chain carbonyl oxygen of Asn4, thereby positioning the scissile amide at the catalytic centre. On the opposite side, the side chain amide of Asn107 contacts the side-chain carbonyl oxygen of Asn4, likely preserving the geometry of the catalytic pair in the mutant complex. Additional recognition involves the peptide backbone around Lys5: the side chain of Glu126 contacts the backbone carbonyl oxygen of Lys5, the side-chain amine of Lys5 forms a hydrogen bond with the backbone carbonyl oxygen of Ile291, whereas the backbone carbonyl of Gly289 contacts the backbone amide nitrogen of Lys5. The N-terminal region of the peptide is also contacted by the backbone carbonyl of Met258, which interacts with the side-chain of Lys1. The most extensive peptide recognition is centred on Thr6, the +2 residue of the sequon. Here, the side chain of Asp478 forms one hydrogen bond with the backbone amide nitrogen of Thr6 and a second with its side-chain hydroxyl group. The side chain of Arg104 also contacts the side-chain hydroxyl of Thr6. In addition, the side chain of Arg523 forms hydrogen bonds with the backbone carbonyl oxygen and the terminal carboxylate of Thr6, while the side chain hydroxyl of Tyr481 also contacts the terminal carboxylate. Tyr106 further contributes through a CH-π interaction with the Thr6 side-chain methyl group. Together, these interactions indicate that PNGaseA
Bif
 does not simply bind a glycan-bearing peptide but selectively reads the Asn and +2 Thr residues of the NXT sequon in the captured complex.

Comparison with PNGaseF highlights both the conserved and distinctive features of glycan recognition (Figs.

). In PNGaseF, the chitobiose core is recognized by Asp60, Arg61, Trp59 and Trp120, whereas the equivalent recognition platform in PNGaseA
Bif
 is formed by Asn107, Arg108, Tyr106 and Tyr161. Thus, the basic mode of chitobiose recognition is conserved between the two enzymes, even though PNGaseA
Bif
 uses tyrosines rather than tryptophans for the two principal CH–π interactions. A key difference lies near the second GlcNAc, where PNGaseF contains Glu118, which has been implicated in excluding N-glycans carrying core α1,3-fucose
31
, whereas PNGaseA
Bif
 contains Thr159. The smaller threonine side chain creates a more open environment around the OH6 of B
G2
 and the OH3/OH6 region of A
G2
, providing a structural explanation for why PNGaseA
Bif
 can accommodate core-fucosylated substrates. This is consistent with our biochemical observation that PNGaseA
Bif
 releases N-glycans from horseradish peroxidase, whose glycans predominantly carry core α1,3-fucose decorations.
Structural models generated with Protenix of PNGase
Bif‘
 in complex with G2-peptides carrying core α1,3- or α1,6-fucose are also consistent with this interpretation, as the fucose is accommodated without steric clashes in the active-site region. In both models fucose remains largely solvent-exposed, with only limited contacts, in the case of α1,3-fucose, with Lys230 (Supplementary Fig. 
17
). On the opposite side of the disaccharide, PNGaseA
Bif
 is also more open than PNGaseF. Whereas PNGaseF contains residues that pin the chitobiose core into the active site, PNGaseA
Bif
 lacks the equivalent constraining environment, further supporting its ability to accommodate differently decorated cores. As noted above, PNGaseF lacks the additional β-sheet cradle, and the superposed G2-peptide therefore remains much more solvent-exposed (Supplementary Fig. 
15
), consistent with its more open substrate-binding architecture. These structural and biochemical data show that PNGaseA
Bif
 can accommodate core α1,3- and α1,6-fucosylated N-glycans because the binding site remains open around the chitobiose core while preserving core glycan recognition.
N-Glycopeptide-induced β-sheet cradle closure in PNGaseA
Bif
 is supported by molecular dynamics simulations
Comparison of the three crystal structures of PNGaseA
Bif
 shows that the most prominent conformational differences are localized around the substrate-binding cleft. In the apo and ligand-free E236Q structures, the region between the catalytic core and the β-sheet cradle remains relatively open. In contrast, in the D107N–E236Q–G2-peptide complex, glycopeptide binding is associated with closure of the β-sheet cradle toward β-sandwich 1, generating a more enclosed binding site over the peptide portion of the substrate (Fig. 
4
 and Supplementary Fig. 
15
). This transition is driven primarily by movements in two β-sheet cradle segments, loop A (~residues 473–481) and loop B (residues 524–527), which shift toward the bound peptide and define a glycopeptide-induced cradle-closure mechanism. Furthermore, B-factors in the apo structure suggest that the cradle loops near the active site are dynamic, consistent with a binding region that can open and close to accommodate substrates of different sizes (Supplementary Fig. 
18
).
To further examine this open-to-closed transition, we performed molecular dynamics simulations on the wild-type enzyme in complex with the G2-peptide and after ligand removal. Whereas the peptide-bound enzyme remained in a more compact state, removal of the G2-peptide led to retraction of the cradle loops toward a more open conformation, consistent with the structural snapshots provided by the apo and E236Q structures (Supplementary Fig. 
19
). The closed state is further stabilized by contacts between the β-sheet cradle and β-sandwich 1 around the peptide-binding region (Supplementary Fig. 
15
). In particular, accommodation of the C-terminal portion of the peptide appears to promote additional direct interactions between these two domains. For example, the side chain of Asp478 in the cradle forms a hydrogen bond with the hydroxyl group of Tyr166 in β-sandwich 1, whereas the side chain of Arg104 in β-sandwich 1 forms a hydrogen bond with the side-chain carbonyl group of Asn479 in the β-sheet cradle. Together, the three crystallographic states, apo, ligand-free E236Q and the D107N–E236Q–G2-peptide complex, show the cradle in increasingly closed conformations, and the molecular dynamics simulations are consistent with this open-to-closed transition, supporting a glycopeptide-induced cradle-closure mechanism. Although this model rests on structural and computational evidence, direct mutation of the individual cradle contacts would provide independent validation and is a clear objective for future work.
The molecular dynamics simulations also support the peptide interactions observed in the structure and help explain how cradle closure tightens the C-shaped pocket. Interactions with the backbone carbonyl oxygen and terminal carboxylate of Thr6 are stabilized during dynamics, while interactions with its side chain are less frequently maintained (Supplementary Fig. 
16
). Thus, recognition of the +2 Thr involves both backbone and side chain features of the peptide and draws contributions from both β-sandwich 1 and the β-sheet cradle. These interactions explain why the C-terminal portion of the peptide becomes buried upon cradle closure and identify the +2 Thr as the dominant peptide recognition element in the captured complex. This is reminiscent of the importance of the +2 Ser/Thr residue in substrate recognition by oligosaccharyltransferases and soluble bacterial N-glycosyltransferases
12
,
13
. At the same time, the active-site cleft remains sufficiently open that longer peptide substrates could also be accommodated. This dynamic cradle-closing mechanism explains how glycopeptide binding encloses the peptide portion of the substrate and stabilizes the binding cleft of PNGaseA
Bif
.
AlphaFold3 models indicate that a variety of protein sequences could be accommodated in the C-shaped pocket
To test the potential for the C-shaped pocket to accommodate a variety of peptides, AlphaFold3 was used to model different peptides (Supplementary Fig. 
20

The results indicate that the negatively charged end of the pocket (formed by β-sandwich 2 loop 2) accommodates the N-terminus of the peptide and a maximum of three or four residues at the N-terminus would allow the correct positioning of an asparagine in the active site. Furthermore, the C-terminal part of the peptide was accommodated in the positively charged part of the C-shaped pocket and threaded through to the open-end of this pocket.
Two catalytic mechanisms remain plausible for the PNGase families
The substrate-bound complex also provides insight into the catalytic mechanism. The scissile Asn is positioned between residues 107 and 236, corresponding in the wild-type enzyme to Asp107 and Glu236. In the D107N–E236Q–G2-peptide complex, the OD1 atom of Asn107, which approximates the position of one Asp107 carboxylate oxygen after reversion to the wild-type residue, is 3.2 Å from the carbonyl carbon of the scissile amide. This distance places residue 107 within a range compatible with direct nucleophilic attack. Because the Asn and Gln substitutions are expected to preserve the overall hydrogen-bonding geometry of the native acidic residues, this mutant complex likely represents a reasonable approximation of the substrate-bound catalytic arrangement. Computational analysis using the Protoss server
33
, further suggests that, in the wild-type enzyme, Glu236 is protonated whereas Asp107 remains deprotonated, consistent with a division of labour in which Glu236 polarizes the scissile amide carbonyl and Asp107 carries out the key catalytic chemistry. To assess the structural stability of the substrate-bound complex and probe the participation of Asp107 in catalysis, MD simulations of 500 ns were performed on the PNGaseA
Bif
-G2-peptide complex after reverting the D107N and E236Q mutations to the wild-type residues. Backbone RMSD of the protein remained consistently stable across all replicas (Supplementary Fig. 
10a
). The distance between the Asp107 carboxylate oxygen and the carbonyl carbon of the scissile amide was monitored to test the catalytic competence of a direct nucleophilic attack (Supplementary Fig. 
10b
). Although this distance tends to increase during the early phases of the simulations, it transiently visits values of ~3–4 Å, within the expected range for this attack to occur (~3
\(\,{\text{\AA}}\)
). Consistent with this interpretation, the E236Q substitution does not abolish activity, supporting a primary role for Glu236 in substrate polarization rather than direct catalytic turnover. In the most widely accepted scenario, Asp107 would act as a general base to activate a water molecule for nucleophilic attack on the amide carbonyl. However, because no catalytic water is observed in the present structure, an alternative mechanism in which Asp107 participates more directly in catalysis cannot be excluded. Thus, the current structural and computational data support two plausible catalytic scenarios (Fig.

Discussion
The colonisation of the infant colon by species of 
Bifidobacterium
 plays a vital and holistic role in the development of a healthy gut microbiota. A full mechanistic understanding of how these microbes colonise so successfully, particularly in the presence of breast milk, has yet to be fully elucidated. One of the main factors driving successful colonisation are the human milk oligosaccharides found in breast milk, but other factors, including N-glycans, also influence this process
51
.
In this study, we characterise a PNGaseA superfamily member from 
B. bifidum
 LMG13195 and show that it preferentially acts on N-glycans attached to short peptides rather than to denatured or native glycoproteins. At the same time, PNGaseA
Bif
 accommodates a broad selection of different N-glycan structures, including high-mannose, mammalian complex, and simple plant N-glycans with core α1,3-fucose. Within the broader host-glycan utilisation network of 
B. bifidum
 LMG13195 (Fig. 
6
), PNGaseA
Bif
 therefore emerges as one component of a wider glycan-foraging strategy that extends beyond human milk oligosaccharides to O-glycans, N-glycans and glycosphingolipids. Importantly, our biochemical data show that access to N-glycans is influenced not only by glycan composition, but also by the physical state of the protein carrier. In this context, the model in Fig. 
6
 supports the idea that cradle-bearing PNGaseA enzymes are best suited to N-glycopeptides, whereas denatured or native glycoprotein substrates are more likely to be processed by other N-glycan-releasing enzyme classes. In the case of colonic-dwelling 
B. bifidum
, the protein component of a glycoprotein would likely first be degraded either by host digestive enzymes or by proteolytic members of the microbiota, thereby generating N-glycopeptides that can then serve as substrates for PNGaseA
Bif
.
The major advance of this work is that PNGaseA
Bif
 defines a structurally and functionally distinct class within the PNGaseA superfamily. Rather than acting as a generic PNGaseA that merely tolerates a broader glycan repertoire, PNGaseA
Bif
 is specialized for N-glycopeptide recognition. This specialization is encoded by the β-sheet cradle, which emerges here as the defining architectural feature of this class. The cradle is not a passive appendage to the catalytic core but an active structural element that, together with the catalytic module, forms a C-shaped binding environment which partially encloses the peptide portion of the substrate and couples binding to cradle closure. This architecture does not simply broaden substrate tolerance but provides a structural basis for selective recognition of peptide-linked N-glycans. The structure thus explains why PNGaseA
Bif
 prefers N-glycopeptides over larger glycoprotein substrates and supports the idea that cradle-bearing PNGaseA enzymes represent a dedicated N-glycopeptide-processing class.
The substrate-bound complex further shows that recognition is not driven primarily by distal glycan features, but by the conserved chitobiose core together with specific reading of the peptide context. Most notably, recognition is concentrated around the glycosylated Asn and the +2 Thr residue of the bound N-glycopeptide. Since the bound glycopeptide carries a Thr at the +2 position, our data define recognition of an NXT motif, leaving open whether Ser-containing NXS sequons are recognised in the same way. In the captured complex, the +2 Thr makes the most extensive peptide contacts, involving residues from both β-sandwich 1 and the β-sheet cradle. This indicates that PNGaseA
Bif
 does not simply recognize a glycan on a peptide, but instead reads key elements of the canonical N-glycosylation sequon itself. This suggests that the same NXS/T logic that guides N-glycan installation by oligosaccharyltransferases and soluble bacterial N-glycosyltransferases may also be exploited here during N-glycan removal, although this is based on a single NXT glycopeptide and would need additional substrates to confirm for NXS sequons. Cradle-bearing PNGaseA enzymes therefore appear adapted not only to glycopeptides in general, but specifically to N-glycopeptides carrying sequon features that can be productively engaged in the active site.
The structural data also provide a mechanistic framework for catalysis. The trapped D107N–E236Q complex places the scissile Asn between the two catalytic residues and, together with the computational analysis, supports a division of labour in which Glu236 polarizes the scissile amide carbonyl whereas Asp107 carries out the key catalytic chemistry. Two mechanistic scenarios remain plausible. In the most likely pathway, Asp107 acts as a general base to activate a water molecule for nucleophilic attack on the amide carbonyl, while Glu236 enhances electrophilicity of the scissile bond through amide polarization. However, because no catalytic water is observed in the present structure, an alternative route in which Asp107 participates more directly in catalysis cannot yet be excluded. Taken together, the available data favour a water-mediated mechanism in which Asp107 acts as the principal catalytic base and Glu236 primarily contributes by polarizing the substrate amide, while still leaving open the possibility of a more direct catalytic role for Asp107. This establishes a much more precise mechanistic framework for PNGaseA catalysis than was previously available.
Comparison with PNGaseF reinforces the idea that PNGases have evolved distinct architectural solutions for different substrate niches. PNGaseF retains the conserved core logic of chitobiose recognition but lacks the β-sheet cradle and presents a more open substrate-binding environment, consistent with processing N-glycans in larger glycoprotein contexts. By contrast, PNGaseA
Bif
 combines a conserved catalytic platform with cradle-mediated peptide recognition and active-site enclosure, thereby adapting the enzyme to N-glycopeptide substrates. These comparisons support a model in which distinct classes of N-glycan-releasing enzymes are matched to distinct substrate states, with cradle-bearing PNGaseA enzymes occupying the N-glycopeptide niche.
Finally, a glycan utilisation model for 
Bifidobacterium bifidum
 LMG13195 was constructed and provides a broader view of how this species may access host-derived glycoconjugates in the infant gut. This model underlines what is known about the glycobiology of this species and what remains to be understood. Overall, our findings indicate that N-glycan acquisition in infant-associated 
Bifidobacterium
 is governed not only by glycan composition, but also by the structural context in which the glycan is presented. Within this broader host-glycan foraging network, PNGaseA
Bif
 represents a specialized solution for N-glycopeptide processing in which the β-sheet cradle enables selective recognition, peptide enclosure and deglycosylation of peptide-linked substrates. Together, these results define a cradle-bearing class of PNGaseA enzymes that couples sequon recognition, active-site closure and catalysis, thereby expanding the nutrient landscape accessible to infant gut 
Bifidobacterium
 species.

Molecular dynamics simulations
The starting point for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations conducted in this study was the crystal structure of PNGaseA
Bif
 D107N–E236Q in complex with G2-peptide (PDB ID 29JQ). Catalytic residues mutations (D107N and E236Q) were reverted using PyMOL. Protonation states of these residues were chosen using Protoss server
33
. The carboxylic group of E236 was considered to be protonated to keep important interactions with G2-peptide. The system was inserted in an orthorhombic box with a minimum distance of 15 
\(\mathring{\rm A}\)
 between the solute and the edges (box dimensions 112.496 × 87.922 × 97.185 
\({\mathring{\rm A} }^{3}\)
). The box was solvated with TIP3P water molecules
67
 and sodium and chloride ions were added to neutralize the charge, resulting in a total of 80716 atoms of which 24169 are water molecules. Protein residues were described using Amber ff14SB force field
68
, while carbohydrates were described using GLYCAM06
69
. Both force fields have been demonstrated to accurately reproduce the structural and dynamic properties of carbohydrate acting enzymes in molecular dynamics simulations
32
,
70
. LEaP module of AmberTools
71
 was used to obtain topology and coordinate files for the MD simulations. Simulations were performed using Amber22
72
 with the CUDA version of the PMEMD module
73
. The solvated system was minimized in two steps: first the solvent was minimized with 5000 steps of steepest descent minimization and 5000 steps of conjugent gradient, with positional restraints on all atoms of the PNGaseA
Bif
 and the G2-peptide; and secondly, the entire system was minimized with 7500 steps of steepest descent and 7500 steps of conjugant gradient. Then, the system was heated to 300 K in the NVT ensemble using the Berendsen thermostat, with a time constant coupling of 1.0 ps for the coupling and 50 kcal mol
−1
 Å
−
2
 positional restraints applied to heavy atoms of PNGaseA
BIf
 and the G2-peptide. Restraints were decreased to 5 kcal mol
-1
 Å
−
2
 gradually. After that, the systems were equilibrated during 1000 ps in the NPT ensemble using the Berendsen thermostat and barostat to maintain the system at 300 K and 1 atm. Three replicas of 500 ns were run with a time step of 2 fs in the NPT ensemble with the Langevin thermostat and the Berendsen barostat. The SHAKE algorithm
74
 was used to constrain all bonds that involve hydrogen atoms. Molecular dynamics simulations for the apo structure of PNGaseA
Bif
 were performed following the same protocol after removal of the G2-peptide from PDB ID 29JQ. Trajectory analysis was performed using CPPTRAJ
75
.

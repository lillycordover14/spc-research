Forester Pass, Through the Notch — John Muir Trail Day 5

Follow Papa George

Get new posts by email. Unsubscribe anytime.

Elevation Gain:  2,274 feet

Total Elevation Gain:  12,228 feet

Pace (minutes per mile):  30:04

Am I Losing My Mind?

A truly scary thing happened in the middle of last night.  I lay in my sleeping bag and suddenly realized I couldn’t remember the name of the highest mountain in the Lower 48.  Yes, the one I had climbed myself just two days previously.  I mean, I couldn’t think of it AT ALL.

I thought, “Ummmm… Mount McKinley, that’s in Alaska.  Mount Mansfield, that’s in Vermont.  Mount…”   I came up with Mount Whittier and I thought, “Maybe that’s it, Whittier.  Is it?”  It didn’t really sound right, but I honestly couldn’t think of the name.  I groped for it, but it just would not come to me.

Finally, I fell back to sleep and then, as I woke up, it gradually came to me.  “Oh, Whitney, yeah, Whitney, yes, Mount Whitney!”  But what was going on?  Was I getting  Alzheimer’s?  As I said, it was a very scary thing.

Thinking About Forgiveness (Instead of Sleeping)

I also spent a couple of hours of my allotted sleeping time thinking about my sisters.  Sam urged that I find forgiveness.  I thought maybe I could write to them and say, “Look, I have a deep well of sadness and disappointment at how this has played out.  Yes, and anger too.”

“But there is also a well of forgiveness in me waiting to be awakened.  At some point in the future, in the near or far future, you may consider the possibility that you were not completely fair to your brother.  A little doubt may creep in about whether you honored your sibling relationship in a truly loving and caring way.  If that day ever comes, remember that well of forgiveness remains in my heart.”

Alas, the result of all that tossing and turning was that eight hours of sleep was actually four hours of real sleep.

The Trail Calls… at 4:30 a.m.

Then the alarm went off, right at 4:30.  We wanted to get up early to go over Forester Pass.  At 13,153 feet elevation, it is the highest point on the Pacific Crest Trail and the highest pass on the John Muir Trail.  We wanted to get across before the weather changed.

The tendency had been for good weather in the morning and then, by late morning or early afternoon, the clouds built up, and the weather got stormy.

When the alarm went off, I felt like I hadn’t slept much at all.  Also, I was still coughing.  So I was not moving very quickly.

Keeping Warm and Eating in the Tent

One brilliant move I made was to set up for breakfast in my sleeping bag.  For the first time on trail, it got cold during the night.  When I went out to pee, I thought it might be nearly morning and I moved my bear cannister close to my tent flap.

Back within the tent, I wondered whether I ought to insert my silk liner inside the sleeping bag for the first time.  I decided if it was nearly 4:30 I would not bother.  But the Garmin showed it was barely 12:30 – half past midnight!  Facing four more hours of attempted sleep in the cold I got out the silk liner.

Oh, my goodness, the sleeping bag liner makes such a difference!  It’s just wonderful, the difference between chilly and toasty.  It weighs almost nothing too; definitely one of my favorite hiking luxuries.

After the alarm, I had my “breakfast in bed.”  Then I lay in my sleeping bag for a while letting my digestion settle before going out to poop.  Less than ten minutes later I had assembled my tools and was facing the cathole I had so cleverly dug the night before.  All well there, I returned to my campsite and began to break it down and pack.

Breaking Camp on a Beautiful Morning

I was slow.  I don’t know why, because my pack was getting smaller with less food in it.  It should have been easier and faster.  In any case, our planned 5:30 departure turned out to be closer to 6:30, but still earlier than most of the campers in our vicinity.

We were on our way to Forester Pass.  I worried that it might actually be harder to tackle than Mount Whitney because we would cross with our full packs, not the lighter version we had carried to the summit.  As we left camp, the first thing we discovered was a sign indicating the pass was not four miles distant but five.

But it was a glorious morning, so beautiful.  Soon after our departure, the sun rose and the skies were blue; the glow shone across the mountains, across the boulder-strewn hillsides.

We cast long shadows in the early morning!

We even discovered a little early morning company!

How does Mr. Rabbit know that I am not a predator?

Meeting Other Humans on the Trail (Marty)

We ran into a guy named Marty, a carpenter from Salt Lake City.  He had decided that being a carpenter alone was not going to provide him with income in his old age.  So, he began buying dilapidated houses and fixing them up.  He then turned them into rentals.

By getting a rental income from all these properties, he became well off.  But he seemed bitter that the way to ultimately take the money out, by selling the houses, was going to cost him a great deal in taxes.  It seems most people hate taxes, but I subscribe to Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.’s view that “Taxes are the price we pay for a civilized society.”  Rather than make it about Marty, I pointed out that I thought Elon Musk should pay a hell of a lot more in taxes than he did.

Sam sensed the possibility of a political dispute.  He interjected diplomatically that it was time we kept moving.  Still, we walked with Marty for quite a long time as we gradually made our way up the valley, higher and higher, closer and closer to Forester Pass.

Meeting Other Humans on the Trail (Lauralyn)

Along the way we met another hiker, Lauralyn.  She and her husband Doug own the Mt Williamson Motel and Basecamp, a lodging in Independence, CA famous among hikers in the High Sierras.  I wondered whether Sam and I might stay there a night when we got off trail.  She encouraged us.

Lauralyn seemed really nice. She was also inspirational.  She was no longer young and skinny, but her enthusiasm for hiking the High Sierra was unabated.  Her pace may have slowed, but her vigor and sunny disposition still glowed.  I really hoped we would stay at her motel, and I volunteered to recite 
The Raven
 for the owners and guests if we stayed there.

Meeting her brought home that Sam and I were just a couple of days away from going off trail at Onion Valley.  I asked her where the best steak was in the vicinity and she told us to go to The Seasons restaurant in Lone Pine.  I was salivating already.

Salivating, yes, but coughing too.  I was still coughing so much.  Blowing my nose frequently or hawking up big globs of mucus from my lungs.  It was disgusting and demoralizing.  Since starting out, I had better days and worse days, but overall, I had not felt healthy and well at all.  This respiratory infection just felt endless.

Some History of Forester Pass

As we grew nearer, Marty pointed out a low-lying ridge that he thought was Forester Pass.  I disagreed, suggesting it might be a notch on a different ridgeline. As we got closer it became clear that it was the tiny, narrow notch.  The approach is a slow and steady uphill until the base of the mountain, when it becomes an almost vertical face.

The “notch” seen from below. If you look closely, you can see the outline of the trail passing just below the giant boulder filling the notch.

There was a stream at the base and Marty went on alone as Sam and I stopped to replenish our water.  The ascent is basically into a rock wall.  In 1930 they cut zigzag switchbacks going up the side of the cliff face.  It was a big engineering feat. At one point, a young man died in the process of making it.

Donald Downs
 was only 18 years old. He was a few weeks shy of his 19th birthday when he died, and his fellow workers created a plaque and left it there in his honor.

Photo courtesy of Lawrence Beck (since I forgot to capture it when I passed by)

In a Snap, the Mountain Weather Changes

I was amazed to have the energy to hike, especially as we began to steadily climb.  It was a little past 10:00 a.m. and our theory about “good weather in the morning” was about to take a hit.

Sam ahead of me on the trail.

It is astonishing, in the mountains, how fast the clouds can come in.  Suddenly, as we hiked upwards, we got pelted with graupel.  Those little pellets – neither snow nor ice — are not hard enough to be hail and not wind-driven enough to be sleet, not flaky enough to be snow, nor wet enough to be rain.  In a way, if we had to face precipitation, this was perfect because they hit us softly and then bounced off of us.  Neither of us really needed rain gear because it wasn’t accumulating on us.  On the other hand, it was not easing up either.

Not the best shot of it, but you can see the graupel landing and sitting on the plant’s leaves.

Finally, we reached the notch we had seen from afar and climbed the last rocky ascent to Forester Pass.  The moment was triumphant but short-lived, since the weather was bad and the thunder was thundering.

Pretty ominous, right?

Sam said, “Let’s keep going,” and we did.  On the other side of the pass was a long, long, long downhill.

Geology Lesson on the Descent

It began with switchbacks at first, all well above the tree line.  At that altitude the rocks are plainly visible and, as I frequently did on the trip, I asked Sam to explain the geology to me.  He studied the subject in college and made a career in it.  For my part, I am curious about everything and glad when I find a passionate expert who seems to welcome my questions.

We would look at a slab of granite, for example, and Sam would explain that granite is made of mica, quartz, and feldspar in varying proportions.  He got especially excited when we encountered crystal inclusions in the rock, which he termed phenocrysts.  In fact, I suggested that his trail name should be “Phenocryst,” though I am not sure it stuck.

For the First Time (that I Know of) I Annoy Sam

When the conversation turned back to my sisters, I saw Sam get visibly annoyed for the first and only time.  He was talking about the Dalai Lama’s book, 
The Wisdom of Forgiveness.  
In it, the Dalai Lama apparently discusses forgiving the Chinese for their atrocious behavior in overrunning Tibet.

I could not resist commenting that if there was any analogy with my sisters, then, seeing how little success the Tibetans have had in changing Chinese hearts and minds, maybe the lesson is that I should just give up on my sisters.  Sam clearly did not appreciate my flip remark.  He has such an even temper that I was astonished by his reaction and immediately apologized.

Snow Turns to Rain as We Descend

As we conversed and descended the graupel turned to snow for a little while and then to rain.  We passed some patches of snow left from the winter.

This was not the first snow we saw, but given our elevation I was surprised not to see more than we did.

We concluded the rain was not a passing shower, and I finally put on my rain poncho, and Sam donned his rain gear.

It wasn’t truly miserable, but it was not very pleasant hiking in the rain.  The next day we were going off trail at Onion Valley, where Sam’s rental car was parked, so we wanted to reach Vidette Meadow to ensure a moderate distance the following day.

As we hiked, we watched the weather changing in the mountains.  It is so unpredictable, because you cannot see what is just over the nearest ridge.  At times it felt like we were about to get clear skies, then it felt like the heavens were going to burst open in a thunderous flood.  In fact, it did neither, it just rained and rained as we descended.

Reaching Our Destination For the Day

Vidette Meadow was a large area with many potential campsites.  We checked out about ten of them, seeking a couple that were not too wet and soggy from the rain.  At last Sam found two fairly close together.  One was a very dicey spot right under a tree, but Sam said he wanted it.  Who was I to say no?

I took the other site which was wetter, but larger and flatter, and we set up.  It was still dripping rain when we started and then it came down a little harder while we were making camp.  So it was not terrible, but not so nice.  I was really tired.

Approving my campsite, with a little hill behind me…

Tired After a Long Day

It was a long day. Basically, I moved everything I owned into the tent. When the weather is nice, I leave a lot of my gear outside. But I thought, with the rain, let me just bring it all in.

I cold-soaked a beef stew, my last dinner before resupply.  Then I got ready for bed.  Sam was really cold the night before, when I used my liner, so I loaned him my puffy.  I was mostly using it for my pillow anyway, and I figured I could work out some alternative method for that with some odds and ends.

As I lay in my sleeping bag, I hoped the weather would be clear and dry the next morning.  I really didn’t want to pack out in the rain. We would go over Kearsarge Pass and back down to Onion Valley to pick up Sam’s car.

What Next?

Then what?  I committed to doing this blog, even if I didn’t succeed in completing the John Muir Trail.  And I had to admit, completing the trail was in doubt.  The cough and the mucus and the endless, endless, respiratory infection were getting more-and-more debilitating.

Mount Whitney and now Forester Pass are achievements, nothing to sneeze at. But I had hoped and dreamed of the John Muir trail for at least a year.  To abandon that now would really be disappointing.

We will just have to see what happens.  Tomorrow is another day.

The News:
 On November 2, 2026, Salesforce announced a definitive agreement to acquire Cresta. Salesforce positions Cresta as a voice-first operating system for customer experience, with three functions: AI voice agents that handle complex calls end to end, real-time guidance for human representatives during live calls, and analysis of every interaction.

Salesforce said Cresta’s voice agents hold natural, real-time conversations, handle interruptions, and run at enterprise scale for brands including Marriott, Comcast, and Alaska Airlines. Paired with Service Cloud, the combination is meant to tie each conversation to the customer record and to the outcome it drives, which Salesforce frames as resolution, retention, and revenue.

After close, Cresta will continue to support the contact center and telephony platforms its customers already use. The transaction is expected to close in the first quarter of fiscal 2028 (February to April 2027), subject to customary closing conditions and regulatory approvals. Salesforce did not disclose a purchase price in its communications.

Salesforce Moves to Acquire Cresta

 Salesforce is buying Cresta to give Service Cloud and Agentforce a production-grade voice layer for the contact center, the channel where Salesforce has relied most on partners.

Voice Is the Gap in Salesforce’s Service Story

Salesforce owns the service desk record, the case, and increasingly the AI agent through Agentforce, but the phone call itself has largely run on someone else’s infrastructure. Service Cloud Voice depends on partner telephony, and much of the real-time intelligence on live calls has come from specialist vendors. Cresta closes that gap with a voice stack already proven in large, high-volume contact centers.

The phone remains the channel where customers bring their hardest, most expensive problems. A chatbot that deflects password resets does little for a hotel guest with a cancelled booking or an airline passenger rerouting mid-trip. Cresta’s named customers, Marriott, Comcast, and Alaska Airlines, are exactly that profile: complex calls, high volume, and direct revenue at stake.

Cresta Covers Both Sides of the Call

Cresta brings three capabilities that most voice AI vendors sell separately: autonomous voice agents, real-time coaching for human representatives, and conversation analytics across all calls. That mix matters because few enterprises will hand every call to an AI agent in 2027. Most will run a blend, with AI resolving routine calls end to end and humans taking escalations with AI guidance at their side.

Owning both sides lets Salesforce sell a single path from assisted to autonomous service. A customer can start with agent guidance, measure results in Service Cloud, and shift call types to autonomous handling as confidence grows, without changing vendors.

The Outcome Link Is the Real Prize

Salesforce’s framing, connecting every conversation to resolution, retention, and revenue, points to the larger strategic aim. Contact center analytics has long measured handle time and sentiment in isolation. Tying call data to the CRM record, Data 360 profiles, and downstream revenue lets Salesforce argue that service calls drive measurable business outcomes, which supports outcome-based and per-conversation pricing for Agentforce.

The deal also fits Salesforce’s 2026 acquisition pattern. Informatica added data management, Loops added analytics for Tableau, and the pending Braze deal adds real-time B2C engagement. Cresta extends the same logic to voice service: buy the specialist engine, then run it on Salesforce’s data and agent layer.

Neutrality Will Be Tested

The pledge to keep supporting existing contact center and telephony platforms is necessary, because many Cresta deployments sit on contact center platforms that compete with or partner with Salesforce. Buyers should get specifics in writing: which platforms, for how long, and whether new features ship equally across them or arrive first on Service Cloud Voice.

Competitive Implications

The deal puts Salesforce in direct competition with contact center platform vendors that are also its partners, and it removes one of the larger independent voice AI specialists from the market.

During the pending period, expect contact center vendors to target Cresta’s enterprise accounts with migration offers and to press Salesforce on its neutrality commitments in joint deals.

What It Means for Buyers

Organizations running Salesforce for service should treat the deal as a reason to revisit their contact center AI roadmap now, not after close, because contract and architecture decisions made in the next six months will be harder to reverse.

Pause long new commitments to overlapping voice AI tools:
 before signing multiyear renewals for real-time assist, conversation analytics, or voice agents, ask Salesforce what Cresta will cost inside Service Cloud and Agentforce, and when it will be available.

Map where Cresta would sit in your stack:
 document which contact center platform, telephony provider, and CRM each call touches today. That map shows whether Cresta simplifies the stack or adds another layer between telephony and Salesforce.

Get the neutrality pledge in writing:
 current Cresta customers, and anyone evaluating it, should ask for contract terms covering supported third-party platforms, the support period, and feature parity across them.

Plan a staged path from assisted to autonomous calls:
 start with real-time guidance and analytics on human-handled calls, measure resolution and retention against the CRM record, then move specific call types to voice agents once results hold.

Settle data governance early:
 call recordings and transcripts will flow into Data 360 and Agentforce. Confirm consent, retention, residency, and redaction rules with legal and compliance before voice data starts feeding customer profiles.

Watch pricing, not just features:
 Salesforce’s outcome framing points toward per-conversation or outcome-based pricing. Model those costs against current per-seat contact center licenses before the first renewal conversation.

Organizations with contact centers built on NICE, Genesys, or Five9 and a CRM other than Salesforce face a different question: whether a Salesforce-owned Cresta remains a neutral choice over a three- to five-year horizon, or whether an independent voice AI vendor is the safer bet.

What to Watch:

Deal terms and regulatory review:
 the purchase price is not yet public, and Salesforce’s run of 2026 acquisitions across data, marketing, and service could draw closer antitrust attention and push the close past April 2027.

Neutrality in practice:
 watch whether Cresta’s support for third-party contact center platforms comes with a stated term and feature parity, or whether new capabilities land first on Service Cloud Voice.

Agentforce integration:
 the first proof point is Cresta voice agents running as Agentforce agents on Data 360 profiles with shared guardrails and pricing, not a separate product connected by an API.

Customer retention:
 Marriott, Comcast, Alaska Airlines, and other Cresta customers on non-Salesforce CRM or contact center stacks will show whether the platform pledge holds up at renewal.

Partner response:
 NICE, Genesys, and Five9 may deepen ties with other AI agent vendors or with Microsoft and Google to counter a Salesforce-owned voice layer.

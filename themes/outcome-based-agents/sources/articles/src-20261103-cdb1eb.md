BOISE, Idaho (CBS2) — 
The Idaho Supreme Court ruled that portions of body-camera footage from the fatal shooting of Ada County Sheriff's Deputy Tobin Bolter must be disclosed in response to a public records request.
In a unanimous decision issued Monday, Nov. 2, the justices determined the entire recording qualifies as a public record under Idaho law. However, they concluded that releasing the portion showing the immediate aftermath of Bolter's shooting would constitute an unwarranted invasion of his family's privacy.
Bolter was shot during a traffic stop near West Overland Road and South Raymond Street in Boise on April 20, 2024. He died from his injuries the following day.
The ruling stems from a public records request filed by Garth Gaylord approximately six months after the shooting. The Ada County Sheriff's Office initially denied the request, citing ongoing investigations. In the resulting court proceedings, Sheriff Matt Clifford also argued that disclosure would invade the privacy of Bolter's family and others captured in the recording.
A district court ordered the entire video released, but Clifford appealed.
The Supreme Court reviewed the approximately 13-minute recording and divided it into three sections.
The justices ruled that the first 37 seconds, showing Bolter initiating the traffic stop and approaching the vehicle before being shot, must be disclosed.
The court determined that this footage serves a significant public interest because it allows people to evaluate the official account of what happened.
However, the justices ruled that footage from 38 seconds to 8 minutes and 35 seconds may be withheld. That portion captures the immediate aftermath of the shooting, including efforts by bystanders, law enforcement officers and emergency personnel to save Bolter's life.
In its opinion, the court recognized the substantial privacy interests of Bolter's widow and daughter, concluding those interests outweigh the public's interest in seeing that portion of the recording.
The final portion, beginning at 8 minutes and 36 seconds, must also be released. By that point, Bolter had been moved away from the camera, which continued recording the emergency response and management of the scene.
The ruling also establishes important guidance for future public records disputes in Idaho.
The justices determined that footage recorded by an officer's body camera can remain a public record even after the officer is incapacitated, because the actions of responding law enforcement and emergency personnel are still matters of public business.
The court also clarified that judges must weigh legitimate and substantial privacy interests against the public's interest in disclosure when evaluating certain law enforcement records.
The justices declined to impose a civil penalty against Clifford, finding that the sheriff's refusal involved legitimate legal questions and was not made in bad faith.
The decision partially reverses the district court's order requiring release of the entire recording. The Supreme Court directed that the portions not protected by the privacy exemption be produced to Gaylord.
The ruling does not establish when those portions of the recording will become publicly available.

In the late nineteenth century, women usually cared for their hair at home, with some brushing, homemade remedies, oils and beeswax. The idea of going into a shop for a scalp treatment was often ignored or far beyond their reach.
 Then a woman named Martha Matilda Harper changed that. Harper spent about 25 years in domestic service before she opened her first salon in Rochester, New York, in 1888. She used $360 of her own savings, roughly $10,000 in todayâs money, according to UNH Paul College. Harper relied on a hair tonic formula passed down by a doctor she had once worked for. Researchers at the 
Kellogg School of Management
 at Northwestern University describe her as the founder of the franchise model for business growth and say that she built this business model to help and lift young women out of poverty and domestic service by turning them into business owners.
From domestic worker to a salon owner 
Martha Matilda Harper was born in 1857 in Ontario, Canada. She was sent out to work as a domestic worker at the age of seven. She worked in domestic service for nearly a quarter of a century, first in Ontario and then in Rochester, where she arrived in 1882. Along the way, she also worked for a doctor who taught her how a clean, well-stimulated scalp supports healthy hair. Later on, inspired by the doctorâs method, she came up with the herbal tonic recipe that she later built a business around.
First hair salon in the city 
Rochester was also a home to Susan B. Anthony and a lively circle of women activists. Harper was encouraged to strike out on her own. In 1888, she set up shop in the Powers Building in downtown Rochester, a prestigious address picked to draw the cityâs social elite and opened the first public hair care salon in the area. Her own floor-length hair worked as her advertisement. In the shop, she hired women from the same background she came from, including former servants who were trained in her methods.
 Women who visited from other cities wanted a Harper shop in their own communities and that created an issue familiar to any successful shop owner.
Harperâs early success rested on more than a good address. She had a tonic built on a doctorâs ideas about scalp care, a staff trained in her own methods, and a founder whose long hair did the advertising for her. The women she hired, many of them former servants like herself, learned the work from the person who had designed it, so the method stayed the same from one salon floor to the next. 
That consistency is what visitors from other cities noticed, and it is why they wanted a Harper shop at home.
 Demand of that kind could never be met by one owner in one building, and that left her with a decision to make.
The franchise idea
Rather than open branches and run them all herself, Harper offered ownership to women she trained. According to the 
Rochester Museum & Science Centreâs
 guide to her papers, the first franchise shop opened in Buffalo, New York, in 1891 and more than 500 Harper Method shops opened in the United States and overseas during her lifetime. 
The owners were known as Harperites. They learned a method built around cleanliness, scalp stimulation and exercise and used only Harper products.
Training did not stop after opening day. The company held reunions, continuing education sessions and the monthly magazine kept owners informed about business matters while building the sense of community among them. It is fair to call her a franchise pioneer; the model did not become a dominant way of extending a business until decades after her salons first opened.
Martha Matilda Harper, with hair reaching down near her ankles. Credits: Wikimedia Commons
A lasting legacy 
The 
National Womenâs Hall of Fame 
says hundreds of women became shop owners by buying into her network through flexible financing and thousands more trained and worked in it. Her system combined centralised management, quality control and independent ownership. The hall also credits her with employment practices that were unusual for the period, including child care, evening hours, flexible time, profit sharing and paid personal time off.
Harper mainly relied on naturally occurring substances and refused the newer chemical hair dyes and synthetic permanents. The Rochester Museum & Science Center credits her with the invention of the reclining shampoo chair and the basin with a neck cutout, though she never patented either, so she never received legal recognition for them.
Just like that, in her own community, she became the first woman member of the Rochester Chamber of Commerce and her customers reportedly included Susan B. Anthony and President Calvin Coolidge, who is said to have visited her Paris shop for scalp massages. Harper died in 1950 and the business she started carried on until 1972. A woman who had spent roughly 25 years as a house worker opened a salon in Rochester, which gained quite a success in its time and also helped many women financially.

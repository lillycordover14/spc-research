Most virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) displays present virtual objects at a fixed focal distance, creating a mismatch between where the eyes converge and where they focus. Researchers tested a varifocal display that adjusts focus to match virtual-object depth.

In a 3D pointing task, it significantly improved average movement speed and information throughput without significantly affecting error rates. However, benefits varied among users, highlighting the potential for more personalized AR display designs.

The mismatch between depth and focus

Augmented reality (AR) overlays digital objects on the real world, while virtual reality (VR) immerses users in computer-generated environments. In both technologies, interaction with virtual objects at different depths can be challenging when displays do not reproduce how human eyes focus. Looking at different distances requires the eyes to converge on an object and adjust focus together.

Most AR and VR headsets show images at a fixed focal distance, even when virtual objects appear at different depths. This mismatch, called the vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC), may affect comfort and interaction.

(c) A participant performing the 3D pointing task by manipulating the wand to select virtual targets. Credit: Assistant Professor Xiaodan Hu from SIT, Japan
            
 

Addressing this challenge, assistant professor Xiaodan Hu from the Shibaura Institute of Technology, Japan, led a collaborative international research project alongside a team of experts from Japan, China and Canada. The team members include Dr. Monica Perusquía-Hernández and Dr. Kiyoshi Kiyokawa from the Nara Institute of Science and Technology, Japan; Dr. Yan Zhang from Shanghai Jiaotong University, China; Dr. Mayra Donaji Barrera Machuca from the University of Calgary, Canada; Dr. Anil Ufuk Batmaz from Concordia University, Canada; and Dr. Wolfgang Stuerzlinger from Simon Fraser University, Canada.

The collaboration brought together expertise in hardware development and user studies from the Asian team and interaction experiment design from the Canadian team.

Testing focus adjustments in 3D pointing

The team tested whether a varifocal display, which adjusts focus to match an object's depth, could improve 3D pointing. With a stereoscopic AR prototype, participants used a tracked wand to select virtual targets of different sizes and distances. Researchers compared fixed-focal and varifocal viewing during lateral movements and movements in depth. They measured movement time, error rate and throughput, a measure of task efficiency. The first study involved 24 participants, followed by a second study with 12 additional participants to verify the hardware's performance.

                Demonstration of how the varifocal display adjusts the focal distance of virtual images. The white spheres represent virtual objects. By adjusting the focal plane to different depths, the display allows the virtual objects to be brought into focus at their corresponding distances, providing more natural accommodation cues. (a) Focal plane adjusted to a nearer distance. (b) Focal plane adjusted to a farther distance. Credit: Assistant Professor Xiaodan Hu from SIT, Japan
            
 

In the first study, varifocal viewing significantly reduced movement time and increased throughput. Error rates did not significantly change, indicating that faster performance did not come at the cost of accuracy. Benefits appeared greater for depth movements, although the difference by direction was only borderline significant for throughput.

"Our results show that adjusting focal distance can improve how people interact with virtual objects, beyond its effects on visual perception," says Hu. "But users do not all benefit equally, making individual differences an important question for future research."

Individual benefits and prototype limits

Varifocal displays could help AR and VR headsets provide more natural focusing cues when people interact with objects at different distances. However, this study did not test long-term eye health or establish that the technology prevents eyestrain. Participants' ratings showed no clear preference between display modes, and discomfort was mild.

Overall, the findings indicate that reducing VAC with varifocal technology can improve 3D pointing performance, while highlighting the need to understand why responses vary. Differences in binocular vision or depth perception may contribute, but these factors were not measured. The prototype also had a limited field of view and did not use eye tracking to adjust focus, limiting its resemblance to everyday AR use.

"Future studies can examine which user characteristics predict greater benefits and how displays might adapt to individual needs," says Hu. This work offers a basis for evaluating personalized AR systems.

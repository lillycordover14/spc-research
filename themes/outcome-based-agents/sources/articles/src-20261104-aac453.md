The neural basis of flavour–nutrient learning&nbsp;&nbsp;Nature

[page text]
Abstract
Animals learn about the external world in part through interoceptive signals
1
,
2
. For example, the nutrient content of food is first estimated in the mouth, in the form of flavour, and then it is measured again through slower signals from the gut. How these signals from the mouth and gut are integrated to drive learning is unclear. Here we identify a dopamine (DA) pathway that enables learning about the nutrient content of food. We show that DA neurons in the ventral tegmental area are necessary for associating nutrients with flavours, and that post-ingestive nutrients trigger DA release selectively in a small region of the anterior basolateral amygdala (BLA) but not in canonical DA targets in striatum. This nutrient-triggered DA release occurs preferentially in the left BLA in mice, and this same structure is preferentially activated by post-ingestive nutrients in humans. We identify the gut sensors that are responsible for nutrient-triggered DA release; show that they activate BLA-projecting DA neurons defined by expression of cholecystokinin; and demonstrate that stimulation of DA axon terminals in the anterior BLA drives flavour–nutrient learning but not other aspects of feeding behaviour. Two-photon imaging of neurons in the left anterior BLA reveals that they integrate gustatory and post-ingestive cues, and silencing these neurons prevents flavour–nutrient learning. These findings establish a neural basis for how animals learn about the nutrient content of their food.

For deciding what strength to assign a projection, large databases like the Allan Mouse Brain Connectivity Atlas (ref. 1 of the Supplementary References) were preferred, supplemented by additional sources comprising reviews and anatomical tracing studies (refs. 2–32 of the Supplementary References).

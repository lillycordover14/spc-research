The Thompson School Board heard an ADA transition plan at its study session Wednesday evening, learning about the ways the school district is falling short of compliance with the Americans With Disabilities Act and establishing a process to correct issues.

The most pressing issues carry a price tag of over $7.5 million.

The plan, created by Julee Quarve-Peterson, Inc. (JQP) and Skulski Consulting LLC incorporates findings from site visits made by JQP, Skulski and Wold Architects and Engineers to schools across the district.

JQP found 2,176 individual instances of potential noncompliance in facilities across the district; around 10% of those were priority one issues, or issues that provide significant barriers like parking or entryways. Fixing priority one issues across the district carries an estimated cost of $7.65 million.

The largest offender was Big Thompson Elementary School, the mountain school that was built over 100 years ago. Big Thompson alone is responsible for 47% of the total cost estimate to correct the top issues, said Mara Peterson of JQP. The school has major accessibility issues with its parking lot, route to the entryway, the entryway itself, and interior routes like those between floors.

“Big T, it’s a challenge,” Peterson said. “Cody (Knoblock, of Wold Architects and Engineers) came up with some great options, but options come with large dollar signs. So it’s a challenge, not an insurmountable challenge, but a challenge.”

Additional areas of concern include Ray Patterson Field, which has a challenging entry for visitors in wheelchairs, Peterson said, and parts of Bill Reed Middle School, like locker rooms and bathrooms. Accessible signage including raised braille is an issue across the district, according to the report.

Peterson did point to areas of success, including the fact that nearly all the door hardware across the district was updated to accessible door handles rather than knobs, even in older schools. The report also found 23 policy tasks, ranging from accessible job applications to the availability of listening devices, in need of attention.
 

One major change recommended by the plan was the hiring of a dedicated ADA Coordinator, according to Jennifer Skulski of Skulski Consulting. Currently, ADA compliance is fragmented across multiple departments, she said. But hiring a coordinator does not mean that compliance ceases to be a part of departments throughout the district, she added.

“What I have come to learn in the last 36 years of ADA compliance is that most successful entities are those that adopt a team approach,” she said. “You don’t just have a central ADA coordinator, you designate roles across all key responsibility units.”

The cost of correcting the priority one issues will come from existing bond funds, as well as a bond premium, or money collected from taxes above what was originally estimated, as well as the district’s general fund. The plan calls for corrections to the top priority issues within the next five years.

Board members were grateful for the report, and recognized the difficulty of the task ahead.
 

“It was, I felt, very thorough, in the brick and mortar part and in the policy part,” board member Alexandra Lessem said of the plan. “I’m very glad we got here, even if it took us a very long time to do so.”

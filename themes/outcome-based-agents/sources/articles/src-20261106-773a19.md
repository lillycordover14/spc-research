BILLINGS. Mont. — As Veterans Day approaches, the City of Billings is recognizing an organization working to support veterans throughout Montana and beyond.
Mayor Mike Nelson has proclaimed November 6, 2026, as Adaptive Performance Center Day, recognizing the work of the Adaptive Performance Center (APC) and its efforts to help veterans feel valued, supported and connected.
The organization has locations in Billings and Helena, but its reach extends well beyond those two communities. Its co-founders, Mitch Crouse and Karen Pearson, say they’ve built relationships with veterans across Montana and the country including people who first connected with APC in Montana but have since moved away.
At the center of APC’s work is what Pearson calls “upstream suicide prevention.” Rather than waiting until someone reaches a crisis, the organization works to meet veterans where they are and help with the challenges they may be facing in everyday life.
“We meet veterans where they are every day with whatever need they may have,” Pearson said. “They just need somebody to talk to. We’re here for them. If they need somebody to help them physically train, we’re here for them. If they need housing, if they’re struggling with food, they’re struggling getting medical care. We meet them where they are and we intervene before it becomes a crisis.”
That support can look different from one veteran to another. It may mean having someone to talk to, getting connected with resources or finding opportunities to stay physically active. The goal is to make sure veterans have somewhere to turn and don’t feel like they have to navigate those challenges alone.
The need for connection is also something Crouse says extends beyond state and national borders.
He shared the example of a veteran in the Netherlands who has been in contact with APC for several weeks.
“Karen was dealing with a gentleman in the Netherlands the other day. We’ve been dealing with him for about three weeks now. So, APC reaches everywhere,” Crouse said.
For the co-founders, those relationships are about more than providing a service. They’re about making sure veterans know they still have a place where they belong, even after their military service ends or life takes them somewhere new.
Pearson says veterans should never return home feeling isolated or as though they no longer have a place to turn.
“At no point should they ever have to come home and feel like they don’t belong and feel like they don’t have a place to go and be and feel safe,” Pearson said. “We can be there for them and we can provide a place for them where they feel safe, secure, loved, wanted, and that they matter.”
Crouse encourages people who know a veteran who may need support to help connect them with APC.
“Don’t gloss over it. If you see a veteran, you know, a veteran, send them to us and it’s a safe place for them,” he said.
The city’s proclamation comes as APC prepares to share more information about its work through a study involving the University of Arizona.
Following staff training with Give an Hour, the organization has been part of research examining its work. The study is scheduled to be publicly released on Veterans Day, November 11. APC is also participating in a national media effort surrounding the research.
For Crouse and Pearson, the recognition from the city is meaningful, but they say supporting veterans shouldn’t be limited to one day or one week each year. Their goal is to continue making sure veterans know they have somewhere to turn and that they matter long after their service.

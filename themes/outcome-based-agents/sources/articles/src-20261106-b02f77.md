Adonis Georgiadis: How they "ordered" 150 Porsches with his email and how to protect yourself

By
 
Digital Fortress

November 6, 2026, 10:07

On his birthday, Health Minister 
Adonis Georgiadis
 reported to X that unknown individuals had “hacked” his email and, using his name, “ordered” 150 Porsche Cayennes, 50 Mercedes Maybachs, 24 Benelli shotguns and a Springfield Armory Echelon pistol. He put the word “hacked” in quotation marks, and the screenshots he posted show why: what appears to be his account is not necessarily a breach. We explain how it could have happened, how one can distinguish the misuse of an address from a real hack, and what protection measures a minister needs.
What did Adonis Georgiadis denounce?
“On my birthday, they gave me the following surprise,” the minister wrote in his post, as reported by 
Documento
. He added that he thanked the perpetrators “for the original way” of wishing him well, but that “I have just contacted the Cybercrime Prosecution Service and will take legal action.” 
Dnews
 notes that the service is now called the Cybercrime Prosecution Service. The messages, according to 
Proto Thema
, read: “Hello, I am Adonis Georgiadis, MINISTER OF HEALTH, I AM MAKING THIS PURCHASE FOR ME AND MY WIFE.” As 
SKAI
, the “miswritten order” appears to have been responded to by the agencies themselves.
Image source: X / @AdonisGeorgiadi
What do the snapshots show?
If one reads the images of the post carefully, these are not messages sent by the minister’s account. These are 
automated responses
 he received. The first one reads “Thank you for your interest in Porsche. Your question has been sent to Porsche Center Athens” and is followed by the section “Your message to Porsche Center Athens”. The second one, for the Mercedes-Benz S-Class Maybach, begins with “Dear Adonis Georgiadis”, quotes “Your message: Hello, I am Adonis Georgiadis, MINISTER OF HEALTH…” and ends with the phrase “This is an automated email”.
This is the format of confirmations from website contact forms: you fill in your name, email, and message, click "Send," and the company automatically sends you a "thank you" with a copy of your text. For such a message to reach the minister's inbox, it is enough for someone to write their address in the form. They don't need to be logged into their account.

Scenario 1: Form abuse, no hack
The technique is known as form bombing, a variation of subscription bombing. As 
CloudSEK
, contact and quote request forms send automated “thank you for contacting” replies to any address entered, without any proof that the owner requested it. The Swiss National Cyber ​​Security Center (
NCSC
) notes that because the messages come from real companies with properly configured servers (SPF and DKIM), spam filters consider them harmless and let them through.
In the case of Adonis Georgiadis, the address is not secret: two of his email addresses appear on the 
MP's page on the Parliament website
. The number of "orders" (150 and 50 cars) and the tone of the text are more reminiscent of a hoax or targeted harassment than a financial scam. This is not the first time that his name has been used in this way. Previously, as Minister of Development, he had complained, according to 
Liberal
, that "some fraudster" sent emails to real estate agencies that were falsely attributed to him.

Scenario 2: Actual account breach
However, it cannot be ruled out from the images that a real breach also occurred. The most common methods are an email or SMS phishing that steals the password, a password that has been used elsewhere and has been leaked, an infostealer, i.e. malicious software that steals passwords and sessions from the browser, or a SIM swap, with which the perpetrator obtains the mobile number and SMS passwords. If something like this had happened, there would be traces: messages in the "Sent" that he did not send himself, forwarding rules or filters that he did not create, connections from unknown devices or locations in the account's activity history. These will be checked by the Cybercrime Prosecution. From what has been published so far, none of these emerge.

What measures should the minister have taken?
Against form abuse, the owner of the address can do almost nothing: no settings in the account prevent someone from writing their address on a foreign website. But they can limit the damage. The first practice for any public figure is separation: a public contact address, managed by the office and receiving the "noise", and another, not published anywhere, for personal accounts, banking and password recovery. Thus, a wave of automated messages never hides a real security alert.
To prevent the account itself from being compromised, the US 
CISA
 recommends that "high-risk individuals", i.e. high-ranking government and political executives, use FIDO authentication, ideally with physical security keys or alternatively with passkeys. It asks that SMS passwords be disabled, a password manager be used
and
a PIN be entered into the mobile phone account, so that SIM swapping is not easy. If the account is in Gmail, 
Google's Advanced Protection Program
 requires connection only with a security key or passkey, so that, as Google writes, someone who knows the username and password cannot connect without the key. Google explicitly recommends this for those involved in elections and political campaigns.

The responsibility of companies with forms
A large part of the problem is solved on the website side. The Swiss 
NCSC
 recommends that administrators protect all forms, including contact forms, with CAPTCHAs, limit requests per IP address and time period, and require a CAPTCHA confirmation before any automated email is sent. The 
Maskbreak guide
 also suggests that the automated response to a contact form should have a fixed text or not be sent at all. If the delegations did not copy the text typed by the stranger in the response, the messages would not look like an “order” from the minister.
What do you do if it happens to you?
If your inbox is suddenly flooded with unsolicited confirmations, don’t delete them all at once. The 
NCSC
 explains that the aim is often to disguise a genuine message, such as a transaction notification, and recommends looking for words like “code,” “payment,” “order” or “security,” checking your bank and cards directly and, if necessary, changing your passwords. Don’t reply to the messages. If your name has been used to defraud others, file a complaint with the Cybercrime Enforcement Directorate, as the minister did.

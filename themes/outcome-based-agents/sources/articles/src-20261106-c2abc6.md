Key Takeaways
Thread AI's Angela McNea argues full AI automation fails in production because decision automation only works in constrained feature spaces, so enterprises must build horizontal governance, identity and logging infrastructure and iterate workflows to accumulate learning data.
Summary
Angela McNea, CEO and co-founder of Thread AI, argues that most attempts to automate decisions with AI fail once the problem space stops being tightly constrained, and that the durable path is "controlled autonomy" — agents acting inside governed systems with humans injectable at any point. The conversation spends most of its time on why enterprises keep rebuilding the same infrastructure, how routing and decision gates are really business-logic problems, and what high-stakes failure looks like in finance and defense; the title's "full automation fails" theme is the closing argument, not the through-line.

McNea previously spent six years at Palantir, four of them alongside co-founder and CTO Maya, and writes the Handshake Protocol newsletter on human-AI collaboration. The briefing below covers Thread AI's product thesis, its defense work with Army DEVCOM, the "every good decision is an ensemble" argument, and her view that workflow design — not model selection — is the hard part.

The real bottleneck is operability, not model building

McNea says the constraint has shifted from training models to deploying them, and enterprises respond by assembling and reassembling stacks from scratch. A real enterprise runs tens to hundreds of software systems, multiple identity providers, heterogeneous data, and layered risk and regulatory rules. Thread AI's pitch is to supply the missing primitives — isolation, metering, identity, logging, governance — so teams focus on the workflow instead of rebuilding plumbing.

She describes two failure patterns she observed before founding the company:

Pattern
What companies did
What broke
Vertical tool sprawl
Bought point tools for invoice processing, procurement automation, marketing automation, reconciliation
Vendor management "goes pretty wild"; successful builds in one business unit could not move elsewhere
Everyone-is-a-builder
Non-engineers built large capability maps and assembled stacks
Operability collapsed when a coding provider changed, a team turned over, or the system needed load testing

Thread AI governs the whole transaction rather than individual actions, and its platform is built on "controlled autonomy": agents reason and act inside limits the customer sets, humans are injected either when an agent escalates or by policy, and everything is captured in a record. McNea and Maya left Palantir in spring 2023 and raised a Series A in 2025. She frames the founding thesis as a bet that "the glue, the harness, the control, the governance" would matter — and says the compounding of frontier, sovereign, and local models has only reinforced it.

Routing is easy; defining "hard" and "expensive" is the barrier

In her essay "every good decision is an ensemble," McNea argues that routing debates focus on the wrong layer. The router technology itself, she says, is less interesting than the application around it: someone has to predefine what counts as a hard problem, what counts as expensive (time, people, money), and what the system is optimizing for — all before any routing happens. Thread AI lets customers build routers natively, but she says the team spent most of its effort on those explicit decision and logic gates because "in the real world, those were the actual barriers to putting something in production."

She extends the ensemble idea beyond model routing to institutions, citing how the judicial system distributes decisions rather than vesting them in one actor. The practical implication for buyers: model-selection tooling is commoditizing, while the codified business logic around it is where differentiation and risk both live.

Workflow design, not model choice, is the hard part

McNea's sharpest example is a model deciding who can join a clinical trial. A classification model outputs yes or no, and evaluation surfaces accuracy, precision, and recall — but a business person and the scientists must decide which error is worse: admitting someone who is a poor fit and may be harmed by the drugs, or excluding someone who might have benefited. "It's never like yes or no. It's an investment someone makes in the logic as they're building these flows," she says.

The same logic applies to a triage workflow: building it is tractable, but deciding who receives alerts and at what volume — whether they are too noisy or not important enough — is the challenging part. She argues existing enterprise processes were designed deliberately, with legal, regulatory, and people factors baked in, and that injecting AI does not remove those factors; it forces a redesign. She says enterprises are spending a meaningful share of their IP on exactly that redesign, while individual productivity tools remain "wild west."

"The construction of the system, the construction of the process, is often the intellectual work."

She also links this to a shift in the developer role at large institutions: engineering is moving toward managing, reviewing, and designing rather than implementing, and she sees a rough parallel emerging for knowledge workers and domain experts.

Stakes range from billions in losses to life and death

McNea says being a dual-use company mattered to her and Maya from the start, and calls the US Department of Defense the most demanding customer she can imagine. Thread AI has publicly discussed research with Army DEVCOM focused on manual deconfliction — getting accurate information to the right person at the right time, both for scientists maintaining and innovating control systems and for warfighters in the field. She describes the stakes there as life or death.

On the commercial side, she gives reconciliation flows at a large financial institution as the canonical example: an agent error there can cost billions of dollars and carries legal and reputational exposure. Thread AI sells across financial services, manufacturing, hospitality, and advertising, and McNea says the same primitives serve both the defense and enterprise workloads.

Best practices were modernized, not discarded

Asked what is hardest to automate, McNea points to the design layer rather than the technology. She credits her and Maya's "superpower" as building primitives that have adapted to four years of technological change, and says they were explicit from day one about not throwing away 20 to 50 years of distributed-systems and security best practice — instead modernizing it for adaptability.

The platform is self-service, with an applied arm that works alongside customers to share best practices and build with them. McNea reads the surge in enterprise AI spend partly as consulting dollars — companies paying outsiders to assemble stacks — which she takes as a clear signal of a gap a platform should fill. On the product roadmap, she flags hiring around public-sector work and upcoming building blocks for companies that sell products and want them more adaptable, agentic, and AI-forward.

The founding idea came from a Meta paper, and the method is scientific plus human

McNea traces Thread AI's origin to Meta's Toolformer paper, in which a language model's last layers are trained to call simple APIs like a calculator or weather service. As a software engineer, she saw the mismatch: enterprise APIs are thousands of providers, each with thousands of endpoints and hundreds of parameters, across different protocols, authentication schemes, and data types. But the idea that flexible decision-making could live in software was compelling, and working backward from a best-case future produced an infrastructure company.

Her problem-solving lens comes from two climate-scientist parents and a Columbia degree in computer science and math — deconstruct the problem, establish a base case, hypothesis-test. She pairs that with what she calls the central lesson of her forward-deployed engineer years at Palantir: founders need "a balance of confidence and conviction and humility," because the customer is the expert in their domain, not you.

McNea is not in the AGI camp, and expects iteration not instant automation

In the closing exchange, McNea says plainly she does not count herself in the AGI camp, though she believes in building and using intelligent systems. Her evidence is empirical: automated decision-making across the stack has mostly been unsuccessful except where the feature space is tightly constrained — a finite set of paths and options, such as routing to a person or trying one of a few actions. "When you open up the aperture, I've seen things struggle still."

She attributes part of that to expectations rather than capability. AI workflows are iterative, and the death of innovation is the inability to get started. Her prescription is to ship the building blocks early, capture the human-AI exchange, and build a data asset the system can learn from and evolve on — rather than assuming every decision can be automated from day one.

"I'm not in the AGI camp."

The unresolved tension across the episode is between McNea's skepticism of broad automation and Thread AI's commercial bet on agents operating in high-stakes settings. Her answer is governance and human injection points, but she does not quantify how much human involvement her customers actually retain once a workflow matures — a question worth pressing as the public-sector work scales. Also worth tracking: whether the consulting spend she cites as a platform opportunity converts into self-service adoption, or persists as services revenue that keeps enterprises dependent on outside assemblers.

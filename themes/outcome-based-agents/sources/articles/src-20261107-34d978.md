How Bryce Carter started an adaptive field at Gravel Burn&nbsp;&nbsp;Escape Collective

[page text]
Wade Wallace

7th Nov 2026

Wade Wallace

On the descent into the finish of one of Gravel Burn’s longest stages, Bryce Carter's head unit reads 75 km/h. A year ago, on the same road, he saw 80. He's sitting only a few inches off the ground on a 40-plus kg, three-wheeled, e-assisted 
Bowhead RX
, arms out wide, and he's passing people.
The motor cuts out at 32 km/h, like most e-bikes, and everything above that is gravity, line choice and shifting his weight. The 23-year-old South African grew up racing BMX and enduro. "Once you've ridden enough bikes, you know the fundamentals," he told me midweek in the Hyperice recovery tent, flushing out his arms while I did my legs. 
Bryce and I recovering (and interviewing) after a hard stage.
The descents were the easy part. Most of this year's Gravel Burn wasn't. Juanita Mackenzie, the friend who rode every kilometre beside him as his companion, told me that at the end of day one she wasn't sure they'd see the finish.
"I really didn't think on day one that we would make it," she said.
One door shuts, another one opens
Bryce's father, Matt, had a way of explaining life to Bryce long before his accident. Picture the warehouse of bedroom doors in 
Monsters, Inc.
, he'd say. Every door is an opportunity, and each one you walk through leads somewhere with more doors.
At 20, on a family outing to a BMX track near Stellenbosch, Bryce went over the bars and landed on his head,
 
dislocating his spine at T11 and T12
. He knew straight away. Lying there, he told me, it felt like every door had slammed shut at once. "It was just a field with nothing in front of me." He knew straight away what had happened and how the rest of his life would change.
The first door reopened about four weeks into his hospital stay. A friend, a motocross rider who'd had a similar accident, showed him a video of Bowhead riders 
bombing down San Francisco's streets and stairs
. "Going down stairs is not something that's easy on a regular bicycle," Bryce said. "And these guys were flying."
Re-imagining the gravel racing experience at Gravel Burn
Why Kevin Vermaak believes "scalable intimacy" is the future of Gravel Burn.
Escape Collective
Wade Wallace
The next door opening came as a phone call. Race founder Kevin Vermaak asked Matt whether, if Bryce got a Bowhead, he thought his son could finish Gravel Burn. Matt said yes. Bryce collected his Bowhead at the Sea Otter Classic in California, and on his first day riding it, a little ambitious on an off-camber section of mountain bike trail, he leaned the wrong way and tipped it.

Key Takeaways:

The essay treats “Mizrahi” as a modern Israeli administrative category that conceals a two-millennia-old constellation of Jewish communities across Babylonia, Persia, Yemen, Egypt and the Maghreb, deeply embedded in Islamicate society yet retaining distinct law, liturgy and institutions under the dhimma system.

Drawing on the Cairo Geniza, rabbinic centers in Baghdad and Córdoba, Judeo-Arabic literature and regional case studies, it shows a civilization of commercial mobility and intellectual synthesis that was neither an interfaith utopia nor continuous persecution, and that was transformed by the 1492 Sephardic influx and nineteenth-century Europeanization.

The mid-twentieth-century exodus under decolonization, Arab nationalism and the Arab-Israeli conflict largely ended this presence, generating the “Mizrahi” label itself and subsequent political, literary and scholarly efforts to reclaim an Arab-Jewish past that remains contested in Israeli and regional memory.

The term “Mizrahi” (Hebrew: “Eastern”), used to designate Jews whose ancestral communities lay in the Middle East and North Africa rather than in Christian Europe, is a retrospective, largely Israeli-administrative category that conceals a far older and more heterogeneous civilizational reality (Shohat, 2001). Long before its coinage, Jewish populations scattered across Babylonia, Persia, Yemen, the Levant, Egypt, and the Maghreb constituted not a single ethnos but a constellation of communities bound less by common origin than by a shared immersion in the linguistic, legal, and devotional idioms of the Islamicate Mediterranean and its eastern hinterland. To speak of a Mizrahi civilization in the Mediterranean is therefore to speak of a family of overlapping cultural formations — Judeo-Arabic, Judeo-Persian, Judeo-Berber — that took shape within, and were never fully separable from, the wider Arab-Islamic ecumene (Stillman, 1979).

This essay approaches that civilization neither as a footnote to European Jewish history nor as a simple prelude to the state of Israel, but as a subject in its own right: a two-thousand-year continuum of settlement, commerce, scholarship, and coexistence whose twentieth-century rupture has obscured, without erasing, its salient civilizational aspects. What follows traces that continuum from its late-antique origins through the Islamicate golden ages, the post-1492 Sephardic convergence, the colonial-era transformations of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the mid-century exodus, and the contemporary struggle over memory and historiographical framing — pausing along the way on the regional constellations, linguistic architecture, and communal institutions that gave this civilization its distinctive texture.

The analytical stakes of this undertaking extend beyond antiquarian reconstruction. How this civilization is narrated bears directly on contemporary political argument: on debates over the historical legitimacy of Jewish claims to Middle Eastern belonging, on the politics of compensation and property advanced by organizations representing Jewish refugees from Arab lands, on the framing of Israeli-Arab normalization agreements as a “return” to some prior condition of coexistence, and on the place Mizrahi communities occupy within Israeli society itself. A historically rigorous account cannot resolve these contemporary arguments, nor should it be expected to; but it can supply the empirical texture — the specific institutions, languages, regional variations, and documented ruptures — without which such arguments too easily collapse into competing myths of the kind this essay will later discuss explicitly.

Terminology and the Problem of a Single Civilization

Any account of Mizrahi civilization must begin by unsettling its own vocabulary. “Mizrahi” is not an emic term inherited from the communities it describes; it is a twentieth-century Israeli sociological category, coined to aggregate Jews from Iraq, Yemen, Syria, Egypt, Iran, and the Maghreb into a single administrative and, eventually, political bloc defined chiefly by its difference from Ashkenazi Jews of European origin (Shohat, 2001). Before this aggregation, a Baghdadi Jew, a Fassi Jew, and a Sanaani Jew would not necessarily have recognized one another as members of the same cultural category; each was embedded in a distinct local Islamicate society, with its own dialect, liturgical custom, and communal leadership. The scholarly literature further complicates the picture by distinguishing “Mizrahi” from “Sephardi” in the strict sense — the latter denoting descendants of the Jews expelled from Iberia in 1492 — even though, as will be discussed below, the two strata merged so thoroughly across North Africa and the Ottoman lands that the distinction is often more analytical than lived (Lehmann & Marglin, 2020).

Matthias Lehmann and Jessica Marglin’s edited volume, Jews and the Mediterranean, is instructive on this point: it warns against what its contributors call the “new Mediterranean Studies,” a scholarly tendency to celebrate fluidity, hybridity, and cosmopolitanism across the sea’s shores while paying insufficient attention to what actually made Jewish communities distinctive within that connective space (Lehmann & Marglin, 2020). Their caution is salutary: a civilizational account of Mizrahi Jewry must hold together two claims that are easily allowed to cancel each other out — that these communities were deeply, structurally embedded in Arab-Islamic society, and that they nonetheless constituted specific, bounded, self-reproducing communities with their own law, liturgy, and collective memory. The civilization under discussion here is, in other words, neither simply “Jewish” in a generic diasporic sense nor simply “Mediterranean” in an undifferentiated connective sense, but a particular historical alloy of the two.

Antique and Late-Antique Substrata

The deepest layer of this civilization predates Islam by a millennium or more. Communities in Babylonia, on the Persian plateau, in Yemen, and along the North African littoral trace their presence to the exilic and diasporic movements of the first millennium BCE, well before the Arab conquests gave the region its later Islamic character (Stillman, 1979). The Babylonian exile of the sixth century BCE seeded what would become, over the following thousand years, the single most consequential Jewish community outside the Land of Israel; by the time of the Arab conquest of Mesopotamia in the seventh century CE, Babylonian Jewry already possessed the institutional scaffolding — academies, a lay exilarchate, an extensive legal literature — that Islamic rule would inherit rather than create.

Persia’s Jewish communities, similarly, long antedate Islam, with settlement traditions reaching back to the Achaemenid period and a continuous presence through the Parthian and Sasanian empires; it was this Persian-Babylonian matrix, not the Roman Mediterranean, that produced the Babylonian Talmud, the foundational legal text of rabbinic Judaism (Stillman, 1979). Yemen’s Jewish community, geographically isolated at the southern tip of the Arabian Peninsula, developed an equally ancient and largely self-contained tradition, with its own liturgical rite, pronunciation of Hebrew, and manuscript culture that would remain comparatively insulated from Mediterranean currents until the mid-twentieth century. Egypt’s Jewish presence stretches back further still, to the Ptolemaic and Roman periods and the great Hellenistic-Jewish center of Alexandria, while smaller but continuous communities persisted along the North African coast from Cyrenaica to the Maghreb under Roman, Vandal, and Byzantine rule, absorbing over time elements of Berber culture that would later fuse with Arabic and Islamicate influences.

That the central achievement of rabbinic civilization — the Babylonian Talmud — was produced not in the Land of Israel but in a Mesopotamian diaspora is of more than antiquarian interest: it establishes that Mizrahi civilization is, from its inception, a civilization of the eastern Mediterranean and Near Eastern crossroads rather than a derivative offshoot of a Palestinian center. When Islam arrived in the seventh century, it did not confront a diasporic void to be filled but a mosaic of already ancient, institutionally mature Jewish societies stretching from the Atlantic shore of Morocco to the Iranian plateau.

The Islamicate Political and Legal Order:
 
Dhimma
 
and Communal Autonomy

The consolidation of Mizrahi civilization as a distinct historical formation is inseparable from the political order established by the Islamic conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries. Under the 
dhimma
 — the covenant of protection that Islamic law extended to Jews and Christians as “People of the Book” — non-Muslim communities were guaranteed physical security, freedom of worship, and internal legal autonomy in exchange for payment of the jizya poll tax and acceptance of a subordinate social status marked by sumptuary and other restrictions (Stillman, 1979). This arrangement, whatever its structural inequalities, produced conditions of security, mobility, and cultural integration that were, for most of the medieval period, considerably more favorable than those available to Jews in contemporary Latin Christendom, where expulsion, blood libel, and periodic massacre were recurrent features of communal life.

Dhimmi
 status was not static: its practical severity varied enormously by dynasty, century, and locality, oscillating between periods of remarkable openness — as under the early Abbasids or the Muslim rulers of al-Andalus — and periods of harsh enforcement, sporadic persecution, and forced conversion, as under the Almohads in twelfth-century Iberia and the Maghreb (Stillman, 1979). What remained constant across this variation was the institutional logic of the arrangement: Jewish communities retained their own courts, applied their own religious law to internal civil matters, elected or confirmed their own communal leadership, and managed their own charitable, educational, and burial institutions, all under the ultimate suzerainty of the Islamic state. This structure of communal autonomy nested within an overarching Islamicate polity is the political precondition for everything else discussed in this essay: the economic networks, the intellectual efflorescence, and the linguistic hybridity that together define Mizrahi civilization all presuppose a legal order that made room — however unequal — for a permanent, self-governing Jewish presence within Dar al-Islam.

Historiographical Debate: Neither Utopia nor Persecution

The account offered so far of dhimma-era coexistence risks, if left unqualified, sliding into precisely the romanticized narrative that the most rigorous historiography of the field has worked to dismantle. Mark R. Cohen’s comparative study of Jewish life under medieval Islam and medieval Christendom identifies two competing and, in his analysis, equally distorting myths that have long structured popular and even scholarly understanding of this history: on one side, the notion of a medieval Andalusian and Islamicate “interfaith utopia” of untroubled convivencia, cultivated especially by nineteenth-century Jewish historians writing in reaction to their own experience of European antisemitism; and on the other, what Cohen terms the “neo-lachrymose conception of Jewish-Arab history,” a retrospective projection of twentieth-century Arab-Israeli hostility backward onto the medieval period, casting Islamicate rule as scarcely less persecutory than Latin Christendom’s.

Cohen’s own comparative method — examining theological assumptions, juridical structures, economic conditions, and the social psychology of majority-minority relations across both civilizations — concludes that neither myth withstands scrutiny. Medieval Islamic society did subordinate and periodically persecute its Jewish population, and the 
dhimma
 was, in its legal essence, a codified inequality rather than an egalitarian pluralism; yet the structural position of Jews within Islamicate society, Cohen argues, generated markedly less of the theologically driven, socially totalizing hostility that produced recurrent expulsion, forced conversion, and mass violence in medieval and early modern Christian Europe. This is a claim about relative frequency and intensity of persecution, not about its absence, and it is precisely this calibrated, comparative judgment — avoiding both nostalgic idealization and anachronistic retrojection — that the present essay has sought to honor in its own account of Islamicate symbiosis, communal autonomy, and periodic episodes of harsh persecution such as the Almohad conquests (Cohen, 1994/2008).

This historiographical caution matters directly for how the civilization’s twentieth-century rupture, discussed later in this essay, ought to be understood. If the classical and medieval Mizrahi Mediterranean was neither a lost paradise of interfaith harmony nor a site of continuous persecution comparable to the European Jewish experience, then neither the nostalgic communal memory that some Mizrahi families retain of pre-exodus life, nor the retrospective narrative of unbroken Arab antisemitism sometimes advanced to justify or explain that exodus, can be taken as historically self-evident. Both are, in Cohen’s sense, mythic constructions serving present-day political and psychological needs, and any adequate civilizational account — including this one — must remain alert to the difference between the documented complexity of the historical record and the simplifying narratives, of whichever political valence, that have grown up around it.

The Geniza World: Commerce, Mobility, and the Mediterranean Economy

No single body of evidence illuminates the texture of this civilization more vividly than the Cairo Geniza, the storeroom of the Ben Ezra synagogue in Fustat in which, for reasons of religious scruple against destroying documents bearing the name of God, discarded letters, contracts, court records, and even shopping lists accumulated for nearly a millennium. S. D. Goitein devoted the final three decades of his career to reconstructing, from this “marvelous treasure trove of manuscripts,” a six-volume portrait of what he called “a Mediterranean society”: the Jewish communities of the Arab world as they actually lived, traded, married, and worshiped between roughly the tenth and thirteenth centuries (Goitein, 1967–1993).

The economic world disclosed by the Geniza documents is one of extraordinary mobility and interconnection. Jewish merchants moved flax, grain, spices, textiles, and precious metals between Sicily, Ifriqiya, Egypt, the Levant, and as far east as India, financing their ventures through commenda partnerships and informal credit networks that spanned the length of the Islamicate Mediterranean and beyond (Goitein, 1967–1993). Correspondence preserved in the Geniza shows merchants in Fustat corresponding with agents in Qayrawan, Palermo, and Aden with a fluency and regularity that presupposes not merely commercial opportunity but a shared legal culture — rooted equally in Islamic commercial law and rabbinic responsa — capable of enforcing contracts and adjudicating disputes across vast distances without recourse to any single sovereign authority. This was, in Goitein’s phrase, a genuinely Mediterranean society: one whose Jewish participants were not marginal to the region’s economic life but among its principal intermediaries, moving as easily between Islamicate ports as between the Jewish communities that dotted them.

The Geniza documents also disclose the sheer geographic reach of individual careers and family networks within this system: a single merchant house might have branches or correspondents simultaneously active in Palermo, Tunis, Alexandria, and Aden, coordinating shipments of Indian pepper, Yemeni indigo, and Sicilian silk through a web of familial trust and rabbinic-court enforceability that functioned, in effect, as a private international law for a community with no state of its own. Goitein’s meticulous prosopographical reconstruction of hundreds of such individuals and families — their marriages, bankruptcies, lawsuits, and pious bequests — remains, more than half a century after its publication, the single richest documentary foundation for any claim made about the social and economic history of this civilization’s classical period (Goitein, 1967–1993).

Beyond commerce, the Geniza record documents the institutional density of Jewish communal life under Islam: synagogue endowments, charitable foundations for the ransom of captives and the support of the poor, scribal and educational institutions, and an elaborate apparatus of communal taxation and record-keeping. It is this combination — commercial cosmopolitanism resting on a foundation of dense local institutions — that Goitein’s work identifies as the hallmark of the classical Mizrahi Mediterranean: a civilization of intermediaries rather than of insulated enclaves, in which mobility across the sea and rootedness within the local qahal (community) were not in tension but mutually reinforcing.

Communal Institutions: Exilarchate, Geonate, and Rabbinic Authority

Underlying the commercial cosmopolitanism of the Geniza world was a distinctive structure of religious and political authority inherited from the late-antique Babylonian community and adapted across the Islamicate world. The exilarchate — a hereditary lay leadership claiming Davidic descent and recognized by the caliphate as the official representative of the Jewish community — exercised nominal political authority, while the geonim, heads of the great rabbinic academies of Sura and Pumbedita in Iraq, exercised the more consequential religious and legal authority, issuing responsa (halakhic rulings) that were dispatched by courier and caravan to Jewish communities as distant as Qayrawan and Córdoba (Stillman, 1979). This system effectively made Baghdad, for several centuries, the juridical capital of world Jewry, a role it played not despite but because of its position within the Abbasid caliphate’s own imperial and scholarly networks.

As Islamicate political unity fragmented after the tenth century into competing caliphates and regional dynasties — the Fatimids in Cairo, the Umayyads and later successor states in al-Andalus, the Zirids and Almohads in the Maghreb — rabbinic authority regionalized in parallel, giving rise to local geonates and chief rabbinates in Qayrawan, Fustat, and elsewhere that increasingly rivaled Baghdad’s primacy. This pattern, in which religious and communal authority tracked the political geography of the Islamicate world rather than any autonomous Jewish territorial logic, is itself a salient aspect of Mizrahi civilization: its institutional history is legible only against the backdrop of the caliphates, sultanates, and emirates within which it was nested, a dependency that distinguishes it sharply from later Ashkenazi communal structures organized within the very different political fragmentation of medieval Latin Christendom.

At the local level, this authority was exercised through the qahal or jama’a, the self-governing communal council responsible for allocating tax obligations among community members, supervising kosher slaughter and other ritual functions, administering charitable endowments for orphans, widows, and the ransom of captives, and adjudicating civil disputes through rabbinic courts whose jurisdiction Islamic authorities generally respected so long as Muslims were not party to the case. This nested structure of authority — caliph or sultan, chief rabbi or nagid, local qahal, individual household — gave Mizrahi communal life a resilience that allowed it to survive successive changes of dynasty and even outright conquest with its essential institutions intact, a resilience visible in the fact that many of the communal structures documented in the Geniza persisted, in recognizable form, into the Ottoman and even the colonial period some seven or eight centuries later.

The Golden Ages: Baghdad, Córdoba, Kairouan, and Fez

Out of this matrix of communal autonomy, commercial mobility, and rabbinic institution-building emerged the intellectual efflorescence conventionally associated with the Islamicate “golden ages.” In ninth- and tenth-century Baghdad, Jewish scholars participated in the same translation movement and philosophical culture that produced the flowering of Arabic science and philosophy under the Abbasids; the Gaon Saadia ben Yosef, writing in Judeo-Arabic, produced both a philosophical defense of rabbinic Judaism against Karaite and rationalist challenges and a foundational Arabic translation and commentary on the Hebrew Bible, exemplifying a scholarly practice indistinguishable in method from that of his Muslim philosophical contemporaries (Stillman, 1979).

In al-Andalus, this pattern reached its most celebrated expression. Jewish courtiers, physicians, grammarians, and poets — Hasdai ibn Shaprut, Samuel ha-Nagid, Solomon ibn Gabirol, Judah Halevi, and, in the twelfth century, Moses Maimonides — moved fluidly between service to Muslim rulers, participation in Arabic philosophical and scientific discourse, and the composition of Hebrew liturgical poetry that borrowed Arabic metrical forms and courtly conventions wholesale. Maimonides himself, born in Córdoba and eventually settling in Fustat under Ayyubid rule, wrote his single most influential philosophical work, the Guide of the Perplexed, in Judeo-Arabic; his career — physician to Saladin’s vizier, head of the Fustat Jewish community, author of both the Arabic Guide and the Hebrew legal code Mishneh Torah — is arguably the single clearest embodiment of the civilizational synthesis this essay describes.

Beyond Iberia, Kairouan in Ifriqiya developed its own vigorous school of Talmudic scholarship and medicine in the tenth and eleventh centuries, producing figures such as Isaac Israeli, whose medical and philosophical treatises circulated in both Arabic and Latin translation across the medieval Mediterranean. Fez, further west, hosted its own tradition of rabbinic scholarship associated with figures such as Isaac Alfasi, whose halakhic compendium, composed in the eleventh century, would remain a foundational reference for Jewish law across the Mizrahi and, later, Sephardi world for centuries afterward. Taken together, these centers — Baghdad, Córdoba, Kairouan, Fez — constitute not isolated peaks of Jewish achievement but nodes within a single Arabic-language intellectual ecumene, in which Jewish philosophers, physicians, and poets worked within the same disciplinary vocabulary, the same literary conventions, and often the same royal or vizieral patronage networks as their Muslim counterparts.

Judeo-Arabic and the Linguistic Architecture of Mizrahi Civilization

No single feature captures the specificity of this civilization better than language. Judeo-Arabic — Arabic written in Hebrew script and inflected by Hebrew and Aramaic lexical strata — served for nearly a millennium as the primary vehicle of Jewish philosophical, legal, exegetical, and everyday communication across the Islamic world, from Baghdad to the Maghreb (Alcalay, 1993). It was in Judeo-Arabic that Saadia Gaon translated the Bible, that Maimonides composed the Guide of the Perplexed and his early legal commentaries, and that ordinary merchants, as the Geniza correspondence attests, wrote to their business partners, their wives, and their rabbis about matters as mundane as shipping delays and as intimate as marital disputes.

Ammiel Alcalay has argued that the West’s later insistence on a rigid opposition between “Jew” and “Arab” performs a kind of retrospective violence on this linguistic and literary reality, in which a native, place-bound Levantine and Mediterranean sensibility — rather than the myth of the perpetually wandering exile — was the lived norm for centuries (Alcalay, 1993). In Alcalay’s reading, the very existence of a rich medieval and early modern corpus of Judeo-Arabic philosophy, poetry, and biblical exegesis by writers such as Yehuda al-Harizi, Sa’adia Gaon, and later figures working in Ottoman lands demonstrates that Arabic was never, for these communities, a merely instrumental or borrowed tongue but a native medium of Jewish religious and intellectual expression in its own right.

This linguistic continuum extended well beyond literary Judeo-Arabic into a family of regional vernaculars: Judeo-Berber among Amazigh-speaking Jewish communities of the Atlas mountains and pre-Saharan oases; Judeo-Persian, attested from as early as the eighth century and eventually producing its own body of poetry and biblical paraphrase; and distinct dialects of spoken Judeo-Arabic in Baghdad, Cairo, Tripoli, Tunis, and Fez, each marked by phonological and lexical features that identified a speaker’s communal origin as precisely as any surname. This linguistic hybridity — the routine, unselfconscious inhabitation of Arabic, Berber, and Persian vernaculars alongside Hebrew liturgical and literary registers — is among the most salient aspects of Mizrahi civilization: a civilization organized around multilingual fluency and cultural intermediation rather than around the linguistic and territorial segregation that would later come to define the modern nation-state’s approach to ethnic and religious minorities.

Family, Gender, and Everyday Life in the Geniza Record

Beyond its testimony to trade and scholarship, the Geniza corpus offers an unusually intimate window onto family structure, gender relations, and daily life within the medieval Mizrahi Mediterranean. Marriage contracts (ketubbot), wills, divorce documents, and personal correspondence preserved in the Geniza reveal a legal culture in which women, while subordinate in formal status to men, nonetheless possessed enforceable property rights, could initiate divorce proceedings under specified conditions, and appear repeatedly as independent economic actors — lending money, managing family businesses during a husband’s absence on trade voyages, and appearing before rabbinic courts to defend their interests (Goitein, 1967–1993).

Goitein’s reconstruction of “the family” and “daily life” from this material — the subjects of the third and fourth volumes of A Mediterranean Society — depicts households whose material culture, culinary practice, and social ritual were often indistinguishable from those of their Muslim neighbors, differentiated chiefly by dietary law, life-cycle ritual, and religious calendar rather than by any wholesale civilizational separation (Goitein, 1967–1993). Long-distance trade imposed frequent, sometimes years-long separations between husbands and wives, generating a substantial correspondence of longing, reproach, and negotiation that humanizes the commercial history discussed above and underscores a recurring theme of this civilization: the constant negotiation between mobility and domesticity, between a cosmopolitan public life conducted largely in Arabic and a more insulated domestic and liturgical life conducted in Hebrew and Aramaic.

The 1492 Convergence: Sephardim and the Reconfiguration of the Mizrahi Mediterranean

The expulsion of Jews from Spain and, shortly afterward, Portugal at the close of the fifteenth century layered a second, properly Sephardi stratum onto these older Eastern and North African communities, permanently reconfiguring the demographic and cultural map of the Mizrahi Mediterranean. Sephardic refugees, expelled in successive waves between 1492 and the early sixteenth century, resettled overwhelmingly within the Ottoman Empire and its North African periphery — in Salonika, Istanbul, Izmir, Sarajevo, Tunis, Tripoli, and Fez — where they frequently merged with, and were in some cases gradually absorbed into, the indigenous Mizrahi populations they encountered (Lehmann & Marglin, 2020).

The pattern of convergence was uneven. In some cities, notably Salonika and Istanbul, Sephardic immigrants arrived in such overwhelming numbers, and with such organizational and economic advantages, that they effectively supplanted the pre-existing Romaniote (Greek-speaking) Jewish communities, imposing Judeo-Spanish (Ladino) as the new communal vernacular. In North Africa, by contrast, the outcome more often ran the other way: Sephardic newcomers settling in Fez, Tetouan, or Tripoli frequently assimilated into the numerically dominant indigenous Mizrahi and Judeo-Berber communities, retaining only a residue of Sephardic liturgical custom, distinctive family names, and a self-identification as megorashim (“the expelled”) as against the toshavim (“the indigenous”) — a distinction that in some Moroccan cities persisted, at least nominally, into the twentieth century.

Lehmann and Marglin’s volume cautions against collapsing this complexity into a facile narrative of Mediterranean fluidity: the specificity of the Jewish experience — its legal disabilities, its communal institutions, its distinct demographic rhythms — must be held alongside, not dissolved into, the connectivity of the wider Mediterranean world (Lehmann & Marglin, 2020). The result, by the early modern period, was less a single Mizrahi bloc than a mosaic: Baghdadi, Aleppan, Cairene, Yemenite, Tripolitanian, and Moroccan communities each retained distinct liturgical customs, dialects, and social structures while sharing a common Islamicate matrix, now further enriched — and in places substantially reshaped — by the Sephardic influx from the Iberian Peninsula.

Regional Constellations I: Babylon and Iraq into the Modern Era

Of all the regional communities that composed this civilization, none preserved a more continuous sense of its own antiquity than the Jews of Iraq, direct heirs of the Babylonian exile and the geonic academies. Into the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Baghdadi Jewry remained one of the most economically and culturally prominent Jewish communities of the Middle East, deeply integrated into the commercial life of Ottoman and, later, monarchical Iraq, with substantial trading networks extending to India, Southeast Asia, and East Asia through Baghdadi Jewish merchant families based in Bombay, Calcutta, Rangoon, Hong Kong, and Shanghai.

This position of relative prominence and integration makes the community’s mid-twentieth-century unraveling especially dramatic. The Farhud, a violent pogrom that struck Baghdad’s Jewish quarter in 1941 amid the collapse of a pro-Axis coup, marked an early rupture in what had been, until then, a relatively stable communal existence; the subsequent rise of Arab nationalism, the 1948 Arab-Israeli war, and Iraqi government policies culminating in the 1950–51 denaturalization and mass airlift of the community (“Operation Ezra and Nehemiah”) brought the more than two-millennia-old Jewish presence in Iraq to an effective end within less than a decade. It is precisely this community — heir to the geonic tradition, architect of the Babylonian Talmud, prominent in Ottoman and Iraqi commercial life — whose rapid twentieth-century dissolution supplies the paradigmatic case for the rupture discussed later in this essay.

Regional Constellations II: Egypt — Cosmopolitan Alexandria and Cairo

Egypt’s Jewish community offers a related but distinct case, marked less by continuous antiquity of institutional leadership than by successive layers of settlement and an unusually cosmopolitan communal composition. Joel Beinin’s study of the community after 1948 describes an indigenous population of Arabic-speaking Rabbanite and Karaite Jews, some tracing their presence to the early Islamic era, joined in the mid-nineteenth century by Sephardic immigrants drawn by the economic opportunities of Suez Canal construction and the cotton boom, and later by Ashkenazi Jews fleeing persecution in Eastern Europe — producing, in Beinin’s phrase, “a heterogeneous community of cosmopolitan hybrids” that was simultaneously a source of communal vitality and, ultimately, a factor in the community’s fragility (Beinin, 1998).

That fragility became decisive in the wake of the 1948 war and, still more sharply, the 1956 Suez/Sinai crisis, when a substantial portion of Egypt’s Jewish population — regardless of Zionist sympathy or lack thereof — was expelled, denaturalized, or otherwise compelled to leave, dispersing to Israel, France, and the United States. Beinin’s account is notable for insisting that this experience cannot be adequately narrated by either Egyptian nationalist or Zionist historiography alone: the Egyptian Jewish story, in his analysis, is one of multiple, overlapping, and often conflicting identities — Egyptian, Jewish, Arabic-speaking, cosmopolitan, sometimes communist, sometimes Zionist — whose dispersion produced not a single diaspora but several, each preserving a different, contested memory of what Jewish life in Egypt had actually been (Beinin, 1998).

Regional Constellations III: Yemen — Isolation, Piety, and Rupture

Yemen’s Jewish community stands at something of a civilizational pole from cosmopolitan Alexandria or mercantile Baghdad: geographically remote, largely excluded from the commercial networks that connected the Geniza world, and subject to a particularly restrictive legal regime under the Zaydi imamate, including, in its most severe expression, the eighteenth-century-derived “Orphans’ Decree” mandating the conversion of Jewish orphans to Islam, a practice reinforced into the twentieth century. Isolation of this kind produced, paradoxically, one of the most religiously conservative and textually meticulous Jewish communities of the Mizrahi world, renowned among scholars for the accuracy of its Hebrew biblical manuscripts and the antiquity of its liturgical pronunciation.

The community’s modern rupture came with dramatic suddenness. Between December 1948 and September 1950, in an operation popularly known as “Operation Magic Carpet,” the newly established state of Israel, in cooperation with British colonial authorities in Aden and the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, airlifted approximately 49,000 Yemenite Jews to Israel. Esther Meir-Glitzenstein’s revisionist account of this operation complicates its popular characterization as an unambiguous rescue, documenting serious mismanagement in the transit camps established at Aden, where hundreds of refugees died of hunger and disease while awaiting transport, and arguing that the operation’s later commemoration as a miraculous “magic carpet” functioned in part to obscure the human cost of an improvised and poorly coordinated undertaking (Meir-Glitzenstein, 2014). Her account exemplifies a broader pattern that recurs across the Mizrahi exodus: the layering of a redemptive national narrative over a more ambiguous and often traumatic historical reality.

Regional Constellations: Iran and the Judeo-Persian World

Beyond the Arabic-speaking core of this civilization lay a parallel, equally ancient Judeo-Persian world whose relationship to the wider Mizrahi Mediterranean was one of family resemblance rather than linguistic identity. Persian Jewry’s roots, as noted above, reach back to the Achaemenid period, and its communities — concentrated historically in Isfahan, Shiraz, Hamadan, Mashhad, and Tehran — developed under successive Persian and, from the seventh century, Islamic dynasties a religious and communal life structured by the same broad logic of dhimmi status that governed Jewish communities across the Arabic-speaking lands, even as everyday and literary language diverged along Persian rather than Arabic lines (Stillman, 1979).

Judeo-Persian, attested in manuscript form from as early as the eighth century, produced its own substantial literary tradition, including biblical paraphrase, epic poetry modeled on Firdawsi’s Shahnameh, and a body of mystical and devotional verse that drew as readily on Persian Sufi imagery as on rabbinic sources. This literary hybridity, structurally analogous to the Judeo-Arabic phenomenon described above, underscores a broader point about the civilization under discussion: its linguistic architecture was never confined to Arabic alone but extended, through a shared logic of vernacular absorption and Hebrew-script adaptation, to every major language of the Islamicate world with which Jewish communities came into sustained contact. Iranian Jewry’s own twentieth-century trajectory — relative prosperity and increasing integration under the Pahlavi monarchy, followed by mass emigration after the 1979 Islamic Revolution to Israel, the United States, particularly Los Angeles, and Europe — reproduces, on a compressed timescale and for partly distinct political reasons, the broader pattern of rupture traced elsewhere in this essay.

Karaites and Rabbanites: Internal Religious Diversity

No account of this civilization’s salient aspects would be complete without acknowledging that “Mizrahi” Jewish life was never religiously monolithic even at the level of core doctrine and legal authority. The Karaite movement, which emerged in eighth-century Iraq and rejected the authority of the Oral Law codified in the Talmud in favor of direct scriptural interpretation, established substantial and enduring communities across the Islamicate world, with particularly significant populations in Jerusalem, Cairo, and later Istanbul and Crimea. The Cairo Geniza itself, though associated with a Rabbanite synagogue, preserves extensive evidence of Karaite-Rabbanite intermarriage, commercial partnership, and theological polemic, indicating a relationship considerably more entangled than a simple sectarian divide would suggest (Goitein, 1967–1993).

Saadia Gaon’s own philosophical and exegetical corpus, discussed above chiefly for its contribution to Judeo-Arabic letters, was in substantial part a polemical response to Karaite challenges, illustrating how internal religious controversy — no less than external Islamicate influence — shaped the intellectual output of this civilization’s formative centuries. Egypt’s modern Karaite community, numbering several thousand at its twentieth-century peak and concentrated in Cairo, shared fully in the mid-century rupture described above, dispersing chiefly to Israel, where Karaites continue to maintain distinct communal and religious institutions to the present day. This persistent internal diversity — Rabbanite and Karaite, to say nothing of the Samaritan community that maintained its own separate tradition in Palestine and Egypt throughout this period — is itself a salient civilizational aspect: the Mizrahi Mediterranean was never a single religious bloc but a field of competing textual authorities and communal boundaries negotiated, for the most part, within a shared Arabic or Judeo-Arabic linguistic and legal idiom.

Music, Liturgy, and Material Culture

The civilizational synthesis traced throughout this essay is nowhere more audible than in liturgical and secular music. The Andalusian classical tradition known variously as ala in Morocco, gharnati in Algeria, and ma’luf in Tunisia and Libya, though cultivated by both Muslim and Jewish musicians, was disproportionately preserved and transmitted across the modern period by Jewish orchestras, cantors, and instrumentalists, several of whose recordings from the early and mid-twentieth century remain foundational to the modern scholarly reconstruction of this repertoire. In the Judeo-Spanish and Sephardic-influenced communities of the eastern Mediterranean, the romancero tradition of Ladino ballads preserved, in sung form, a body of medieval Iberian narrative material that had otherwise disappeared from the Spanish-speaking world itself.

Synagogue liturgy across this civilization likewise developed distinct regional traditions of cantillation and piyyut (liturgical poetry) composition, often incorporating Arabic maqam modal structures directly into Hebrew prayer melodies — a practice so thoroughgoing in some communities that congregants could identify the specific liturgical occasion being marked simply from the maqam employed. Material culture followed a parallel logic of shared regional idiom overlaid with communal specificity: domestic architecture, textile and jewelry design, and above all cuisine — the North African Sabbath stew known as dafina or skhina, itself a regional cousin of the Sephardic hamin and the Iraqi Jewish tebit — demonstrate a culinary continuity with surrounding Muslim populations modified chiefly by the constraints of Jewish dietary law and the particular rhythm of the Sabbath and festival calendar. This dense material and expressive culture, largely destroyed as a living communal practice by the mid-twentieth-century dispersion, has become in the decades since an object of active ethnomusicological and culinary-historical recovery, paralleling the literary and political reclamation discussed above.

Contemporary Diplomacy and Heritage Politics

The civilization discussed throughout this essay has, in the twenty-first century, acquired a further and distinctly contemporary dimension: its incorporation into the diplomatic and cultural politics of Israeli-Arab normalization. Morocco’s December 2020 agreement to normalize relations with Israel, concluded under the framework popularly associated with the broader Abraham Accords, was explicitly framed by Moroccan and Israeli officials alike in terms of the two countries’ shared Judeo-Moroccan heritage, with the restoration of synagogues, Jewish cemeteries, and heritage sites across cities such as Fez, Essaouira, and Marrakech serving as both genuine acts of preservation and instruments of public diplomacy.

This heritage politics operates on both sides of the Mediterranean: in Israel, Mizrahi cultural and culinary revival — the mainstreaming of previously marginalized Iraqi, Moroccan, and Yemenite musical, culinary, and liturgical traditions within Israeli popular and even elite culture — has proceeded alongside continued political mobilization of the kind Chetrit documents, while in Morocco, Tunisia, and, to a lesser extent, Egypt, state-sponsored heritage tourism and museum development, including institutions such as Casablanca’s Museum of Moroccan Judaism, the only dedicated Jewish museum in the Arab world, have positioned Jewish history as an asset of national cultural diplomacy rather than a suppressed or merely tolerated chapter of the past. Whether this contemporary heritage politics constitutes a genuine civilizational continuity with the Islamicate synthesis described earlier in this essay, or merely a curated and instrumentalized memory of it severed from any living community, remains a live question within the Mizrahi Studies scholarship discussed above — and one that this essay does not presume to resolve, so much as to note as among the most salient contemporary aspects of a civilization whose classical and medieval expressions are considerably easier to characterize with confidence.

Regional Constellations IV: Syria, Lebanon, and the Levantine Cities

The Jewish communities of Aleppo, Damascus, and Beirut occupied yet another position within this civilizational mosaic: neither as isolated as Yemen nor as cosmopolitan in composition as Alexandria, but deeply embedded in the overland and Mediterranean trade routes that connected Ottoman Syria to Iraq, Anatolia, and Egypt. Aleppo in particular developed a distinctive liturgical and musical tradition, the pizmonim repertoire, and safeguarded for centuries the Aleppo Codex, one of the most authoritative medieval manuscripts of the Hebrew Bible — a fitting symbol of a community that saw itself as custodian of a textual tradition reaching back to the geonic period even as it remained thoroughly Arabic-speaking and locally rooted in Syrian urban life.

As with Iraq and Egypt, the mid-twentieth century brought rapid dissolution: the 1947 partition vote and subsequent Arab-Israeli war triggered riots and restrictive legislation in Aleppo and Damascus, and by the 1990s the once-substantial Syrian Jewish community had all but disappeared from the country, its members dispersed chiefly to Israel, the United States — notably to a large and cohesive Syrian Jewish community in Brooklyn — and Latin America. The Levantine case thus reproduces, in miniature, the broader pattern this essay traces: a centuries-old, thoroughly Arabized Jewish urban culture, deeply integrated into the surrounding society’s commercial and linguistic life, dissolved within a single generation by the combined pressures of regional war, nationalist politics, and Zionist mobilization.

Lebanon presents a partial exception worth noting for its very anomaly. Beirut’s small but prosperous Jewish community, concentrated in the Wadi Abu Jamil quarter, initially benefited from the country’s relative political stability and confessional power-sharing system, and unlike Baghdad, Cairo, or Damascus did not experience mass expulsion in the immediate aftermath of 1948; emigration instead proceeded gradually, accelerating sharply only with the outbreak of the Lebanese civil war in 1975, after which the community effectively ceased to exist as an organized presence. That even Lebanon’s comparatively gradual attrition ended in the same near-total disappearance observed elsewhere in the Mashriq underscores how thoroughly the region-wide combination of nationalist consolidation and recurrent Arab-Israeli conflict foreclosed the possibility of continued Jewish communal life across the Levant, irrespective of a given country’s particular political trajectory.

Colonial-Era Transformations: The Alliance Israélite Universelle and Modernization

The nineteenth-century encounter with European colonialism introduced a further, transformative layer into this civilization’s history, distinct from both the medieval Islamicate synthesis and the twentieth-century rupture. The Alliance Israélite Universelle, a Paris-based Jewish educational and philanthropic organization founded in 1860, established a network of French-language schools across Morocco, Tunisia, Algeria, Egypt, Syria, Iraq, and Iran, explicitly aimed at “regenerating” what its French-Jewish founders regarded as a backward, oriental Jewry through European-style education, French language instruction, and vocational training.

The Alliance schools produced, over two or three generations, a new stratum of Francophone- or Anglophone-educated Mizrahi Jews increasingly oriented toward European culture, commerce, and, in some cases, emigration — a shift that measurably widened the cultural distance between educated urban elites and more traditionally observant rural or working-class Jewish populations within the same countries. This colonial-era Europeanization did not simply supersede the older Islamicate civilizational matrix; it was layered onto it, producing communities — particularly in Morocco, Tunisia, and Egypt — whose members might move fluidly between Judeo-Arabic domestic speech, French professional and educational life, and Hebrew liturgical practice, a trilingual or quadrilingual competence that further complicates any simple narrative of Mizrahi civilization as either purely “Islamicate” or purely “traditional.” It also, not incidentally, positioned a portion of these communities to emigrate toward France and other European destinations once the mid-century ruptures began, in parallel with the larger movement toward Israel.

The direct colonial administration that followed in Morocco, Tunisia, and Algeria after 1830, 1881, and 1912 respectively introduced a further complication, since French rule extended differential legal treatment to Jewish and Muslim colonial subjects. Algeria’s 1870 Crémieux Decree, which granted French citizenship en bloc to Algerian Jews while leaving the Muslim majority under a distinct and inferior indigénat legal status, is the starkest instance: it inserted a formal juridical break between Jewish and Muslim populations that had previously shared, whatever their religious and social distinctions, a common status as colonized subjects, and it left a lasting mark on Muslim-Jewish relations in Algeria that would resurface, with considerable political charge, during the Algerian war of independence and the community’s near-total departure at independence in 1962. Morocco and Tunisia, retained as protectorates rather than annexed outright, preserved a greater degree of indigenous legal and monarchical authority over Jewish communal affairs, a difference in colonial architecture that helps account for the comparatively more gradual, and more contested, character of Moroccan Jewish emigration discussed later in this essay.

Rupture: Decolonization, Arab Nationalism, and the Making of an Exodus

The consolidated civilizational world described above — antique, Islamicate, linguistically hybrid, regionally diverse, and by the twentieth century partially Europeanized — underwent a rupture of extraordinary speed and scale between roughly 1948 and the early 1970s. The convergence of three distinct historical processes — the end of European colonial rule across North Africa and the Middle East, the rise of Arab nationalism as the dominant regional ideology, and the Arab-Israeli conflict inaugurated by the 1947–49 war — combined to make continued Jewish residence in Iraq, Yemen, Syria, Egypt, Libya, and, more gradually, Morocco, Tunisia, and Algeria, increasingly untenable.

The resulting population movement was immense in both scale and speed: within roughly a quarter-century, communities that had maintained a continuous presence across the region for periods ranging from several centuries to well over two millennia were reduced, in most cases, to remnants numbering in the hundreds or low thousands, with the overwhelming majority relocating to Israel and smaller but significant populations settling in France, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Latin America. The causes of departure varied by country and period — outright expulsion and denaturalization in Iraq and Egypt, communal panic following violence in Libya and Aden, more gradual and voluntary emigration in Morocco and Tunisia — but the cumulative effect was the same: the effective termination, within a single generation, of a Jewish presence across the Arab world that had defined the region’s religious and cultural landscape since before the rise of Islam.

It bears emphasis that this rupture was experienced, and continues to be remembered, in sharply divergent ways. Israeli and much Western historiography has tended to frame the episode as an exodus or expulsion analogous to the far better-documented Palestinian displacement of 1948, emphasizing persecution, discrimination, and forced departure; some Arab nationalist historiography has minimized or denied the coercive elements of the departures, emphasizing instead Zionist organizational efforts to encourage emigration; and a smaller body of scholarship, including much of the work discussed in the following sections, has sought to hold both dimensions together — real, mounting insecurity and discrimination on one hand, and active recruitment, sometimes involving controversial tactics, by Zionist emissaries on the other — without collapsing the episode into either a purely nationalist or a purely Zionist narrative.

The Invention of “Mizrahi”: Zionism, Categorization, and the Arab Jew

It is against this backdrop of rupture that the very category organizing this essay took shape. As the displaced communities described above arrived in Israel between the late 1940s and the 1960s, they encountered an Ashkenazi-dominated Zionist establishment that read their Arab and Islamicate cultural formation — their language, dress, music, and family structure — as a mark of civilizational backwardness rather than of Mediterranean sophistication, and that required, for ideological and administrative purposes, a single aggregate category capable of encompassing Iraqi, Yemenite, Moroccan, and Syrian newcomers alike. “Mizrahi” emerged from precisely this administrative and ideological need.

Ella Shohat’s influential critique holds that Zionist historiography required the invention of “Mizrahi” as an administrative category precisely in order to sever these communities from their Arab civilizational context, reframing what had been a millennium of native Levantine and North African belonging as a story of exile and redemptive “return” (Shohat, 2001). In Shohat’s account, the resulting identity is one of enforced rupture: Jews who were, in the fullest cultural sense, Arab, were compelled to adopt a national identity that disowned that heritage even as it absorbed their communities into the demographic project of the new state (Shohat, 2001). The concept of the “Arab Jew,” which Shohat and later scholars have deliberately revived as a term of self-description, is meant precisely to name the identity that the Mizrahi category was constructed to dissolve: a subject for whom “Arab” and “Jew” were, for the greater part of two millennia, not opposites but a single lived reality.

This process of categorization carried concrete social consequences that extended well beyond nomenclature. Newly arrived Mizrahi families were frequently settled in peripheral “development towns” and transit camps (ma’abarot) under conditions markedly inferior to those afforded many Ashkenazi immigrants; Mizrahi children were disproportionately tracked into vocational rather than academic secondary education; and Mizrahi cultural production — Arabic and Judeo-Arabic language and music above all — was actively discouraged within Israeli state institutions in favor of a Hebrew, European-oriented national culture. The category “Mizrahi,” in other words, was not merely descriptive but constitutive: it helped produce the very social and economic marginalization that later Mizrahi political movements, discussed below, would mobilize to contest.

Poetic Trespass: Bilingual Literatures and the Afterlives of Arabic

One of the most illuminating sites for observing both the rupture and its incomplete character is literature. Lital Levy’s study of the relationship between Hebrew and Arabic in the culture of Israel/Palestine traces a body of prose, poetry, film, and visual art — produced by both Jewish and Palestinian citizens of Israel across more than a century — in which writers repeatedly cross the boundary that Israeli national ideology worked to establish between a Hebrew-speaking Jewish nation and an Arabic-speaking Palestinian other (Levy, 2014). Among her central cases are Mizrahi and Arab-Jewish writers such as Samir Naqqash, an Iraqi-born author who continued writing fiction in literary Arabic in Israel decades after his community’s displacement, precisely as an act of resistance to the erasure of that linguistic and cultural inheritance.

Levy’s analysis demonstrates that Jews writing in Arabic, or in markedly Mizrahi registers of Hebrew inflected by Arabic phonology and syntax, cannot but challenge a nationalist linguistic project that depends, in significant part, on the exclusive identification of Hebrew with Jewishness and Arabic with Palestinian or generalized Arab otherness (Levy, 2014). The persistence of such writing — marginal, often institutionally unsupported, but never fully extinguished — constitutes evidence that the civilizational rupture discussed above, however administratively and socially thorough, never achieved complete cultural closure: the Judeo-Arabic and Arabic literary sensibility that Alcalay locates in the medieval and early modern Mizrahi Mediterranean survived, in attenuated and contested form, into the literature of the state that had been built, in part, on that sensibility’s official disavowal.

Political Mobilization in Israel: The Black Panthers and the Mizrahi Democratic Rainbow

The social marginalization produced by Mizrahi categorization did not go uncontested. By the early 1970s, Mizrahi Jews constituted more than half of Israel’s Jewish population yet remained substantially excluded from its political, economic, and cultural leadership, then as now dominated by Ashkenazi elites. In 1971, a group of young Mizrahi Jews in Jerusalem’s impoverished Musrara neighborhood, explicitly modeling themselves on the African American civil rights movement, founded an organization known as the Black Panthers (HaPanterim HaShehorim), which for nearly two years organized protests, demonstrations, and direct actions against housing discrimination, educational tracking, and police harassment.

Sami Shalom Chetrit’s history of intra-Jewish conflict in Israel situates the Black Panther movement as the decisive turning point in a longer arc running from the initial “shock and protest” of the 1950s ma’abarot period through a subsequent era of what he terms “radical awareness” extending into the twenty-first century, culminating in movements such as the Mizrahi Democratic Rainbow Coalition, of which Chetrit himself was a co-founder, and the rise of the religious Sephardic party Shas as an alternative, more institutionally embedded vehicle for Mizrahi political mobilization (Chetrit, 2010). Chetrit’s analysis frames this trajectory explicitly through the vocabulary of ethnic and, at moments, racialized conflict — “white Jews, black Jews” — arguing that the encounter between Ashkenazi Zionism and Mizrahi immigrants reproduced, within a single ostensibly unified national community, hierarchies of the kind more commonly associated with settler-colonial or racially stratified societies elsewhere (Chetrit, 2010). Whatever the merits of that comparative framing, the Black Panther movement and its successors mark the moment at which Mizrahi civilizational distinctiveness was transformed from a stigmatized inheritance into an explicit basis for collective political claim-making within the Israeli state.

Mizrahi Studies and the Reclamation of Historical Memory

Since the 1990s, and building directly on the political mobilization described above, a body of academic scholarship self-consciously organized as “Mizrahi Studies” has sought to reverse the historiographical erasure that accompanied the mid-century rupture, recovering the literary, musical, and intellectual production of Iraqi, Egyptian, Syrian, Yemenite, and Maghrebi Jews as integral to modern Arab and Mediterranean cultural history rather than as a mere prelude to Israeli absorption (Shohat, 2001; Alcalay, 1993). Shohat’s own scholarly project has been foundational to this field, proposing what she terms a “diasporized” analytical framework that situates Mizrahi culture and history within a multi-chronotopic understanding of time and space, resistant to the linear, redemptive narrative of exile and return that Zionist historiography imposed upon it (Shohat, 2001).

This scholarship treats the Mizrahi Mediterranean not as a closed chapter but as an active site of historical and political contestation, in which questions of language revival, heritage preservation, diplomatic normalization between Israel and Arab states, and communal memory continue to reshape how the civilization is narrated on both shores of the sea. It has also generated its own internal debates — over the political utility and possible essentialism of the “Arab Jew” category, over the relationship between Mizrahi and Palestinian claims on Israeli society, and over how to weigh the genuine hardship and discrimination faced by Mizrahi immigrants against the deeper civilizational loss represented by the near-total disappearance of organized Jewish life across the Arab world. These debates remain unresolved, and properly so: they are constitutive of an ongoing historiographical reckoning rather than a settled scholarly consensus.

The Moroccan Paradigm

Morocco offers perhaps the most instructive single case of this longue-durée civilization, precisely because its Jewish community — the largest in the Arab world prior to the mid-twentieth-century exodus — combined every stratum discussed above: an indigenous pre-Islamic and Amazigh-inflected substratum, a Judeo-Arabic and Judeo-Berber linguistic culture, a substantial post-1492 Sephardic infusion concentrated in the northern coastal cities, an Alliance Israélite Universelle educational network that Europeanized a portion of the urban elite, and, in the modern period, a relationship to the Moroccan state and monarchy markedly different from the pattern of rupture that characterized much of the Mashriq (Chtatou, 2023; Gottreich, 2021).

Emily Gottreich identifies the Jewish presence as one of five constitutive pillars of Moroccan identity, alongside Malikism, Amazighity, Sharifism, and Arabness — an unusually explicit acknowledgment, within a national historiography, of the co-constitutive role Mizrahi and Sephardi Jews played in shaping a modern Muslim-majority polity rather than functioning merely as a tolerated minority within it (Gottreich, 2021). This framing helps explain features of the Moroccan case that distinguish it from the more abrupt ruptures documented in Iraq, Egypt, Syria, and Yemen: the continued, if now largely symbolic, royal patronage of Jewish heritage sites and institutions; the survival of the mellah as a recognized urban and architectural form in cities such as Fez, Marrakech, and Essaouira; and the persistence, well after the community’s demographic decline, of Jewish cultural references — in cuisine, music, and popular memory — within mainstream Moroccan society (Chtatou, 2023).

None of this should obscure the fact that Morocco, too, experienced a substantial exodus: from a community numbering some quarter of a million at its mid-century peak, emigration — driven by many of the same regional pressures affecting Mashriqi communities, alongside more specifically Moroccan factors including uncertainty surrounding independence in 1956 and organized clandestine emigration networks — reduced the community to a few thousand within a generation, the overwhelming majority relocating to Israel, France, and Canada. What distinguishes the Moroccan case is less the scale of departure than the character of what remained: an official and popular Moroccan discourse that continues to claim Jewish heritage as part of the national patrimony, formalized most explicitly in the 2011 constitution’s acknowledgment of the kingdom’s “Hebraic” cultural tributary, and reflected in continuing scholarly, touristic, and diplomatic engagement with Judeo-Moroccan history (Chtatou, 2023). The Moroccan paradigm thus complicates any narrative of total Mizrahi rupture from the Arab-Islamic Mediterranean, suggesting that the civilizational synthesis this essay has traced, while everywhere destabilized by the mid-twentieth-century crisis, was not everywhere dissolved with equal finality.

A Comparative Note: Mizrahi and Ashkenazi Civilizational Trajectories

Much of the analytical force of the Mizrahi category, both as an Israeli administrative classification and as a critical scholarly tool, derives from an implicit or explicit contrast with Ashkenazi Jewish civilization — the Yiddish-speaking, Central and Eastern European Jewish world that, for demographic and historiographical reasons connected to the Holocaust and the political dominance of European Zionist founders, has long occupied the default position in general accounts of “Jewish history.” The contrast is instructive precisely because it clarifies, by opposition, the specific features this essay has attributed to the Mizrahi Mediterranean: a civilization built within, rather than alongside, a single overarching religious-political order (Dar al-Islam) rather than the politically fragmented and religiously exclusive polities of medieval and early modern Christian Europe; a primary vernacular, Judeo-Arabic and its regional cognates, that was a dialect of the majority language rather than an internally developed Jewish language such as Yiddish; and a modern rupture driven by decolonization and Arab-Israeli conflict rather than by industrialized genocide.

These differences should not be overstated into a simple hierarchy of relative suffering or relative integration; Cohen’s comparative framework, discussed above, insists on holding both civilizational trajectories to the same rigorous standard of evidence rather than treating either as a template of either idyllic coexistence or inevitable persecution (Cohen, 1994/2008). What the comparison does usefully reveal is the historical contingency of treating “Jewish history” as a single, Ashkenazi-centered narrative into which Mizrahi communities are inserted as a regional variant. The civilization this essay has traced possessed its own centers of religious authority, its own golden ages, its own internal sectarian debates, and its own catastrophic modern rupture, developing for the greater part of two millennia along a trajectory shaped primarily by its relationship to Islamicate rather than Christian political order. Any comparative history of world Jewry adequate to this fact must treat the Mizrahi Mediterranean not as a periphery of Ashkenazi Europe but as one of at most two or three civilizational centers — alongside Ashkenaz and, in its own right, Sepharad — around which the modern Jewish world’s demographic and cultural geography has been organized.

Concluding Synthesis

The salient aspects of Mizrahi civilization in the Mediterranean are therefore not reducible to a single trait but to a layered and contested formation built up over some two and a half millennia. Its foundation is an antique Eastern diasporic substratum — Babylonian, Persian, Yemenite, Egyptian, North African — that long antedates Islam and that produced, in the Babylonian Talmud, rabbinic Judaism’s own foundational legal text. Onto this foundation the Islamic conquests grafted a durable political and legal order, the dhimma, within which Jewish communities achieved a degree of communal autonomy, economic integration, and intellectual participation that gave rise, across Baghdad, Córdoba, Kairouan, and Fez, to a genuine shared civilization rather than a merely tolerated coexistence — a civilization whose primary linguistic vehicle, Judeo-Arabic, and whose most vivid documentary record, the Cairo Geniza, together attest to a Mediterranean world organized around mobility, intermediation, and multilingual fluency rather than segregation.

The post-1492 arrival of Sephardic refugees thickened rather than erased this substratum, producing by the early modern period a genuine mosaic of regionally distinct communities — Iraqi, Egyptian, Syrian, Yemenite, Moroccan — each retaining its own liturgical, linguistic, and social particularity while sharing a common Islamicate matrix, further complicated in the nineteenth century by the partial Europeanization introduced through colonial-era educational institutions such as the Alliance Israélite Universelle. It was this multilayered civilization, not a homogeneous or static one, that underwent the extraordinarily rapid rupture of the mid-twentieth century, as decolonization, Arab nationalism, and the Arab-Israeli conflict combined to displace the overwhelming majority of these communities within little more than a generation — a rupture that, in the very process of displacement, generated the administrative category “Mizrahi” through which this civilization has most often been discussed ever since, and that has, since the 1970s, become the object of sustained political mobilization and, since the 1990s, of a deliberate scholarly and literary reclamation.

To read this civilization adequately, then, requires holding together what Zionist, Arab-nationalist, and Orientalist historiographies have each, for different reasons, preferred to keep apart: the fact that for the greater part of two millennia, to be a Jew in Baghdad, Fez, Cairo, Aleppo, or Sana’a was to be, in the fullest and least contradictory sense, a person of the Mediterranean and Islamicate East — and that the twentieth-century unmaking of that reality, however historically decisive, has not exhausted its civilizational afterlife, whether in the bilingual literature Levy identifies, the political consciousness Chetrit traces, the scholarly recovery Shohat and Alcalay have undertaken, or the still-living, if diminished, presence that cases such as Morocco’s continue to preserve.

It is worth closing, finally, on a methodological note that follows directly from Cohen’s comparative caution discussed above. Any civilizational history of this kind is written from a present vantage point shaped by the very rupture it describes, and is therefore always at risk of reading that rupture backward into the historical record — either as an inevitable culmination of ancient antagonisms, or, in reaction, as a wholly external betrayal of an otherwise harmonious past. The evidence surveyed in this essay supports neither simplification. It supports instead a civilization that was, across its documented history, simultaneously more integrated into its surrounding Islamicate societies and more institutionally distinct from them than either romantic or polemical accounts typically allow; that produced genuine intellectual and commercial achievement precisely through that combination of integration and distinctiveness; and that was undone, in the space of a single generation, not by any single cause but by the historically specific convergence of decolonization, nationalist consolidation, and an unresolved territorial conflict whose consequences continue to shape how the civilization’s own history is told. A dense and compact account such as this one can only gesture toward the fuller regional, linguistic, and archival scholarship on which it draws; its more modest aim has been to establish that the Mizrahi Mediterranean deserves to be studied, and taught, as precisely such a civilization — layered, contested, and only partially recoverable, but irreducibly its own.

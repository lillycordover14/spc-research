Everyday Clay: Permission to change

By 
Clay Bowen / Special to The Dispatch 
 • 4 hours ago  

  • 5 mins to read

		Clay Bowen	

We’re all gonna be okay, even me. Although try telling me that last Sunday while I was riding my bike alone at the riverwalk. What I presumed to be a quintessential autumn morning for a bike ride quickly turned into an existential crisis.

My reprieve by the river was interrupted by a restless mind and a feeling of loneliness. I was operating from a place of nostalgia, which managed to create a liminal space for my fear of change.

It’s commonly accepted that “fall” is the flagship cliché we like to use to best describe a time for change and transition. As the foliage of surrounding trees goes from green to orange and then brown, it’s hard not to immerse ourselves into such a metamorphosis.

But as life changes around us, do we change as well, or do we just adapt?

Historically, my bike rides have been accompanied by my son, Landon. Since he was a little boy, we would explore the streets of the Southside. Our adventures were endless and the conversations were too. Landon doesn’t miss the slightest nuance in his surroundings and he loves discussing each detail.

From the railroad tracks east of the “W” to Snake Hill, we’ve explored it all. We’ve visited the river while in flood, analyzed footprints in the mud, pointed out trumpet vines, and quenched our thirst at River Hill Chevron, marveling at the price of Arizona tea hovering at 99 cents for over three decades.

Those conversations welcomed complexities for us to investigate on the “ride” that I always cherished. The bikes were simply a vessel for making it happen.

I actually wrote a short story a while back titled “Biking With My Boy.” In summation, it was about the melancholic progression of change viewed through those rides with Landon.

I’m reminded that time truly is enigmatic! It ditches the map and builds the road for us.

I’d asked Landon to grab his bike and join me that day, hoping he was feeling as nostalgic about autumn as I was, but he had other plans; plans that didn’t include me.

I pedaled a little slower than normal on that particular ride, mainly because I wasn’t trying to keep up with Landon, and moreover, so that I could process what was going on inside and around me.

I’m in a weird place right now. I’ve written about it before, but middle age is deceiving. When I look in the mirror, I still see a younger man, but my family is ever evolving, which frequently makes me feel that I didn’t get the memo.

I guess I’ve forgotten that life doesn’t need my permission to do things on its terms?

As I pedaled along, my bike tires crunched across the fallen leaves and mists of water sprayed my back from the wet concrete. I had a heightened sense of awareness for every sight, sound, and emotion that sought my permission to join me.

The colors of the trees were like flames, the river was ever-present and slow; slower than me. I thought about how I love that river, and how it’s been a quiet listener to so many of our conversations. The wind blew across my body, reminding me of when I was 12, riding my bike down First Street. It felt like flying back then, and it did that day too.

I began to think of everyone I love and how they’re exactly where they should be in life; even Landon, even in that moment.

There’s an interesting facet of life that is somewhat paradoxical. As time marches on, we find ourselves happy that we’re sad. This pensive feeling is typically brought on by change. It’s counterintuitive, but sometimes when we lose, we win.

Change is an acquired taste. Especially when it’s seasoned with bittersweet irony. There’s an ebb and flow to our existence; a contract between us and the universe that was signed long before our mortal bodies decided to get in the way. Call it what you will, destiny, fate, maybe purpose. Whatever it may be, it’s guided by change whether we give it permission or not.

As I made my way back home, I knew that my outward emotions didn’t match my newfound inner peace. I was missing the same smile that once existed when “Biking With My Boy.”

I crested the hill at Main Street and stopped for a refreshment at River Hill Chevron. I pulled my phone out to check the time and realized I had a text from Landon. After taking a swig of Arizona tea, I wiped the moisture from my glasses and opened the text. It was a picture of Landon and some friends, doing exactly what life would have them doing.

I looked down at my 99-cent can of tea and chuckled. I then hopped on my bike and smiled the rest of the ride home.

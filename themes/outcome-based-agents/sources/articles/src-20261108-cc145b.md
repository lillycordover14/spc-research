Nooks CEO Dan Lee argues that persuasion, not reasoning, is the technical bottleneck holding AI back from sales — and that a structural shortage of persuasion data in frontier-model training sets has left an opening for vertical specialists. Armed with $500 million in non-dilutive capital from General Catalyst, Nooks is fine-tuning its own open-weight models on proprietary sales data, building reinforcement-learning simulations of customer reactions, and pitching a "self-learning revenue system" where humans set strategy and agents do everything else. Lee draws a sharp line between "easy persuasion" deals that can be fully automated and complex multi-stakeholder sales that still require a human — a distinction that determines which sales jobs survive. He also makes a contrarian economic bet: unlike support, sales efficiency gains expand headcount demand rather than shrink it. The central performance claim is unverified — Nooks publishes no benchmark numbers against Claude or ChatGPT — and the $500 million's terms are undisclosed. The question for investors is whether Nooks can demonstrate reproducible benchmarks and whether "more efficient means more selling" holds in a world already drowning in AI-generated outreach.

The $500 million bet that persuasion is the next AI frontier

Dan Lee has a problem with the way most people think about AI and jobs. Everyone assumes that when a model gets good at a task, the people doing that task disappear. Lee, co-founder and CEO of the San Francisco sales-AI startup Nooks, thinks the exact opposite will happen in his industry — and he has $500 million of other people's money to prove it.

"In the US there are actually 12 million Americans who sell for a living, and it's more than all these other jobs combined," Lee said on the MTS podcast, comparing sales to legal, customer support, and back-office roles. Sales, he argues, is the largest knowledge profession in the country, the one that has changed the least in the AI era, and the one where improving the tools actually makes companies want 
more
 of it.

That counterintuitive dynamic is the heart of Lee's pitch. In support or accounting, making a team more efficient doesn't make anyone want more support tickets or more audits. In sales, it's the reverse. "When you can make a sales team more efficient, you actually normally want more sales," he said. "Whereas in support, if you can make a support team more efficient, you normally don't want more support tickets."

If Lee is right, the entire premise of "AI will replace salespeople" is backwards. If he's wrong, he has raised a very large amount of money to find out.

Why sales is a harder AI problem than coding

The temptation is to assume sales is 
easier
 for AI than engineering — it's just talking to people, right? Lee argues the opposite, and his reasoning is the most technically interesting part of the conversation.

The problem is data. Frontier models have been trained on mountains of code, which is verifiable: a program either compiles and passes tests or it doesn't. Sales has no compiler. "There's just been less data on persuasion in the training sets for frontier models because persuasion is all about understanding what people think and that's not observable," Lee said.

That's a structural gap, not a temporary one. You can scrape every public repository on earth to teach a model to code. You cannot scrape the inside of a buyer's head to teach a model to sell. Persuasion is an inference problem about unobservable mental states, and that makes it qualitatively different from the reasoning tasks where models have made rapid progress.

Nooks's answer is to manufacture the missing data. The company builds reinforcement-learning environments for sales — simulations of how a customer might react, run across hundreds of scenarios to find which approach performs best. Lee compares it to how a coding agent pulls relevant parts of a codebase, except that in sales the agent has to decide what counts as a "relevant sales scenario" — same persona, similar objections. He calls that "a hard technical problem" solved through Nooks's own agent harness.

The company also sits between the rep and the end customer, which gives it visibility into every rep decision and every customer reaction. From that vantage point it runs an active-learning process to model the rep's decision policy — mapping scenarios to a discrete set of actions — and builds agents that simulate the customer.

"Sales is a lot less verifiable," Lee said. "We're building like basically these simulations where you can simulate how a customer might react so you can actually test hundreds of scenarios to see which one's going to perform optimally."

The unverified claim: Nooks's models vs. Claude and ChatGPT

The headline assertion from the episode is that Nooks fine-tunes its own open-weight models on proprietary sales data, and that these models outperform Claude and ChatGPT on sales-specific benchmarks across cost, quality, and latency.

That is a company claim, offered without numbers. No benchmark scores, no methodology, no third-party verification. Lee's broader argument — that vertical AI companies can beat general-purpose frontier models by specializing — is plausible and increasingly common in enterprise software. But the specific comparison to Claude and ChatGPT is the kind of claim that should be treated as marketing until the underlying data is published.

What 
is
 verifiable from the company's own disclosure is commercial traction. Nooks says more than 2,000 companies use its platform, spanning AI-native startups like Cursor and Notion to enterprises like Toast and ServiceTitan. The company has reported a net revenue retention rate of 135% and said annual recurring revenue is on pace to nearly triple by year-end, without disclosing the current figure.

The funding itself is unusual enough to warrant scrutiny. The $500 million from General Catalyst's Customer Value Fund is not a conventional equity round — the firm is reportedly not taking a stake and instead will collect a portion of the returns its capital generates. Lee declined to disclose the terms. Pranav Singhvi, a partner and co-head of the fund at General Catalyst, said the platform "has shown a highly predictable revenue engine and strong unit economics."

Lee frames the capital as fuel for an industry transformation rather than a typical venture round. He says it goes to four areas: growth in less AI-advanced verticals, new geographies (a New York go-to-market office plus the UK, Ireland, and Australia), an expanded product suite across the revenue lifecycle, and applied research. "In order to completely transform any industry, you need a lot of capital," he said, calling the structure "the best way to finance both aggressive growth and also taking new products to market."

The line between "easy persuasion" and "hard persuasion"

Here is where Lee's worldview gets practical. He doesn't think all sales jobs are safe, and he doesn't think all sales jobs disappear. He splits the profession in two.

"There's easy sales problems... that's going to be fully automated with agents," he said. "But there are also hard persuasion problems where you have 20 stakeholders in a deal and you haven't talked to 10 of them yet... that's going to need a human."

The distinction is about the structure of the deal, not the seniority of the seller. Easy persuasion is an inbound prospect who arrives mostly sold and just needs a few questions answered. Nooks already has agents that can demo the product and hold a full conversation with a customer. Hard persuasion is a complex enterprise deal with two dozen stakeholders, an unidentified budget, and no product awareness — where the human's job is strategy and relationships, and the agent's job is all the background work.

That second category maps directly onto the products Nooks is now building. The company started with tools for reps — calling, emailing, research — which were then translated for use by agents. It is expanding from top-of-funnel, where results show quickly, into inbound conversation agents, deal agents that track what is slipping and manage follow-ups, and renewal and expansion agents.

The contrarian bet: better AI makes selling more consultative, not less human

Lee's most interesting philosophical claim is that better persuasion tools don't make selling more transactional — they make it more human. Ask him whether a world of highly capable persuasion tools is a better one, and he doesn't flinch.

His argument is that a good salesperson provides value by understanding the person across from them. If AI saves a rep four hours a day, the question is what the rep does with that time. "When you can save a sales rep 4 hours of their day, what do they do with it? I think in a lot of cases it actually means they become a lot more consultative," Lee said.

He offers a head-to-head market test: if Company A uses the reclaimed hours to go deeper with customers and Company B uses them to chase more customers, Company A wins the deal. He concedes this depends on how competitive the space is and how good the product actually is — an important caveat, since a commoditized market rewards volume, not depth.

He illustrates the same principle with a recruiting analogy. If one company fully automates interviews with AI and another uses AI only to save the interviewer time, candidates will likely prefer the second for the better experience. Sales works the same way: trust-building and solving real customer problems stay human, while AI handles the work behind the scenes.

Lee extends the point well beyond sales. "Everything is sales," he said — recruiting, raising capital, hiring candidates. He recalls a 2020 plan to join an Oxford study-abroad research project on emotion detection, motivated by cameras that can read pupil dilation, heart rate through the neck, and capillary flushing in the cheek. Ask a politician whether they will invade a country, he says, and they may not answer — but they may react. It's a reminder that the technical frontier of persuasion is far beyond what most sales tools currently attempt.

Node: a proactive, multiplayer assistant for revenue teams

The newest product is Node, which Nooks launched just before the interview — pitched as the first AI personal assistant built specifically for sales, comparable to general assistants like Instinct or Muse but purpose-built for reps.

Three things distinguish it, according to Lee. It runs on Nooks's sales-fine-tuned models. It is 
proactive
 — surfacing warm introductions and missed follow-ups rather than waiting to be asked, a nod to the founder's experience of managing hundreds of deals in his head. And it is 
multiplayer
, because a real sales motion involves a rep, an account manager, an SDR, and an engineer, each with their own Node.

The stated goal is a self-learning revenue system in which humans do exactly two things — build strategy and engage customers — and agents handle the rest. Winning behaviors from top reps are meant to spread automatically across the team, closing the gap between the best seller and everyone else.

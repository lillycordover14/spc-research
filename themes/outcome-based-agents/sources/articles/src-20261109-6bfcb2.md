From ten paces the boot looks plain: army-green canvas over a black rubber lower, heavy cord laces, a lugged Vibram sole. Open the front zip, though, and the camouflage shows. Restraint is the design here, and it works because everything underneath the canvas stays Sorel.

Maharishi built the Sorel x Maharishi Daystorm Horizon Zip GTX on the standard Daystorm Horizon GTX and left the all-weather construction alone. GORE-TEX waterproofing, the molded rubber lower and the lugged Vibram outsole all carry over untouched from the boot Sorel already sells, at the spec Sorel already publishes.

What changes is the surface: the upper goes canvas in a flat army green, and the branding shrinks to patches.

The Daystorm sits in Sorelâs Horizon line, the range that reworks the Canadian brandâs Caribou duck-boot DNA into lighter boots built for city winters. A boot from that line is a strange base for a fashion collaboration. It is also a fitting one, since the canvas upper takes print and dye the way military kit does.

A shroud over the zip

A removable shroud zips over the laces and tongue and gives the boot a clean, closed face. Under it sits the front zip, so entry stays quick, and lifting the shroud lays the whole front bare.

Zip-front boots usually shout about the zip, and the hardware becomes the whole look. This one hides it.

Thick coiled cord runs the lacing, through metal eyelets, in the military style the design calls for. The cord is fat enough to grab with a gloved hand, and the coils sit proud of the shroud like climbing rope. It is the loudest hardware on the boot, and it is still just cord.

The removable shroud zips over the front of the boot, with the coiled cord lacing running over the top. Photo: Maharishi

The camo stays hidden

DPM: Bonsai Tigerstripe prints the tongue and the inner collar in brushstroke bands of olive, brown and black, with a fine repeat that keeps the pattern tight. Tigerstripe came out of Vietnam-era special forces kit, and Maharishi has cut, recolored and reworked the pattern for three decades, shrinking it here to the smaller Bonsai scale. At a glance it passes for texture. Up close, it is unmistakably camo.

DPM: Bonsai Tigerstripe prints the tongue, hidden until the front zip opens. Photo: Maharishi

Keeping the print inside the boot is a deliberate choice. Maharishi calls its language Pacifist Military Design: military patterns and military construction, cut and sold as clothing, with the aggression designed out of it.

Under the canvas, the spec sheet is Sorelâs, with a GORE-TEX bootie sealing the upper and the technical build carried over whole from the standard boot.

The lower half is molded rubber in the duck-boot pattern, seamed where the canvas meets it and capped at the front with a five-finger rubber toe cap. Debossed detailing runs across the cap. Rubber takes the scuffs, the slush and the curb strikes that a canvas upper cannot, and it keeps the working half of the boot true to the original Sorel design brief: a waterproof shell first, a shoe second.

Grip comes from a lugged Vibram XS Trek Evo outsole.

Collabs usually ask buyers to pay for a logo and forgive the product underneath it. This one reverses the arrangement, and that is why it lands.

Start with what the money buys, because the list is short and specific. It buys the olive canvas, the hidden Bonsai Tigerstripe, the shroud over the zip and the coiled cord lacing, on top of a waterproof boot that already worked. It does not buy better waterproofing, a better sole or a warmer boot, since Sorel sells those in the standard version today. If the surface work is the point for you, the collab is the buy.

If you just need the boot, the standard Daystorm at $199.99 on Amazon is the same weather tool for less money, in your pick of seven colors. The collab is for the buyer who wants the camo, and wants it hidden.

Ema Nikolovska and Sean Shibe Bring a Phantasmagoric Orlando to Hertz Hall&nbsp;&nbsp;San Francisco Classical Voice

[page text]
Guitarist Sean Shibe and vocalist Ema Nikolovska | 
Credit: Peter Adamik
The delightful surprise of seeing a woman in a shimmering jacket covered in dense gold paillettes walk onstage beside a guitarist wearing an Elizabethan neck ruff atop a minimalist modern shirt and ready to play both a custom-made classical instrument and a Fender Stratocaster was the perfect beginning to a thought-provoking program from mezzo-soprano Ema Nikolovska and guitarist Sean Shibe, presented by Cal Performances at Berkeley’s Hertz Hall on Sunday, Nov. 1.

Virginia Woolf’s playful and kaleidoscopic novel 
Orlando
 was the principal inspiration behind the superbly performed recital titled 
Orlando Variations
. Woolf’s novel is about a nobleman at the court of England’s Queen Elizabeth I who is 16 years old when the novel begins, lives for more than three centuries, changes gender from male to female while serving as ambassador in 17th-century Constantinople, and is only 36 years old at the novel’s conclusion in 1928. Nikolovska and Shibe used this structure to create a phantasmagoric program in which ancient and modern are in a dialogue exploring themes of love, politics, identity, religion, gender, depression, and joy. In addition to voice, acoustic and electric guitars, the performers used percussion, plus electronic and recorded sounds. The program included six recent commissions, four of them premiered on this tour, by composers born as recently as 2002, alongside a centuries-spanning lineup that ran from John Dowland and Franz Schubert to Bob Dylan and Laurie Anderson.

 

Ema Nikolovska | 
Credit: Kaupo 
Kikkas
Changing attitudes toward life were dramatically illustrated by the opening and closing compositions. In Jules Massenet’s “Ô Souverain, ô juge, ô père” from his 1885 opera 
Le Cid
, a soldier facing defeat sings a prayer of resignation to an all-powerful God. Almost a century later, Laurie Anderson’s “O Superman” parodies Massenet’s aria by changing God to a Superman representing an automated war-making system that claims to be omnipotent but ultimately fails human beings. Nikolovska is a marvelously flexible artist and sang the Massenet with noble and restrained passion and the Anderson with ironic detachment rooted in minimalism and electronic music.

Shibe followed the opening Massenet with a solo performance of John Dowland’s Praeludium, P. 98. The Renaissance prelude evolved from improvised pieces meant to test the acoustics of a performance space and allow the performer to warm up his hands. As time went on, the improvisatory structure was seen by performer-composers like Dowland to offer an intellectual and musical challenge that could serve as a vehicle for both virtuosity and deep emotion. Shibe’s masterful performance balanced a rhythmic flexibility that honored the piece’s improvisational roots with a sensitivity to the structural coherence that made the work profound. Nikolovska and Shibe then began three Dowland songs, “Come Again, Sweet Love Doth Now Invite,” “Come, Heavy Sleep,” and “Think’st Thou Then by Thy Feigning,” bringing to life the atmosphere of the beginning of Woolf’s novel as well as themes of love, deception, sorrow, sleep, and death.

The afternoon was a hybrid of concert and theater, with the lighting and sound crew playing an important, if offstage, role. The 16th-century set of music by Dowland seamlessly transitioned to the stunning 
1000 parts of you
, written by Sasha Scott when she was 22, for wordlessly intoning mezzo-soprano, bowed, plucked, and strummed electric guitar, and electronic effects. Dramatically enhanced by lighting, it evoked Woolf’s notion that every person contains thousands of selves. Also inspired by the 
Infinity Mirror Rooms
 of Yayoi Kusama, Scott created a sound world that felt both contemplative and overwhelming.

Commissioned pieces by Linda Catlin Smith, Nahre Sol, and Maddie Ashman used texts excerpted from or inspired by Woolf’s 
Orlando
 to explore notions of tenderness, confusion, anxiety, and ecstasy. Cassandra Miller’s 
Dream Memorandum (It Reminded Me of the Truth)
 used recordings of WhatsApp voice notes from Nikolovska to the composer, as well as live speaking, singing, evocative guitar playing, and an arresting extended section of recorded birdsong and a found percussion instrument to create an intimate picture of the emotions and ideals that motivated the entire program of 
Orlando Variations
.

 

Sean Shibe | 
Credit: Kaupo Kikkas
The program did not try to recreate Woolf’s narrative but instead embraced a stream-of-consciousness aesthetic that mirrored Woolf’s. The loneliness of Schubert’s “Einsamkeit,” from 
Winterreise
, led to the melancholy longing of Detlev Glanert’s “Lied der Wehmut,” from his 
Orlando-Lieder
, inspired by Ludovico Ariosto’s 
Orlando furioso
 and the music of medieval troubadours. This was followed by the surreal paranoia of the “Habanera” from Thomas Adès’s opera 
The Exterminating Angel
.

The program concluded with Dylan’s “Masters of War” and Anderson’s “O Superman.” After several bows, cheers, and a standing ovation, Nikolovska and Shibe ended the afternoon with Alex Paxton’s “Be Kind,” a gentle vocal exhortation to “be kind to yourself, be kind to the others” accompanied by ecstatic commentary on electric guitar.

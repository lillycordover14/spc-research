Plurai Raises $13M Seed to Stress-Test AI Agents in Digital Twin Simulations

Plurai, an artificial intelligence startup founded by two former Nexar researchers, emerged from stealth on Wednesday with $13 million in seed funding and general availability for a platform that borrows a page from autonomous-vehicle development to test and harden AI agents before and after they reach production.

Team8 led the round, with U&I, Velocity and Mercer participating alongside more than 20 angel investors and industry figures. Backers include Udi Mokady, founder of CyberArk, and Reah Miyara, a senior director of Google Models at Google Cloud who previously ran model evaluation at OpenAI. Dr. Eyal Carmi, chief technology officer of Microsoft 365 Copilot, has joined the company's advisory board.

Plurai was founded in 2025 by CEO Dr. Ilan Kadar, who holds a PhD in computer science and machine learning, and CTO Dr. Elad Levi, a mathematics PhD. The two spent more than a decade building and deploying large-scale AI systems, most recently at Nexar, where Kadar was vice president of AI and Levi an AI architect. Their work included simulation systems for autonomous-driving companies such as Waymo, which lean on simulated miles to validate self-driving software before it hits the road.

The pitch is that AI agents share the same weakness as early autonomous systems: they are probabilistic programs operating in messy, unpredictable environments. A travel-booking agent can quietly ignore a corporate spending policy. An agent that queries a single database may answer reliably, but stitching together information from several sources often breaks down. Long customer-service conversations can overwhelm an agent with context and send it down the wrong path.

The company's platform builds a digital twin of an organization's knowledge base, tools and policies, maps the flows an agent can take, then runs thousands of simulated users against it inside an isolated sandbox. The tests cover realistic and adversarial scenarios and are designed to surface policy violations, fabricated answers, unsafe actions, tool-use errors and sensitive-data leaks. When a failure turns up, the system proposes a fix and re-runs the simulation to confirm the agent improved without breaking something else.

Plurai says early customer deployments have compressed agent development cycles from months to hours, cut failures reaching real users by 82% and reduced the cost of running reliable agents by 97%. The figures were provided by the company and have not been independently verified.

The simulation data also feeds a second stage: post-training small, open-weight language models tuned to a specific agent's tasks. Those smaller models can act as always-on monitors in production, checking every interaction and enforcing company policy at a fraction of the cost of frontier models. Plurai says its research shows hundreds of such specialized models can be served in real time on a single GPU.

In work with Nvidia and Nebius, a model Plurai post-trained for a global enterprise customer beat leading models from OpenAI and Anthropic on accuracy at 40 and 44 times lower cost, respectively, according to the company. Plurai says developers can turn an open model into a task-specific one in roughly 20 minutes.

"The moment that we saw the wave of AI agents, we saw the same pattern: That it is very easy to build a demo, but [hard] to get something that will be reliable and cost effective," Kadar told TechCrunch. "We can take a large language model, we can take the task, decompose it into a smaller task using the simulation, train [an open-weight] model, and using the simulation, verify that the quality is higher or comparable to the frontier model, and an order or two orders of magnitude cost reduction."

He framed the company's edge in blunt terms: "My view is that no matter how smart OpenAI and Anthropic model will be -- and they will be much smarter -- they will not know your business and your policy."

Miyara, the angel investor, said the advantage lies in understanding how agents fail in production and generating synthetic data to tune models accordingly. As enterprises try to contain AI spending, that could matter: technology leaders are finding that "frontier-capable models are out of distribution with my specific data, and the cost of these things isn't sustainable in the long term," Miyara said.

The market Plurai is targeting is one where demos routinely outrun deployments. A VentureBeat Pulse Research survey cited by the company found that half of enterprises had shipped an AI agent or large language model feature that cleared internal evaluations only to cause a customer-facing failure in production. Benchmarking efforts point the same way: LiveBench 2.0 put Claude Fable 5.2 and ChatGPT-6 Astra at 82.9 and 75.6 tasks out of 100, while a simulated corporate-environment test by infrastructure firm EON found the best-performing model, Claude Opus 4.8, completing tasks at 76.8% accuracy.

Plurai's platform, previously limited to select partners, is now open to developers, including through a free tier. It plugs into tools such as Claude Code, Codex, GitHub Copilot and Cursor, and can test agents built on enterprise platforms including Salesforce Agentforce.

The company employs about 12 people in Israel and the United States and plans to double headcount and expand its U.S. operations by the end of 2026. The fresh capital will go toward research and development teams, deeper enterprise sales and faster U.S. growth.

For investors, the bet is that reliability, not raw model capability, becomes the binding constraint on enterprise agent adoption -- and that a simulation-and-evaluation layer can capture value even as foundation models from OpenAI and Anthropic keep improving. For enterprises, the appeal is economic: monitoring every agent interaction with frontier models is prohibitively expensive at scale, which is why most firms sample. Whether Plurai's distilled monitors hold up across the long tail of production traffic will determine if the Waymo analogy travels from the road to the back office.

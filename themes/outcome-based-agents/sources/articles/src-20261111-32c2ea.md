Given the international success of Toho’s “Godzilla Minus One” in 2023, including an Oscar for Best Visual Effects (a first in franchise history), it came as no surprise that the studio would be gearing up for another go-round with the immortal screen monster.
With Takashi Yamazaki back on board as writer, director, and VFX supervisor, “Godzilla Minus Zero” is nearly the equal of its predecessor, which was considered by many to be the greatest Godzilla movie ever. For devotees of the franchise, it’s an absolute blast, and like the first film, may well attract new fans into the fold.
The action gets off to a rip-roaring start immediately, as the city of Matsumoto is laid waste by a flying dragon-like creature that emits deadly lightning bolts. This creature, believed to be extraterrestrial in nature, is bound to be very familiar to Godzilla fans: Ghidorah. Initially one-headed, it eventually transforms into the trademark three-headed monster we all know and love.
The Large-Scale Disaster Response Bureau, which was initially founded following Godzilla’s earlier attack, is headed by Dr. Noda (Hidetaka Yoshioko, returning from the first film). He is aided in his efforts by meteorologist Makoto (Masami Nagasawa) and biologist Dr. Murakami (Min Tanaka), the latter having filmed Ghidorah’s rampage in Matsumoto.
Also encoring from the first film are Ryunosuke Kamiki as Shikishima, the fighter pilot who successfully vanquished Godzilla earlier (or so everyone thought), and his wife Noriko (Minami Hamabe), who miraculously survived Godzilla’s onslaught. They now live quietly with adopted daughter Akiko (Sae Nagatani) but will eventually be drawn into this new conflict.
The initial attempts to vanquish Ghidorah inevitably fail, and soon enough there’s another (very) big problem on the horizon: the inevitable return of Godzilla. With two monsters threatening Japan, the only conceivable solution is to somehow pit the two against each other and hope the one that survives will be so injured that it will succumb to conventional weaponry.
Dr. Murakami is enthusiastic about this possible solution, but having lost his family at Hiroshima, his motives may not be altogether altruistic. As in the first film, “Godzilla Minus Zero” uses the themes of despair and grief, based on Japan’s actual post-war trauma, to add emotional heft to the human characters.
This time, however, Yamazaki devotes a bit too much time to those themes, which occasionally become repetitive. Not unlike Steven Spielberg’s recent “Disclosure Day,” the first act feels a little padded and heavy on exposition. It’s almost an hour before Godzilla really makes his re-entrance, albeit in a customarily dazzling sequence.
Once the two titans square off, however, “Godzilla Minus Zero” soars beautifully and never looks back. In addition to his obvious filmmaking talents, Yamazaki has a keen understanding of the Godzilla mythos. He hasn’t reinvented it so much as carefully retooled it to place it into a historical context, and in ways that are respectful to the series and enormously entertaining to the audience.
Once again, the visual effects are outstanding, and another Academy Award (or at least a nomination) may well be in the offing. This is the first R-rated Godzilla movie, and some of the violence — inflicted on both humans and monsters — is rough stuff, but it’s undeniably effective. Yamazaki has also added a few new wrinkles to the mythos this time around, which are best not divulged here.
Additionally, composer Naoki Sato contributes a pounding score full of dread and foreboding, and when he strikes up Akira Ifukube’s iconic Godzilla film, it certainly takes this film critic back to his childhood, spending countless hours watching the old films on television.
But “Godzilla Minus Zero” is not just a throwback to youthful nostalgia. Despite the period setting (1949), it feels fresh and new, a true and genuine Godzilla movie for the 21
st
 century. It doesn’t quite reach the heights of “Godzilla Minus One,” but easily comes close enough to rank it among the best films of the year.
(In Japanese with English subtitles)

Native candidates notch significant victories in state legislature elections&nbsp;&nbsp;ICT News

[page text]
Amelia Schafer
ICT

Results are trickling nationwide where Indigenous people are competing to join state legislatures in 24 different states, some of whom would be their state’s first ever Indigenous legislator. 

As of Nov. 6 over 83 percent of Indigenous candidates in declared state legislature races have won. 

Among the winners, history is already being made. 

In Vermont, Alanna Ojibway, Sault St. Marie Band of Chippewa and descendant of the United Houma Nation, is believed to be the first-ever Indigenous person elected to the Vermont General Assembly. 

A spokesperson for the Vermont General Assembly said the state does not collect race specific data on legislators, and was therefore unable to confirm if there have been any previous Indigenous legislators.

 

 

Ojibway won her race to represent Vermont Windsor 6 alongside competitor James Coleman – there were two open seats and only two candidates. 

Races had been called for 86 of the 95 Indigenous people running for state legislature positions across 24 different states as of Nov. 6. 

More than 100 Native candidates vying for statewide offices, state legislative seats

Of the 86 declared races, 67 have won, meaning there have only been 19  losses, including one candidate who withdrew from their race. Eight races in Alaska will remain undeclared until ranked-choice voting counts conclude on Nov. 18. 

Nationwide, 95 Indigenous candidates were on the ballot for state legislature seats in 24 states. These 95 candidates represent over 122 different tribal nations and include 75 Democrats, 12 Republicans, seven Independents/nonpartisan candidates and one Green Party candidate. 

 

 

Of the candidates, 53 were cisgender women, 38 were cisgender men and three identified as non-binary or Two-Spirit. 

Native candidates saw significant wins in Montana, where 12 of the 13 candidates won their races. With two sitting Indigenous state legislators whose seats are not up for re-election, the state’s American Indian Caucus will have reached a record number of 14. The most in the group’s history. 

Montana is the only state in the country to have the largest Native representation in its state legislature where it led to the creation of the American Indian Caucus. 

In several states, all Indigenous candidates on the ballot won their legislature races: Arkansas, Connecticut, Maine, Minnesota, North Dakota, Oregon and Vermont. 

 

 

Candidates appeared on ballots in Alaska, Arkansas, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawai`i, Iowa, Idaho, Maine, Michigan, Minnesota, Montana, North Dakota, New Mexico, Nevada, Oklahoma, Oregon, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Vermont, Washington and Wyoming.

Significant victories 

Democrats made historic gains in the 2026 midterm elections, taking control of both the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate by historic margins. 

The blue wave also hit Indian Country, where 88 percent of the Indigenous state legislator-elects are Democrats (59 out of 67 total declared winners) – some of whom flipped historically held Republican seats themselves. 

A majority of Indigenous state legislator candidates documented by 
ICT
 and Advance Native Political Leadership identified as Democrats in 2026: 75 of the 95 total candidates.

As of Friday afternoon, seven Republican Indigenous state legislature candidates in declared races had won – meaning just 10 percent of the Indigenous legislator-elects are Republican. 

Of the 95 Indigenous state legislature candidates, only 12 identified as Republicans in 2026. 

In Arkansas, Democrat Jacob Allen, Chickasaw Nation, defeated second term incumbent Republican Rep. Mindy McAlindon by just 197 votes, flipping the seat blue. Allen is the third Native American to be elected to the Arkansas state legislature and will be the only Native person currently serving in the state house. 

Allen is one of 10 Democrats who flipped seats in the Arkansas legislature, helping the party end a long-standing Republican supermajority. 

The wave hit Arizona, where five Indigenous Democrats secured seats in the state legislature, three of whom were incumbents. The party is poised to gain control of all chambers of the Arizona Legislature for the first time in 60 years. 

In other states, like Connecticut and Tennessee, voters re-elected their first and only Indigenous legislators: Republican Rep. Bryan Terry of Tennessee who is Choctaw; and Connecticut Rep. Larry Pemberton Jr., Democrat and Eastern Pequot Tribal Nation, for a second term. 

In Hawai`i, Pa’ele Kiakona, Native Hawaiian, is the first member of the Green Party elected to the Hawaii House of Representatives. Kiakona won his race for Hawaii House of Representatives for District 14. 

Losses 

Iowans had the chance to elect their first ever Indigenous legislator, but Jessica Lopez-Walker, Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska and a Democrat, lost her race against incumbent Rep. Robert Henderson, Republican, by 386 votes. 

In South Carolina, incumbent Rep. Fawn Pedalino, Republican, lost to Democrat Patricia Pringle by 144 votes, just 10 votes outside of the amount needed to trigger a recount in the race. Pedalino, the only Indigenous person currently serving in the South Carolina legislature, is a citizen of the Natchez-Kusso Tribe.

Indigenous candidates saw major losses in South Dakota, where only 3 of the 10 Native candidates won their elections. South Dakota’s District 27 Senate seat, which encompasses the Pine Ridge Reservation, is without Indigenous representation for the first time in at-least a decade, and has flipped to Republican control for the first time in over a decade.

Celestine Stadnick, Oglala Lakota, lost by just 235 votes to Republican candidate Anthony Kathol in the race to replace term limited Oglala Lakota Senator Red Dawn Foster, a Democrat. 

Peri Pourier, Oglala Lakota, who ran as an Independent, gathered 1,013 votes, trailing behind Stadnick and Kathol. Pourier previously served as a state representative for District 27 where she notably switched from the Democratic Party to Republican.

Alaska

Only three races with Indigenous candidates on the ballot in Alaska have been declared, the rest are subject to ranked-choice vote recounts. 

The State of Alaska utilizes ranked choice voting, meaning if no single candidate secures 50 percent plus one of the vote, more rounds of counting continue factoring in voters’ next choices. Results are available for races where one candidate secured over half of the vote. 

Ranked choice results will be made available on Nov. 18, according to the Alaska Division of Elections.

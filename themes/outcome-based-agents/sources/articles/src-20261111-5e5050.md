Stack AI's Max Poff: Enterprise Agents Get Crushed Between the Demo and the Trusted Rollout
Add preferred source
Enterprise AI agents are easy to demo and brutally hard to put into production, argues Stack AI forward-deployed engineer Max Poff. The gap between the afternoon a demo works and the moment real employees trust it — filled by user acceptance testing, messy system integrations, and edge-case iteration — is where deals are won or lost. The article digs into three concrete deployments across a European bank, a private equity firm, and a media agency, showing a risk assessment collapsing from a full afternoon to 10–15 minutes. It maps the shift from simple knowledge lookups to multi-system orchestration, Stack AI's modest "memory file" take on self-improvement rather than autonomous model-building, and the hard turn toward private cloud and on-premises deployment demanded by banks, hedge funds, and defense contractors. The closing sections cover the Asana acquisition as a distribution and system-of-record play, the rise of forward-deployed engineers as "the best job in tech," and a brief humanoid-robot prediction that is a flourish, not a thesis. Investors should read this as a reality check on the agent-hype trade: value is migrating from model capability to governance, observability, and deployment plumbing.

Building an AI agent that works in a demo now takes an afternoon. Getting a large bank to trust it in production takes months. That gap — not model intelligence — is where the enterprise agent business is actually decided, according to Max Poff, a forward-deployed engineer at Stack AI, speaking on MTS alongside Alberto Arrighi, who leads customer success.

Poff's framing is blunt: "You can vibe code anything these days," he said, then points at the real problem — "the edge cases that show up between the time stamp when you show the demo and the time stamp where people trusted it's in production being used regularly."

That sentence is the whole thesis. In late 2026, with every software vendor shipping an "agent," the differentiator is no longer whether a workflow can be built. It's what happens when a real employee opens the tool on a Monday morning and discovers half the data they need is behind a permissions wall.

User acceptance testing is the boring machinery that decides winners

Both guests describe the same production checkpoint: a formal user acceptance testing (UAT) phase staffed by beta testers pulled from the customer's own team. The pattern repeats because it has to. The person who scopes the agent knows which systems it needs; the rest of the team immediately finds the ones that were never connected.

Arrighi's illustration is a media planner agent built for what he calls one of the largest media marketing agencies in Northern Europe. It uses multiple large language models to turn a customer brief into a full media plan — which channels to buy, how to split the budget. The original builder validated parts of its behavior. Opening it to the wider team exposed missing data connections, which then had to be built. "It's very easy to build a demo," Arrighi said, "but then when you open it up to the various testers, they realize that they don't have access to some systems or some things are not working."

Poff's example is a private equity firm's executive assistance team, which codes expenses in SAP Concur. Demo, fast. Beta testers, immediate failures. Then iteration on the workflow until it held. The lesson embedded in both stories: the failure mode is almost never the model thinking badly. It's the plumbing.

A European bank's risk team went from an afternoon to 10–15 minutes

The most concrete production case belongs to a large European bank's risk and compliance group — and it shows why these projects get funded at all. A project manager submits a change proposal as a PDF describing a new business initiative. A risk manager must then read it, work through the bank's sprawling risk register to identify which risks the change introduces, and trawl the controls register for mitigating controls. Poff calls the work important but "very time-consuming and tedious."

Stack AI's agent ingests the proposal and returns an Excel workbook formatted to the bank's exact specification, listing the risks and controls. It's triggered inside Slack: a user mentions a bot the bank built, which routes the request to a Stack AI workflow. The task that ate roughly a whole afternoon now finishes in 10 to 15 minutes.

"Self-improvement" here means a shared memory file, not a model building its successor

Poff draws a deliberate line between the recursive self-improvement (RSI) that dominates social media chatter and what his company actually does. The buzzy version — AI systems autonomously designing the next generation of models — is not his business. "That's not where we play," he said. "But if you stretch the definition, we have our own cute version of RSI."

The cute version is memory. Stack AI doesn't train its own models. Instead, an agent keeps a file of memories shared across every user at a given client, loaded into context on each run, so the system gets more useful as usage and feedback pile up. Arrighi adds a second, simpler mechanism: writing back to the data source. When an agent reads from a knowledge base, it writes back to it — which, in his telling, compounds intelligence over time without any of the science-fiction machinery.

The takeaway for anyone tracking AI capability claims: the "learning" happening in most enterprise deployments today is memory management and feedback loops, not autonomous model improvement. That's a much humbler, and much more investable, story than the frontier-lab headlines suggest.

The use cases have moved from lookup to orchestration

Arrighi joined Stack AI around late 2025. A year earlier, the customer requests were simple — connect to a database, manage some knowledge. By late 2026, he says, they are orchestration systems: pulling data from multiple places, triggered by events, acting on the environment while simultaneously gathering information across silos. The scoping conversation now starts with connecting to every system the agent must touch, because without that the agent produces nothing of value.

Poff flags two capabilities that have crossed a threshold. First, heavily formatted Excel workbooks — long the excuse to say AI wasn't the right tool — are now handled well, with agents generating intricate outputs often hosted in SharePoint. Second, more agents run in the background maintaining a database while end users interact in plain conversation.

That second shift powers Poff's most opinionated claim of the episode. "I think that dashboards kind of have a foot in the grave and dashboards just are not that useful anymore because it captures static insights that people think will be useful to users," he said. His argument: dashboards freeze assumptions about what users want, and users still have to slice and dice them — often without getting the answer. A conversational layer over an agent-maintained database hands the user the data directly.

Regulated buyers want the platform in their own cloud — some want it on a USB drive

The deployment conversation has changed harder than the product conversation. Arrighi names on-premises and private-cloud installation as a major trend, especially among regulated industries. Some of the top US financial firms want Stack AI running in their own cloud or their own data centers. Alongside that sits governance: as agents touch more data and make more consequential decisions, customers demand observability and control over what the agent does.

Poff describes the shift from the inside. When he started, nearly every client lived in Stack AI's multi-tenant SaaS instance, with a new organization spun up per contract. Now regulated industries want agents but cannot ship all their data to a vendor's infrastructure. The company's DevOps engineers made private-cloud and on-premises deployments seamless, and the range of customers is striking: banks, hedge funds, and defense contractors that run the system from a USB drive.

Asana buys distribution and a system of record; Stack AI brings the agent layer

The Asana combination gives Stack AI two things it lacked as a startup. Arrighi's version: reach plus a new integration surface. Asana is a system of record holding what he calls tribal knowledge about a company, which can both trigger agents and feed them. A user can post a task in an Asana project and kick off a full agent run without ever opening a chat window; agents can pull project and campaign data through an MCP connection, or use tools like a terminal to build a dashboard while fetching the latest team data from Asana.

Poff frames it from engineering: more work, more clients. Stack AI had a strong sales team but limited distribution as a startup; Asana opens a large slice of the Fortune 500 through accounts that already exist. He credits Stack AI's founding engineers with the conference-floor evangelism that built early demand.

The forward-deployed engineer is the feedback loop that makes the product better

Poff calls forward-deployed engineering "the best job in tech right now." The role splits between serious software engineering and direct work inside enterprises deploying agents, and its real value is the loop it creates. He borrows the framing from Palantir: a good forward-deployed engineer "will metabolize pain that they experience from their clients and then excrete product."

Arrighi's team is built from AI strategists and forward-deployed engineers — he started as a strategist himself. His days go to customer conversations: finding where an agent is worth deploying, mapping pain points and strategy, then writing the specs that become an agentic workflow. He works hand-in-glove with the engineers, Poff especially.

The humanoid robot line is a closing flourish, not a thesis

Asked what will be true in a year that isn't true today, Arrighi's answer is maximalist: Stack AI will be in 99% of companies around the world. Poff's answer supplies the episode's title — he'd love to see a Stack AI workflow operating a humanoid robot, some form of embodied intelligence, and thinks there might be "some inkling" of it within a year. He anchors it to a line he attributes to an NVIDIA engineer: our generation was born too late to explore the Earth and too early to explore the stars, but right on time to solve humanoid robotics. The host notes how fast innovation timelines can compress. The robotics thread occupies well under a minute of the conversation. Treat it as a flourish.

The open question the episode raises without answering is whether one platform can straddle two very different futures. On one side, enterprise demand is getting heavier and more regulated — private clouds, on-premises installs, defense contractors, governance dashboards — where compliance matters more than raw model capability. On the other, Asana points toward broad, SaaS-style distribution across mainstream Fortune 500 accounts. Serving a USB-drive deployment at a defense contractor and a task-triggered agent inside an ordinary Asana customer with the same product is not obviously possible, and the guests don't claim it is. Nor are their quality claims — Excel workbooks "crushed," dashboards in decline — benchmarked results; they are assertions drawn from customer work. For investors, the useful signal is where the value is pooling: not in the model, which everyone rents, but in the unglamorous layer of testing, integration, deployment plumbing, and trust. That layer is slow to build and hard to dislodge — which is exactly why the agent trade may prove more durable than its demo reel.

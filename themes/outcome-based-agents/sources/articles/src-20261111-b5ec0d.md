Abstract
Eukaryotic genomes are exposed to numerous sources of DNA damage that impede replication. Only a decade ago, repriming by the primase–polymerase PRIMPOL was identified as a pathway that enables cells to tolerate DNA damage
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
. However, the molecular mechanism that underlies repriming remains poorly understood, and it is unclear how PRIMPOL is precisely targeted to stalled forks, where along single-stranded DNA PRIMPOL binds and how PRIMPOL is regulated to minimize error-prone DNA synthesis. Here, to dissect the molecular basis of repriming, we perform real-time single-molecule imaging in nuclear extracts that recapitulate genome maintenance. We directly visualize how stalled forks restart through repriming. We show that PRIMPOL is recruited to stalled forks through an interaction with the replicative helicase CDC45–MCM2–7–GINS (CMG). This facilitates primer handover from PRIMPOL to the high-fidelity polymerase ε, minimizing mutagenic DNA synthesis. At near-physiological concentrations, PRIMPOL reprimes leading strands in a CMG-dependent manner and polymerase ε extends those primers. This primary mode of repriming does not require DNA damage checkpoint signalling. PRIMPOL overexpression and checkpoint activation promote a secondary mode of repriming, whereby PRIMPOL restarts DNA synthesis far from CMG and polymerase δ extends those primers. Our data reveal a simple yet effective mechanism that directs PRIMPOL’s activity to stalled forks awaiting restart, and suggest a strategy to inhibit the repriming pathway.

The replicative helicase CMG unwinds parental DNA
23
 and is functionally coupled to polymerase ε (Pol ε)
24
, which synthesizes the leading strand. The lagging strand is primed and extended by primase–polymerase α (Pol α) and polymerase δ (Pol δ), respectively
24

25
,
26

25
,
26
,
27

28

2

7
,
16
,
29

Although repriming is a major RS response pathway and PRIMPOL is an attractive therapeutic target, key mechanistic aspects of repriming remain unclear. For example, although RPA recruits PRIMPOL to ssDNA
30
,
31
, it is unclear why PRIMPOL acts at stalled forks, but not on ssDNA generated by other processes (transcription, recombination). One study reported that high RPA concentrations inhibit PRIMPOL’s activity on ssDNA in vitro
30
, presumably because RPA fully coats ssDNA and prevents PRIMPOL’s access to the template. Another study found that RPA stimulated PRIMPOL’s activity on ssDNA long enough to accommodate the simultaneous binding of both proteins, that Pol ε can extend PRIMPOL’s primers in vitro and that RPA stimulates PRIMPOL’s enzymatic activity in synergy with Pol ε
31
. It remains unclear how repriming occurs in the nucleus, where RPA is abundant and rapidly coats exposed ssDNA. Although PRIMPOL is a known target of checkpoint kinase 1 (CHK1)
22
, how the DNA damage checkpoint regulates repriming remains poorly understood. Finally, it is unclear how PRIMPOL is regulated to minimize mutagenic DNA synthesis during repriming.
To address these questions, we used single-molecule imaging to directly visualize PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart in 
Xenopus
 egg extracts. We found that PRIMPOL preferentially binds near stalled forks, suggesting that it interacts with fork-associated proteins. We identified an interaction between PRIMPOL and CMG that is required for targeting PRIMPOL to stalled forks and for efficient fork restart in nuclear extract and in human cells. Our data indicate that the PRIMPOL–CMG interaction facilitates primer handover from PRIMPOL to the leading strand polymerase Pol ε, therefore minimizing mutagenic DNA synthesis. Finally, in conditions that mimic PRIMPOL overexpression, we found that PRIMPOL can reprime DNA synthesis far from the replicative helicase, and those primers are extended by Pol δ. Our findings support a simple-yet-elegant mechanism that targets PRIMPOL’s activity specifically to stalled forks and suggest a strategy to inhibit repriming.
Direct observation of PRIMPOL-mediated fork restart
To investigate the mechanism of PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart, we used nuclear extract prepared from 
Xenopus laevis
 eggs
32
. This extract contains all soluble nuclear proteins, supports efficient DNA replication and recapitulates several RS response pathways
33
. We could not detect frog PRIMPOL in egg extract using mass spectrometry (MS) or western blotting (Extended Data Fig. 
1a,b
 and Supplementary Table 
1
). To reconstitute repriming, we supplemented nuclear extract with recombinant human PRIMPOL, which displayed robust enzymatic activity in vitro (Extended Data Fig. 
1c,d
).
To visualize PRIMPOL-mediated fork restart in real-time, we adapted a single-molecule assay called kinetics of eukaryotic replication via molecular imaging and tracking (KERMIT)
25
,
34
. A biotinylated linear DNA template was double-tethered onto a streptavidin-coated coverslip in a microfluidic flow-cell (Fig. 
1a
). DNA was incubated with high-speed supernatant—an extract that supports origin licensing but not origin firing, mimicking G1 (ref. 
32
) (Fig. 
1a
). The flow-cell was washed with buffer to remove any soluble proteins while retaining DNA-bound MCM2–7. Licensed DNA was incubated with nucleoplasmic extract—an extract that supports origin firing, elongation and RS response, mimicking S phase
32
 (Fig. 
1a
). Replication was monitored in real-time using total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy.

To visualize CMG, we used GINS-depleted extract supplemented with fluorescently labelled GINS (GINS–LD655). To monitor DNA synthesis, we supplemented extract with FEN1–KikG (flap endonuclease 1 fused to the fluorescent protein KikG)
35
. FEN1 is recruited to chromatin through its interaction with PCNA
36
, and was previously validated as a marker of nascent DNA
37
, which does not require FEN1’s enzymatic activity
37
.
In mock-depleted extract, origin firing generated two diverging CMGs travelling at around 300 nucleotides per min with FEN1–KikG signal accumulating behind each helicase (Fig. 
1b
). To induce RS, the extract was supplemented with 15 µM aphidicolin (APH)—an inhibitor of B-family polymerases (including α, ε and δ) that does not inhibit PRIMPOL (Extended Data Fig. 
1d
). In APH-treated extract, CMG travelled around 10× slower than in mock without any accompanying FEN1–KikG signal, indicating a failure to synthesize DNA (Fig. 
1c
). This substantial CMG slowing during RS (reported by us
25
 and others
38
) is a hallmark of CMG uncoupling
28
 (Fig. 
1b,c
). Adding 30 nM of wild-type (WT) PRIMPOL to APH-treated extract did not support fork restart (Extended Data Fig. 
1e
), reflecting the fact that APH inhibits Pol ε and δ and prevents them from extending PRIMPOL’s primers.
To recapitulate RS without inhibiting Pol ε/δ, we devised two independent strategies. First, we immunodepleted Pol α from nuclear extract (depleting or inhibiting Pol α was previously used to induce RS in cells
22
,
39
,
40
,
41
). In Pol-α-depleted extract, CMGs travelled very slowly (around 30 nucleotides per min) without any accompanying FEN1 accumulation (Fig. 
1d
). This is indicative of helicase uncoupling and is consistent with reports that Pol α is required for establishing leading strand synthesis
42
,
43
. When Pol-α-depleted extract was supplemented with recombinant WT PRIMPOL, we routinely observed that slow/uncoupled CMGs suddenly sped up to around 300 nucleotides per min (Fig. 
1e
 (white arrows)). We interpret this switch to fast CMG progression as fork restart. Around 75% of stalled forks restarted in Pol-α-depleted extract supplemented with 30 nM WT PRIMPOL, versus only around 5% restart efficiency in reactions supplemented with buffer (Fig. 
1f
 and Extended Data Fig. 
1f
). Importantly, the sudden switch from slow to fast CMG progression was accompanied by FEN1–KikG accumulation behind the fork, indicative of DNA synthesis (Fig. 
1e
). To corroborate the idea that fast CMG progression corresponds to DNA synthesis, we repeated these experiments with fluorescently labelled PCNA (PCNA–LD655)—an established marker of nascent DNA
44
,
45
. Like FEN1–KikG, PCNA–LD655 accumulated behind restarted forks (Extended Data Fig. 
1g
). Disrupting FEN1’s interaction with PCNA eliminated FEN1–KikG binding (Extended Data Fig. 
1h
) without impacting fork restart (Extended Data Fig. 
1i
). We conclude that FEN1–KikG is recruited to nascent DNA by PCNA, and that the sudden speed-up of CMG represents fork restart. We subsequently used FEN1–KikG as a DNA synthesis marker because it provided better signal-to-noise than PCNA–LD655 (
Methods
).
Second, we designed a DNA substrate with site-specific DNA lesions on the leading strand template, each consisting of a small (around 4 amino acids) peptide adduct (Extended Data Fig. 
2a
). This lesion should easily fit through CMG’s central channel
46
, and should induce helicase uncoupling. After encountering the lesion, CMG instantly slowed down from around 300 nucleotides per min to 30 nucleotides per min (Fig. 
1g,h
), indicative of uncoupling. In the absence of PRIMPOL, forks stalled at the lesion restarted very inefficiently (around 7%) (Fig. 
1f
 and Extended Data Fig. 
1f
), probably through TLS as shown previously
47
,
48
. In extract containing 30 nM of WT PRIMPOL, around 50% of forks stalled at the lesion restarted (Fig. 
1i,f
), causing CMG to speed up from around 30 nucleotides per min to 300 nucleotides per min—matching the speed of unperturbed forks (Fig. 
1h
 and Extended Data Fig. 
2b
). Importantly, lesion-stalled forks restarted in a PRIMPOL-dependent manner in extract containing endogenous levels of Pol α.
We conclude that recombinant human WT PRIMPOL supports robust fork restart in our single-molecule assay using two orthogonal sources of RS (Fig. 
1f
). We subsequently used the Pol α depletion method because it provided a higher throughput, and was easily adapted for ensemble assays (see below).
Monitoring PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart
To establish an ensemble assay for PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart, we replicated a 3 kb plasmid in Pol-α-depleted extract, which strongly suppressed DNA synthesis in comparison to the mock-depleted extract (Fig. 
1j
 (compare i and ii)). The low levels of DNA synthesis in Pol-α-depleted extract (Fig. 
1j
 (ii)) are probably caused by trace amounts of Pol α that evaded the immunodepletion (Extended Data Fig. 
2c
).
Supplementing Pol-α-depleted extract with 3–30 nM of WT PRIMPOL led to a significant increase in DNA synthesis, in a PRIMPOL-concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 
1j,k
). As human cells contain around 17 nM of PRIMPOL
49
, the concentrations used here are near physiological. Notably, 30 nM of WT PRIMPOL did not fully rescue DNA synthesis (Fig. 
1j
 (v versus i)). We attribute this to the fact that depleting Pol α activates the DNA damage checkpoint (Extended Data Fig. 
2c
), which partially inhibits origin firing and results in fewer forks (Extended Data Fig. 
2d
 (CDC45 blot)). We revisit CHK1’s role in Fig. 
4
.
To verify that PRIMPOL is responsible for the partial rescue of DNA synthesis in the biochemical assay, we tested two previously characterized mutants: (1) PRIMPOL D114A/E116A (hereafter, PRIMPOL(AxA)), which lacks primase and polymerase activity
2
,
3
; and (2) PRIMPOL D519R/F522A/D551R/I554A (hereafter, PRIMPOL(ΔRPA)), which cannot interact with RPA
30
 (Extended Data Fig. 
1c
). The in vitro primase–polymerase assay showed that PRIMPOL(ΔRPA) (but not PRIMPOL(AxA)) exhibits catalytic activity (Extended Data Fig. 
1d
). Chromatin fractionation experiments indicate that WT PRIMPOL and PRIMPOL(AxA) (but not PRIMPOL(ΔRPA)) are recruited to DNA in Pol-α-depleted extract (Extended Data Fig. 
2d
) and in APH-treated extract (Extended Data Fig. 
2e
). In contrast to WT PRIMPOL, neither PRIMPOL(AxA) nor PRIMPOL(ΔRPA) rescued DNA synthesis in Pol-α-depleted extract (Fig. 
1j
 (v–vii)), corroborating reports that repriming requires both PRIMPOL’s enzymatic activity
1
,
4
 and its interaction with RPA
30
,
31
. These data indicate that Pol-α-depleted extract recapitulates PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart, providing a powerful model system to dissect the molecular mechanism of repriming.
Long-lived PRIMPOL binding near stalled forks
It was previously proposed that RPA recruits PRIMPOL to stalled forks
4
,
30
. As uncoupled CMGs generate long tracts of RPA-coated ssDNA, it remained unclear where exactly PRIMPOL binds along ssDNA. To address this question, we visualized AlexaFluor647-labelled WT PRIMPOL (PRIMPOL(WT)–AF647) in Pol-α-depleted extract supplemented with FEN1–KikG, and RPA fused to mCherry-XL
50
,
51
 (hereafter, RPA–mCherryXL) (Extended Data Fig. 
3a
). This enabled us to simultaneously monitor PRIMPOL binding and dissociation, DNA synthesis and the extent of RPA–ssDNA. Importantly, fluorescent labelling did not impair PRIMPOL’s fork restart activity (Extended Data Fig. 
3b,c
).
At 30 nM of PRIMPOL(WT)–AF647, there were too many PRIMPOL binding events on RPA–ssDNA, making it impossible to resolve individual molecules (Extended Data Fig. 
3d
). To distinguish each PRIMPOL binding event, we visualized 3 nM PRIMPOL(WT)–AF647 at 15 s per frame (Fig. 
2a
). Initially, RPA–ssDNA tracts grew bidirectionally very slowly (Fig. 
2a
 and Extended Data Fig. 
3e
)—consistent with uncoupled helicases (Fig. 
1h
). After PRIMPOL-mediated fork restart, RPA–ssDNA tracts grew rapidly, but the RPA signal intensity decreased around twofold (Fig. 
2a
 (bottom)). This suggests that PRIMPOL reprimes synthesis only on one strand (the leading strand, as shown in Fig. 
4
).

We detected numerous short-lived PRIMPOL(WT)–AF647 binding events far from replication forks (Fig. 
2a
 (orange triangles)), which we call fork-distal. Most fork-distal PRIMPOL binding events (~84%) lasted one frame (15 s) or less (Fig. 
2c
) and were randomly distributed along RPA–ssDNA. We also detected fork-proximal PRIMPOL binding events close to the fork (within 1 pixel = 160 nm), which tended to be longer than fork-distal binding events (Fig. 
2c
). Notably, long-lived fork-proximal PRIMPOL(WT)–AF647 binding (Fig. 
2a,b
 (curly brackets)) preceded most (~70 ± 10%) fork restart events (Fig. 
2a,b
 (white arrows)).
To gain further insights, we quantified the frequency of fork-proximal (Fig. 
2d
) and fork-distal (Fig. 
2e
) binding events in Pol-α-depleted extract supplemented with 3 nM of fluorescently labelled WT PRIMPOL, PRIMPOL(AxA) or PRIMPOL(ΔRPA). As expected, PRIMPOL(ΔRPA) exhibited very little fork-proximal or fork-distal binding in Pol-α-depleted extract, indistinguishable from the negative control, for which we measured the frequency of non-specific binding using free AF647 dye (Fig. 
2d,e
 and Extended Data Fig. 
3f
). Both WT PRIMPOL and PRIMPOL(AxA) exhibited high levels of fork-proximal and fork-distal binding in Pol-α-depleted extract, consistent with chromatin fractionation experiments (Extended Data Fig. 
2d,e
). Notably, PRIMPOL(AxA) exhibited the most fork-proximal binding (Fig. 
2d
), probably because it can repeatedly bind to stalled forks without restarting them (Extended Data Fig. 
3g
). By contrast, WT PRIMPOL promotes fork restart, after which it no longer binds to the unstressed fork (the mock-depleted control is shown in Fig. 
2d
 and Extended Data Fig. 
3h
). Notably, there was no statistical difference between binding event durations for WT PRIMPOL and PRIMPOL(AxA) (Fig. 
2f
), even though only WT PRIMPOL supported fork restart (Extended Data Fig. 
2i
).
We also observed long-lived fork-proximal WT PRIMPOL binding events before fork restart on the template with a site-specific lesion (Extended Data Fig. 
3j
). These productive WT PRIMPOL binding events lasted around 3 min, similar to those measured in Pol-α-depleted extract (Extended Data Fig. 
3j
). Notably, there was a delay of 3–5 min between the dissociation of PRIMPOL(WT)–AF647 from the fork, and fork restart (Extended Data Fig. 
3j
). This delay probably reflects the time needed for a replicative polymerase to capture the primer synthesized by PRIMPOL, and for the reassembly of an active replisome.
Occasionally, we observed PRIMPOL binding in consecutive movie frames at nearby positions on DNA (Fig. 
2b
 (light blue ovals)). This could represent a PRIMPOL molecule diffusing along ssDNA, or may represent different molecules binding at slightly different positions along ssDNA. To distinguish between these scenarios, we repeated the experiment at 3 s per frame (Fig. 
2g
, five times faster than in Fig. 
2a–f
). At this higher frame rate, we frequently observed different PRIMPOL molecules briefly binding to ssDNA in non-consecutive video frames (Fig. 
2g
 (white ovals)). Such binding sequences could be misinterpreted as diffusion of a single PRIMPOL molecule if imaged at lower framerates. This experiment also revealed that the vast majority of PRIMPOL binding events are very short-lived, lasting 3 s or less (Fig. 
2h
). We still observed long-lived fork-proximal binding events that preceded fork restart (Fig. 
2g
 (white curly brackets)). In summary, although we cannot rule out PRIMPOL diffusion across short distances, our data argue against long-range PRIMPOL diffusion on ssDNA.
Recruitment to stalled forks via interaction with CMG
Although PRIMPOL can transiently bind anywhere along ssDNA–RPA (Fig. 
2a,b
), the vast majority (over 99%) of binding events are non-productive—that is, they do not lead to fork restart. Productive PRIMPOL binding events occur predominantly near stalled forks and tend to be long-lived (~3 min; Extended Data Fig. 
3j
), raising the question of why fork-proximal binding events are longer. We hypothesized that PRIMPOL’s binding at the fork may be stabilized by yet unknown interactions with fork-associated proteins. Notably, AlphaFold3 predicted that several replisome subunits may interact with PRIMPOL
52
. By cross-referencing AlphaFold predictions for human and frog proteins, we singled out PRIMPOL’s putative interaction with MCM4—a CMG subunit (Fig. 
3a
). Positively charged residues in human PRIMPOL (Arg29, Arg31, Lys34) are predicted to form salt bridges with MCM4 residues (Fig. 
3a
 and Extended Data Fig. 
4a,b
). To test this prediction, we generated three mutants: PRIMPOL R29A/R31A/K34A (hereafter, PRIMPOL(3A)), PRIMPOL R29D/R31E/K34D (hereafter, PRIMPOL(DED)) and PRIMPOL(29–35:Linker) (in which residues 29–35 were replaced with the linker GGGSGGGS) (Fig. 
3b
 and Extended Data Fig. 
4c,d
).

Second, we co-immunoprecipitated MCM2–7 from extract using PRIMPOL residues 1–60 as bait. We compared the WT bait versus the 3A, DED and 29-35:linker mutants (Extended Data Fig.

Finally, we examined whether the PRIMPOL–MCM4 interaction is important for fork restart in human cells, using an established DNA fibre assay

3h

4g

PRIMPOL also supports CMG-independent restart
We found that WT PRIMPOL promotes efficient fork restart at concentrations as low as 3 nM (Fig.

Notably, at higher concentrations of WT PRIMPOL (>10 nM), we observed a distinct subpopulation of restart events that occurred far behind the fork (Fig. 
4a
 (white asterisks)). The frequency of these fork-distal restart events increased proportionally to WT PRIMPOL concentration (Fig. 
4b
 (grey bars)), indicating that they also represent repriming.

Pol ε is required for CMG-dependent fork restart
The PRIMPOL–CMG interaction raises the possibility that PRIMPOL’s primer could be handed directly to the leading strand polymerase Pol ε, which is physically tethered to CMG
54
,
55
,
56
 in a manner that should not prevent PRIMPOL from binding to MCM4 (Fig. 
3a
). If so, Pol ε could capture PRIMPOL’s primer and extend it to restart leading strand synthesis. Alternatively, PRIMPOL’s primer could be extended by Pol δ first, and then handed to Pol ε, reminiscent of how leading strand synthesis is established during replication initiation
42
,
57

To test these hypotheses, we performed experiments in extract depleted of Pol α and Pol δ, or in extract depleted of Pol α and Pol ε (Fig. 
4d
). Depleting Pol δ caused only a minor reduction in CMG-dependent fork restart efficiency, whereas depleting Pol ε ablated CMG-dependent restart events (Fig. 
4d
 (green bars) and Extended Data Fig. 
6a–c
). Importantly, CMG-dependent fork restart was fully rescued by adding 100 nM (Pol ε concentration in human cells
49
) of recombinant Pol ε to Pol-ε-depleted extract (Fig. 
4d
 and Extended Data Fig. 
6d
). These data support the hypothesis that CMG-dependent restart entails the primer handover from PRIMPOL to Pol ε, which subsequently performs long-range DNA synthesis.
Notably, depleting Pol δ (but not Pol ε) eliminated CMG-independent restart events (Fig. 
4d
 (grey bars) and Extended Data Fig. 
6a
). Supplementing Pol-δ-depleted extract with 400 nM (Pol δ concentration in human cells)
49
 of recombinant Pol δ fully rescued CMG-independent restart (Fig. 
4d
 and Extended Data Fig. 
6e,f
). These findings suggest that CMG-independent restart entails the primer handover from PRIMPOL to Pol δ, which subsequently performs long-range DNA synthesis.
We conclude that PRIMPOL primarily acts as a leading-strand primase by synthesizing primers immediately behind CMG, and those primers are extended by Pol ε. However, at higher concentrations, PRIMPOL can also reprime DNA synthesis in a CMG-independent manner, and those primers are extended by Pol δ. Our observation that DNA synthesis proceeds faster during CMG-independent restart (Fig.

CHK1 activation is not required for repriming
It was previously reported that CHK1 phosphorylates human PRIMPOL at Ser255, which promotes more repriming and RS tolerance in human cells
22
. The same study proposed that a disordered loop in human PRIMPOL (residues 201–260) autoinhibits repriming, but that Ser255 phosphorylation by CHK1 relieves this autoinhibition
22
. We examined whether CHK1 modulates PRIMPOL’s activity in our biochemical fork restart assay. We supplemented Pol-α-depleted extract with an ATR inhibitor (AZD6738) that blocked CHK1 phosphorylation (Extended Data Fig. 
7a
 (lane iii versus iv)). Notably, inhibiting ATR in Pol-α-depleted extract enhanced the amount of PRIMPOL-dependent DNA synthesis (Extended Data Fig. 
7b
 (v versus vi) and 
7c
). This indicates that checkpoint activation is not required for PRIMPOL to restart stalled forks. This finding also explains the low levels of DNA synthesis reported in Fig. 
1j
: depleting Pol α activates the DNA damage checkpoint, which suppresses origin firing, resulting in fewer replication forks (Extended Data Fig. 
2d
 (CDC45 immunoblot)). Thus, although PRIMPOL can restart most stalled forks in Pol-α-depleted extract, the overall amount of DNA synthesis is expected to be lower than in the mock control (Fig. 
1j
 (i versus v)).
Immunoblotting of phosphorylated CHK1 in Pol-α-depleted extract collected from the flow cell revealed that the checkpoint is not active in our single-molecule experiments. This is because the DNA template concentration in the flow-cell is very low (10
−3
−10
−2 
ng μl
−1
) while checkpoint activation requires at least around 1 ng μl
−1
 (ref. 
58
) of DNA template. The fact that we observe robust PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart in the single-molecule assay (Fig. 
1f
) in the absence of CHK1 activation (Extended Data Fig. 
7a
 (flow-cell lane)) supports our conclusion that checkpoint activation is not required for repriming.
CHK1 upregulates CMG-independent repriming
Given the report that CHK1 stimulates PRIMPOL activity in human cells
22
, we hypothesized that CHK1 may regulate another aspect of PRIMPOL’s function. To test this idea, we performed single-molecule fork restart experiments with four previously characterized PRIMPOL phospho-mutants
22
 (Extended Data Fig. 
7d,e
): phospho-dead PRIMPOL(S255A), phosphomimetic PRIMPOL(S255E) and PRIMPOL(S255D), and a mutant that phenocopies the phosphomimetic mutants—PRIMPOL(Δ201–260) (in which residues 201–260 were replaced by a flexible linker). At a near-physiological concentration (30 nM), all four mutants supported similar levels (80–90%) of CMG-dependent fork restart to WT PRIMPOL (Fig. 
4e
 (green bars) and Extended Data Fig. 
7f
). However, the mutants supported different levels of CMG-independent restart (Fig. 
4e
 (grey bars)): PRIMPOL WT and PRIMPOL(S255A) exhibited the lowest efficiency (~25%); PRIMPOL(S255E) and PRIMPOL(S255D) supported intermediate efficiency (~40%); while PRIMPOL(Δ201–260) displayed the highest efficiency (~70%).
We further examined PRIMPOL(Δ201–260) across a wide range of concentrations (Fig. 
4f
). At near-physiological concentrations (10 nM and 30 nM), PRIMPOL(Δ201–260) supported significantly more CMG-independent restart than WT PRIMPOL (Fig. 
4f
 (grey bars) and Extended Data Fig. 
7g
). We conclude that CHK1 phosphorylation of Ser255 does not affect PRIMPOL’s primary mode of repriming (CMG dependent), but significantly enhances PRIMPOL’s secondary repriming mode (CMG independent). Consistent with our conclusion, the stimulatory effect of CHK1 phosphorylation on PRIMPOL’s activity was previously observed in cells treated with a Pol α inhibitor
22
—which exposes large tracts of ssDNA behind the fork, generating the ideal substrate for CMG-independent repriming.
Discussion
Our experiments revealed several mechanistic insights into PRIMPOL function, dynamics and regulation, supporting a new model of PRIMPOL-mediated fork restart (Fig. 
5
). First, PRIMPOL can briefly bind anywhere along RPA–ssDNA (Fig. 
5a–c
), but such binding events are very short-lived (Fig. 
5c
) and rarely cause fork restart. Second, PRIMPOL can reside on RPA–ssDNA much longer near uncoupled CMGs (Fig. 
5d–f
), and such long-lived binding events frequently lead to fork restart. Third, the PRIMPOL–MCM4 interaction is required for efficient fork restart (Fig. 
5e
) in nuclear extract and in human cells. Finally, our data indicate that, at near-physiological concentrations, PRIMPOL primarily acts as a leading-strand primase, and that PRIMPOL’s primers are directly handed to Pol ε (Fig. 
5f–h
). Notably, PRIMPOL-driven restart can also occur far behind stalled forks (Fig. 
5i–l
), but only at high PRIMPOL concentrations (mimicking overexpression), or after ATR/CHK1 activation. It is tempting to speculate that chemotherapy-induced PRIMPOL overexpression observed in cancer cells
14
,
15
 may unlock this alternative CMG-independent restart mechanism. As PRIMPOL cannot prime on ssDNA fully coated with RPA
30
, CMG-independent repriming events may occur only when a RPA molecule transiently dissociates from ssDNA in PRIMPOL’s immediate vicinity (Fig. 
5i,j
). Notably, RPA can spontaneously dissociate from ssDNA in vitro
59
, albeit slowly (
k
off
 = 0.006 s
−1
).

We show that efficient repriming requires both PRIMPOL–RPA and PRIMPOL–MCM4 interactions. As RPA binds to PRIMPOL’s C terminus
30
 and MCM4 binds to PRIMPOL’s N terminus (Fig. 
3
), we propose that PRIMPOL simultaneously interacts with both RPA and MCM4 during repriming (Fig. 
5e
). This model provides simple-yet-elegant answers to three long-standing questions. First, PRIMPOL can interact with both RPA and MCM4 only behind uncoupled CMGs, explaining how PRIMPOL is targeted specifically to stalled forks (Fig. 
5e
). Second, the simultaneous interaction with both RPA and MCM4 positions PRIMPOL near CMG’s exit channel (Fig. 
3a
). This enables PRIMPOL to engage the leading-strand template before it is coated by RPA (Fig. 
5f
), explaining how repriming can occur in the nucleus where RPA is very abundant. Third, PRIMPOL’s binding to CMG facilitates rapid primer handover to Pol ε (Fig. 
5g
), minimizing error-prone DNA synthesis during repriming. It is unclear what causes PRIMPOL’s dissociation after primer synthesis but before fork restart (Fig. 
2b
). It is possible that PRIMPOL disengages RPA once the primer becomes too long (Fig. 
5f
), which could promote PRIMPOL’s dissociation from DNA. Alternatively, PRIMPOL may transiently dissociate from MCM4 while CMG recruits replication factors that prevent PRIMPOL from re-binding the helicase.
Notably, we observed a negligible amount of PRIMPOL binding to unperturbed forks (mock in Fig. 
2d,e
 and Extended Data Fig. 
3h
). This could be because, during normal replication, there is no RPA-coated ssDNA immediately behind CMG (Fig. 
5a
). Alternatively, during unperturbed replication, PRIMPOL’s docking site on CMG may be sterically occluded by a replication elongation factor. For example, CTF18 binds to the C terminus of MCM7 (ref. 
45
) (next to MCM4), which may prevent PRIMPOL binding to CMG during replication elongation.
During the preparation of this manuscript, we became aware of a similar study
60
 from the Yeeles group (MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, Cambridge, UK), which reconstituted PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart using purified human proteins and obtained a cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of the CMG–PRIMPOL complex that is highly similar to the AlphaFold prediction (personal communication).
There are a few notable similarities between PRIMPOL’s role in the nucleus and mitochondria. First, PRIMPOL interacts with the mitochondrial replicative helicase Twinkle
61
 and the ssDNA-binding protein mtSSB
9
, reminiscent of its interactions with CMG and RPA. Notably, PRIMPOL also interacts with the mitochondrial DNA polymerase γ
61
, but there is no evidence of PRIMPOL interacting with Pol ε/δ. Second, PRIMPOL is required to restart replication after mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) damage
61
, similar to its role in restarting forks in the nucleus. Notably, Twinkle can stimulate PRIMPOL’s enzymatic activity in vitro
62
, but it remains unclear how this regulates repriming in mitochondria. Third, PRIMPOL overexpression leads to increased levels of lagging strand priming during mtDNA replication, reminiscent of CMG-independent restart events observed by us at high PRIMPOL concentrations (Fig. 
4b
 (grey bars)). The ability of PRIMPOL to restart DNA synthesis far behind CMG may reflect a vestigial or primordial role of PRIMPOL to prime mtDNA lagging strand synthesis. This activity is normally suppressed by ssDNA-binding proteins (RPA or mtSSB), but becomes possible after PRIMPOL overexpression. Notably, DNA damage checkpoint activation and the subsequent phosphorylation of PRIMPOL by CHK1 (ref. 
22
) further upregulate CMG-independent repriming (Fig. 
4e,f
).
Although PRIMPOL is regarded as an attractive therapeutic target
17
,
63
, currently there are no small-molecule PRIMPOL inhibitors. Our findings suggest an alternative strategy to inhibit repriming—namely, to prevent PRIMPOL binding to CMG. This could be accomplished using peptides or molecules that bind to PRIMPOL’s N terminus, which normally interacts with MCM4–CMG.

For the ensemble DNA replication assay, co-IP assays and cell-based fibre assays, we carried out 3 or 4 independent replicates. 
P
 values were computed using the two-sample two-tailed Student 
t
-test function built into MATLAB. The data presented in the Figures are derived from one of the replicates. The exact 
P
 values for all data presented in the Figures and the Extended Data Figures are listed in Supplementary Table 
4
.
Single-molecule data analysis
Each replication initiation experiment imaged 4–10 FOVs over the course of 60–90 min with a resolution of 15, 30 or 60 s per frame. Each FOV typically contained 50–200 DNA molecules stretched to 80–90% of their contour length. A suite of MATLAB scripts written in house were used to select and analyse data as described in our recent methods article
70
. Below we summarize the data analysis pipeline specific for the single-molecule experiments analysed in this study.
Selecting DNA molecules
Regions of interest corresponding to individual DNA molecules were selected manually based on the previews of DNA stained with SYTOX Orange (these were acquired before the start of the experiment). Only well-separated DNA molecules were used in the analysis.
Fork-speed analysis
To measure fork speed before/after the PRIMPOL-dependent fork restart, we fit the GINS (Fig. 
1
) or RPA (Fig. 
2
 and Fig. 
4
) signals with a linear model (mock + buffer, mock + 10 µM APH, ∆Polα + buffer conditions), a bilinear model (∆Pol α + PRIMPOL, leading-strand DNA lesion + buffer) or a trilinear model (leading-strand DNA lesion + PRIMPOL) in MATLAB and extracted the slope of each linear fit (this has units of pixels per frame). To calculate the fork speed, we multiplied the slope of each linear fit segment to 160 nm per pixel (the size of our camera’s pixel), and divided it by 0.34 nm per bp (to convert nanometres into base pairs), and further divided it by 0.85 (to account for the fact that the DNA template is stretched to around 85% of its contour length).
PRIMPOL-mediated CMG-independent fork restart analysis
In Fig. 
4
, PRIMPOL-mediated CMG-independent fork restart was monitored by FEN1–KikG signal. To measure the speed of DNA synthesis during CMG-independent restart, the raw FEN1 signal was automatically thresholded using OTSU’s method using a built-in MATLAB function. The OTSU threshold binary image was used to fit straight lines to the edges of the growing FEN1 signal, and the fork speed value was extracted from the slope of the line (accounting for pixel size, the time resolution of the experiment and the extent to which DNA was stretched). Note that the efficiency of CMG-dependent and CMG-independent restart can add up to more than 1. This is illustrated in Fig. 
4a
, which contains two replication forks: both forks restarted in a CMG-dependent manner (white arrows), and there were two additional CMG-independent restart events (white asterisks).
Analysing PRIMPOL binding-dissociation (Δ
t

1
) and dissociation-fork restart (Δ
t

2
)
To analyse the binding and dissociation (Δ
t
1
) of PRIMPOL–AF647, we used the generalized likelihood ratio test (GLRT) as described in our previously study
71
. Specifically, we used GLRT to convert single-molecule images into binary images, which were subsequently segmented into binding events. The GLRT algorithm accounts for the intrinsic properties of noise in each experiment—a sliding window (we used a five-pixel window) is used to determine whether the signal at any pixel represents noise or signal. Using GLRT, we determined the time of PRIMPOL binding and PRIMPOL dissociation (and therefore Δ
t
1
) before a fork-restart event. To calculate the time delay between PRIMPOL–AF647 dissociation and fork restart (Δ
t
2
), we first measured the time of PRIMPOL–AF647 dissociation (as described above for Δ
t
1
), and the time of fork restart. The time of fork restart was extracted from the bilinear fit to the edge of the RPA signal (Fig. 
2a
): fork restart corresponds to the changepoint when the fork suddenly speeds up.
PRIMPOL fork-distal versus fork-proximal binding analysis
To measure the location and duration of PRIMPOL–AF647 binding events, we used the GLRT method as described above. To determine whether any given PRIMPOL–AF647 binding event is fork-proximal or fork distal, we compared the position of the binding event along the DNA molecule with the position of the replication fork measured by fitting the fluorescent RPA tract (as illustrated in Fig. 
2a
). Any PRIMPOL–AF647 binding event within 1 pixel of the replication fork was classified as fork-proximal, and all other binding events were classified as fork-distal.
To quantify the frequency of fork-proximal binding events across different conditions (no RS and RS), or for different PRIMPOL constructs (WT, AxA mutant or RPA-interaction mutant), we measured (1) 
N
proximal
, the number of fork-proximal PRIMPOL–AF647 binding events for each fork; and (2) 
T
fork
, the total duration that we observed that replication fork. We divided 
N
proximal
 by 
T
fork
 to obtain the normalized quantity shown in Fig. 
2d
.
We chose a different normalization strategy for fork-distal PRIMPOL–AF647 binding events because they can occur anywhere along RPA-coated ssDNA. To this end, for each replication fork we measured (1)
 N
distal
, the number of fork-distal binding events; (2) 
L
RPA–ssDNA
, the extent of the RPA-coated ssDNA; and (3) 
T
fork
, the total duration of time that the fork was monitored. We divided 
N
distal
 by 
L
RPA–ssDNA
 and T
fork
 to obtain the normalized quantity shown in Fig. 
2e
.

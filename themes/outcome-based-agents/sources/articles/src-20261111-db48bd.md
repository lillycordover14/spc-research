A new 
CoBank report
 is warning that an aging and shrinking inland hopper barge fleet threatens to cause a transportation bottleneck on the Mississippi River — the backbone of the U.S. grain export system.
Inland hopper barges transport roughly half of all U.S. corn and soybean exports and a third of wheat exports through the Mississippi Gulf region, according to USDA. Export demand for grain and oilseed exports is projected to rise 9% by 2035 from current levels.
While the need for shipping is forecasted to increase, the U.S. inland hopper barge fleet is shrinking. That fleet currently stands at 19,668 barges — down 1,617 barges, or 7.6%, in the last 10 years, according to Current Data LLC, a commercial vessel database company.
Meanwhile, that fleet is rapidly aging.
The average age of the U.S. inland hopper barge fleet has risen to 17.6 years — up from 13.8 years in 2016. The typical useful life of a barge is 25 to 30 years.
CoBank’s economists said the combination of an aging fleet, slow replacement rates and rising asset costs points toward higher freight rates over time.
“Farmers and elevators in the upper Midwest and river-adjacent grain-producing regions are likely most exposed because they rely heavily on barge access to reach Gulf export terminals,” wrote CoBank’s Tanner Ehmke, lead economist for grains and oilseeds, and Emmie Noyes, row crops and farm supply economist.
Smaller fleet could pressure shipping rates
At the Cairo-Memphis location on the Mississippi River, the three-month average barge rate of $20.04 per ton has climbed above the three-year average of $18.37 per ton for the same point in the year, based on tariff rates published by USDA’s Agricultural Marketing Service.
“Rates, though, are volatile,” the economists noted. “Crop size, export demand, domestic demand, diesel costs, river conditions and industry consolidation can outweigh replacement-cost economics in any individual year.”
While higher freight rates may encourage additional new construction, CoBank said transportation costs will likely trend upward over the long term as retirements outpace new builds.
“As older, lower-cost barges leave service and more expensive replacement vessels enter the market, transportation costs will increasingly reflect today’s higher construction costs,” the economists wrote, predicting that operators may extend existing assets until freight economics justify new construction.
Low water and aging infrastructure compound risks
During periods of reduced river levels, operators cannot load barges to full capacity and must reduce tow sizes, and successive years of low water on the Mississippi River have raised concerns over the viability of moving grain via barges.
Tighter barge availability ultimately raises freight rates and weakens grain basis levels paid to farmers, according to the economists.
“When barge freight rises, elevators often bid less aggressively for grain because more of the export value is absorbed by transportation costs,” CoBank reported. “That weaker bid shows up as lower local basis for farmers.”
Higher transportation costs also delay grain movement, forcing more grain into storage and widening seasonal basis carries, which the economists said ultimately defer grain shipments to relieve short-term pressure on freight demand.
“Without continued investment in hopper fleet replacement and waterway infrastructure, higher transportation costs could pressure farm margins, grain basis levels and U.S. export competitiveness,” the report concluded.
“These outcomes are not driven by fleet age alone and will depend on broader commodity, demand, operating cost and infrastructure conditions.”

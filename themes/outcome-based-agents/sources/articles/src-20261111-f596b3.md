                A Natufian figurine depicting a stylized human body modeled in clay 15,000 years ago, discovered at the site of Eynan-Mallaha (Upper Jordan Valley). The back of the clay figurine bears the fingerprint of an adult, covered by a child's fingerprint. This sequence of gestures in the wet clay, when the figurine was being modeled, is a rare direct testimony of intergenerational knowledge transmission in a hands-on apprenticeship. Credit: Laurent Davin, CC-BY 4.0 (creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)
            
 

Newly discovered artifacts are evidence of the earliest known example of a tradition of making clay figurines in the Middle East—and challenge long-held beliefs about when and how clay began to be used for symbolic purposes in the region, according to a study published in 
PLOS One
 by Laurent Davin from The Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Israel, and colleagues.

These artifacts, originally excavated from three sites in modern-day Israel, date back between about 15,000 and 11,500 years ago. Some appear to represent birds, mammals and humans, and some show traces of orange or red coloring—such as one fragment that may have depicted the facial pattern of a mountain gazelle.

At the time, this region was inhabited by Natufian communities, a sedentary hunter-gatherer culture. While archaeologists have previously found figurines from the Natufian period, most have been made from bone or stone, and it has been believed that clay figurines only became common in this region alongside the agricultural revolution in the Neolithic age, beginning around 11,500 years ago.

But, the authors of this paper say, these new findings show that Natufian figures were more varied and complex than we once thought—and suggest that the seeds of the region's Neolithic culture were sown well before the agricultural revolution.

Credit: Laurent Davin, CC-BY 4.0 (creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)
                
 

                    Fragment of a Natufian figurine depicting a gazelle head with horns made of perishable materials (e.g., twigs) modeled in clay 12,000 years ago, discovered at the site of Eynan-Mallaha (Upper Jordan Valley). The head was painted red with engobe (a mixture of liquid clay and ochre) to depict the gazelle's eyes and fur pattern. Credit: Laurent Davin, CC-BY 4.0 (creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)
                
 

Davin adds, "These discoveries change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in the first villages of humanity. Thousands of years before agriculture and pottery emerged, people were already using clay to create figures, as part of their beliefs and knowledge of the natural world. What is particularly fascinating is the evidence that children were involved in making these objects, suggesting that creativity and cultural knowledge were shared across generations."

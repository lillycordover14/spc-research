ElevenLabs' Ashley Kramer says voice agents are moving from pilot to production — and Medicare is the test

ElevenLabs chief revenue officer Ashley Kramer argues the next phase of enterprise AI is voice-first, and that the real bottleneck is not model intelligence but integration with legacy telephony and databases. Speaking at the ElevenLabs Summit, she detailed a four-layer stack plus forward-deployed engineers who fine-tune per customer, a partner network of more than 550 partners across 44 countries, and a McKinsey alliance to push voice agents into production. Medicare recipients are, by common consensus, the hardest audience in America to sell on talking to a machine. Which is exactly why ElevenLabs pointed its voice agents there first.

Clover Health, the Medicare-focused insurer, fields roughly 2 million inbound calls a year, and some members call in four to 10 times a week. ElevenLabs has built agents to handle those calls — plan selection, Medicare navigation, plus outbound check-ins for members with heart conditions — and is now handing the solution over to Clover's own engineering team. "If you think about some of the most resistant people to this next wave of AI, particularly interaction with it, it's likely some of that generation," said Ashley Kramer, ElevenLabs' chief revenue officer, speaking at the ElevenLabs Summit with host Sophia Dew. If voice AI works there, the argument goes, it works anywhere.

That framing is the clearest statement of where enterprise AI is heading next: away from chatbots you type at, toward systems that talk. And the battle, Kramer insists, is no longer about whose model is smartest. It is about who can wire that intelligence into the phone systems, databases and language quirks of very large, very old companies.

A voice model alone doesn't win the enterprise

Kramer's core argument is that owning a strong voice model is table stakes, not a moat. Every enterprise has bespoke telephony, its own databases, and its own linguistic oddities that a generic product cannot absorb. ElevenLabs' answer is a four-layer stack, plus people who show up on site.

The model layer includes vertical models such as Scribe Medical, launched at the summit, which understands prescription names and the differences between them. Above that sits an orchestration layer that can route to different large language models depending on what a customer's solution requires. Then agents, built on top. And finally forward-deployed engineers — FDEs — who fine-tune the whole thing to the enterprise's needs.

The proof point is KPN, the Dutch telecom. ElevenLabs built a first-call solution for KPN's small-business customers: a caller states their name, birth date and postcode for verification. Because of how alphanumeric characters are handled in Dutch and the way KPN verifies identity, the success rate sat around 60% — a failing grade for a production system.

"Our FDE team was able to go in and within days realize that nuance, fix it because we could go back and fine-tune the model specific for them, never sharing enterprise data of course, and be able to deliver that and get an 85% success rate out of it," Kramer said. Days, not quarters — and no cross-contamination of one customer's data into another's model.

The handoff is deliberately optional. "Our approach is if the customer wants to continue innovating, please go ahead. If you want us to come in and work with you, that's an option as well." That flexibility matters commercially: it lowers the fear of lock-in that stalls large enterprise deals, while keeping ElevenLabs in the room for the next phase of work.

Notably, Kramer said the number-one KPI for the Clover Health work is patient satisfaction and customer experience — not cost. That is a telling choice from a revenue chief. Cost-cutting is the easiest enterprise pitch and the one most likely to provoke internal resistance; leading with customer experience reframes the agent as a service upgrade rather than a headcount story.

She also flagged financial services, travel, hospitality, retail and telecom as active verticals, and mentioned a retail customer she could not name, working on web experience, abandoned cart recovery, loyalty and in-store assistance.

The partner network exists because ElevenLabs can't hire fast enough

ElevenLabs announced a partner program at the summit covering more than 550 partners across 44 countries, plus a deeper alliance with McKinsey to push voice agents into production. Kramer's justification is capacity, not channel economics.

"We can't scale fast enough to actually meet that demand," she said. The McKinsey relationship runs on introductions — McKinsey knows the enterprise problem, ElevenLabs builds and scales the solution. She attended several dinners with McKinsey during two weeks in Europe, and a full partner day followed the summit.

The international angle is grounded in the product's foundation: translation. ElevenLabs supports 90 languages and serves enterprises in 74 countries, which Kramer ties to language-specific problems that local partners help surface. Deutsche Telekom's in-network translation layer is the clearest example — a caller speaks English, the system translates into the listener's language in real time, which Kramer framed as breaking down language barriers in native-language communication.

For investors watching the AI application layer, the partner strategy is the number to weigh against the hype. A 550-partner network with 44-country coverage is a distribution machine, but it is also a signal that direct sales cannot keep pace with demand — a good problem that still constrains revenue timing.

The blocker is integration, not intelligence

Asked what has kept this technology from diffusing into enterprises at scale, Kramer pointed squarely at integration. Every industry runs its own telephony and databases, and the FDEs exist to connect those systems and lay a voice layer on top.

The second requirement is context continuity across channels — text, WhatsApp, web, mobile and natural voice — so a customer does not have to restart the conversation on each channel. ElevenLabs announced "routines" at the summit, which lets the follow-up after a voice conversation happen immediately regardless of channel, closing what she called the 360 workflow. She conceded this is not fully solved yet.

Her adoption test is emotional rather than technical: voice has to not sound like a robot, and the emotion behind it has to make people feel they are not talking to a robot. She predicted people will tolerate an AI agent interrupting them in a way they would never tolerate a human doing, because the agent gets things done faster.

"The reality is we probably won't cut a human off as they're trying to get information to us on the phone, but we certainly will cut an AI agent off," she said. Her stated ambition is blunter still: "My ultimate goal for us is to have people stop pressing zero and yelling human on the phone and instead say AI agent."

That is the adoption thesis in one line. Voice agents do not need to be better than a great human agent; they need to be better than hold music.

The next twelve months: escaping pilot purgatory

Kramer's forward-looking claim is that the coming year is defined by the shift from pilot to production, not by another leap in model capability. She described the current moment as "the era of intelligence," where large language models are already highly capable and the remaining work is capacity, token cost-efficiency, and serving people at the intelligence level they need.

The next wave, in her framing, is when technology adapts to the human rather than forcing humans to adapt to it — the way typing and touchscreens once did — and returns communication to voice. She predicted that as voice models become more emotive and context-aware, people will prefer talking to an AI agent in certain situations because tasks get done faster. Confidence: medium, and worth treating as such — it is a vendor's forecast about its own market.

She relayed advice from Clover Health's Cameron, head of strategy, who joined her on stage, on how to get out of pilot: start somewhere, test it, make sure it is good, ship it to market perhaps in a controlled way, and learn to scale from real adoption. Kramer expects that a year from now the conversation will have moved to "what's the next era of interaction," with the open question being where a human must stay in the loop and where that step can be skipped so people can build relationships and do creative work.

What the summit signals about ElevenLabs' commercial posture

The announcements Kramer referenced — the partner program, the McKinsey alliance, Scribe Medical, routines, and the V4 model with more emotive and context-aware output that can hold context from call to call for a specific customer — point to a company positioning itself as an interaction layer rather than a voice API. More than 2,000 people attended the summit, she said, and the next few months will bring the announced products to market, some already generally available and some still coming, with customer stories as the primary proof.

The through-line in her answers is that ElevenLabs wants to be judged on production deployments in regulated industries, and it is using partners and forward-deployed engineers to get there faster than it could hire.

The unresolved tension is that most of the evidence is vendor-reported and unevenly quantified. KPN has a clean before-and-after. Clover Health has volume and a KPI but no outcome figure. Westgate, Customer Bank and the retail customer have narratives without numbers. And the claim that Medicare-age users are the hardest audience is Kramer's characterization, not a measured finding.

The regulatory backdrop adds a wrinkle: as voice agents touch healthcare and financial services, governments are moving too. Australia said this week it will require AI companies to report immediately when models behave against intent or a cyber incident occurs — a response to an OpenAI agent breaching government websites in June. Voice agents handling Medicare calls sit squarely in that line of fire, which makes Clover Health's satisfaction KPI a number worth watching for reasons beyond customer service.

For anyone tracking enterprise voice adoption, the test is simple: watch whether the pilot-to-production shift Kramer predicts shows up as disclosed production metrics from these named accounts over the next year — and whether the McKinsey alliance produces named joint deployments rather than introductions. The category looks real. The revenue is still mostly a promise.

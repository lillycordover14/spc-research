                                ASIA: adaptive single-diffusion-stage inference acceleration for diffusion-based virtual try-on
                            

Download PDF

Abstract
Virtual try-on synthesises an image of a person wearing a chosen garment. Diffusion models give the most realistic results, but remain too slow for interactive use. The leading method, TPD, runs its diffusion network twice: a first pass predicts a pair-specific mask locating where the garment belongs, and a second pass renders the garment into that region. Each pass takes about 100 denoising steps, so one image needs roughly 200, half of them producing only a binary shape. This first stage does not justify a diffusion model. We propose Adaptive Single-Diffusion-Stage Inference Acceleration (ASIA), which replaces diffusion-based mask generation with a lightweight convolutional network. Using DensePose and the flat garment mask as structural priors, ASIA predicts the pair-specific mask in one forward pass, reducing that stage from seconds to milliseconds. Try-on then needs a single diffusion stage, whose input block alone is finetuned to the new mask distribution. On VITON-HD, inference falls from 15.4 to 7.5 seconds per image, a 2.05-fold speedup. Under a controlled reproduction of TPD, paired structural (SSIM) and perceptual (LPIPS) similarity both improve while the Fréchet Inception Distance rises slightly (9.04 against 8.78). Removing an entire diffusion stage thus trades a small FID increase for a large speedup and improved paired fidelity.

Introduction
Image-based virtual try-on renders a photo of a person wearing a chosen garment. It is a core technology for online fashion retail: it lets shoppers preview clothing on their own body, which raises engagement and helps reduce the return rates that come from buying garments unseen. A single shopper typically browses many items in one session, and every generated image sits directly on their path, so the experience is only useful if it is fast. Slow generation both frustrates the user and raises serving cost, which makes latency a first-class design concern rather than an afterthought.
Diffusion-based methods currently produce the highest-quality try-on results, drawing on the strong generative prior of large pretrained models. The leading such method, TPD
1
, reaches state-of-the-art fidelity without adding any dedicated garment encoders: it lays the garment and the masked person side by side on one canvas and lets the denoising network’s own self-attention carry texture across. It pays for this in time, because it runs that network 
twice
. A first pass, of roughly 100 DDIM steps, takes the masked person together with DensePose and pose maps and predicts the inpainting 
mask
 as an extra output channel; a second pass, of roughly 100 further steps, takes that mask, intersected with the parsing-based garment region, and synthesises the final image. The result is roughly 200 denoising steps per image, and this doubled diffusion is what dominates inference time. Figure 
1
 shows this pipeline. The mask produced by the first pass cannot simply be discarded, either: it is tailored to each specific person–garment pair rather than being a single generic shape reused for everyone, and a generic mask leaves visible artifacts and lowers quality.

Our starting observation is that the first pass does far less work than the second. Its output is essentially a 
binary region
, marking where on the body the known garment should go, yet TPD produces it with the same heavy, iterative diffusion model used for photorealistic synthesis. A single feed-forward network can predict the same region in one pass, in roughly 0.004 to 0.02 s, which is negligible next to the roughly 7.9 s diffusion stage it replaces. In other words, an entire diffusion stage can be removed without touching the part of the pipeline that actually creates image detail.
Building on this, we replace TPD’s first-stage diffusion mask predictor with a lightweight convolutional network, which we call the 
alpha
 predictor, and keep its high-quality second-stage synthesiser, which we lightly finetune to the new mask distribution. This collapses the pipeline from two diffusion stages to one. Our contributions are:

A single-pass mask predictor (UNet++ with a ResNet-34 encoder, approximately 26M parameters) driven only by structural inputs, namely DensePose and the flat garment mask, replacing TPD’s first-stage diffusion.

A 
single-diffusion-stage

2.05 times faster

An evaluation on VITON-HD under a carefully controlled protocol, showing that the speedup costs only about 0.26 FID versus our own reproduction, while improving paired fidelity over that same reproduction.

Related work
GAN-based virtual try-on
Early image-based try-on methods explicitly 
warp
 the garment onto the target body and blend it with a generative adversarial network. VITON
2
 and CP-VTON
3
 introduced the warp-then-refine paradigm; VITON-HD
4
 scaled it to high resolution with an alignment-aware normalisation that suppresses warping artifacts, and HR-VITON
5
 further reduced misalignment and occlusion artifacts by predicting the warp and the segmentation jointly. These methods are fast, but their quality hinges on the accuracy of the warping step: they degrade under large pose or shape changes and self-occlusion, and often blur or distort fine garment texture.
Diffusion-based virtual try-on
Latent diffusion models
6
 substantially raised try-on quality and have largely replaced generative adversarial networks. One line of work still warps the garment first and uses diffusion only to refine it, for example LaDI-VTON
7
 and DCI-VTON
8
; another avoids explicit warping and instead conditions the diffusion model on the garment through attention, for example StableVITON
9
, IDM-VTON
10
 and OOTDiffusion
11
. The latter group typically adds dedicated garment encoders and injects their features through cross-attention, which enlarges the model and its inference cost and can still wash out fine texture detail. TPD
1
 belongs to this warping-free family but avoids extra encoders entirely, placing the person and garment side by side on a single canvas and letting the denoising network’s self-attention transfer texture. The shared cost of all these methods is speed, since generating one image takes many iterative denoising steps, and TPD in particular runs its denoising network 
twice
, once to predict an inpainting mask and once to synthesise the garment, roughly doubling an already expensive process. Our work targets exactly this cost: to our knowledge, we are the first to replace a diffusion-based inpainting-mask stage with a single feed-forward network, keeping the high-quality synthesis stage untouched.
Efficient and accelerated diffusion
A large body of work speeds up diffusion 
sampling
. Faster solvers, such as DDIM
12
 and DPM-Solver
13
, cut the number of steps, and distillation methods, including progressive distillation
14
 and consistency and latent-consistency models
15
,
16
, push generation toward a few or a single step. These techniques make each stage cheaper and are complementary to ours: rather than accelerating the steps 
within
 a stage, we remove an entire diffusion stage from the pipeline. The two directions compose, in that a faster sampler applied to our remaining second stage would compound the speedup. Closer to our goal, a few try-on methods target efficiency directly by slimming the 
architecture
; CatVTON
17
, for example, drops the auxiliary garment encoders and trains far fewer parameters. These methods still run a single diffusion process end to end, whereas we cut the pipeline from two diffusion stages to one, which is orthogonal to such architectural savings.

Discussion
Dropping a diffusion stage costs a small amount of unpaired FID, about 0.26 versus our reproduction, but halves inference time and, against that same reproduction, improves paired fidelity—significantly and with a medium-to-large effect size (“
Statistical significance
”). This supports our central claim: the first diffusion stage is unnecessary for competitive try-on. The evidence is not merely that quality 
survives
 the change, but that the two metrics which compare against a known ground-truth image both move in our favour while the metric that only measures distributional realism moves against us by a fraction of a point.
The reason a diffusion stage can be removed without losing quality is that the two stages of TPD solve problems of very different difficulty. The second stage must invent plausible fabric, shading and folds that were never photographed, which is a genuinely generative task and exactly what an iterative denoiser is good at. The first stage must decide 
which pixels to repaint
, and its output, after thresholding, is a binary region. That is a segmentation problem, and segmentation is a task feed-forward networks have solved well for a decade. Running a 944M-parameter denoising network for roughly 100 iterations to produce a binary map is therefore not just expensive but mismatched to the task. Our result quantifies how mismatched: a 26M-parameter convolutional network, roughly 3% of the size and run once instead of a hundred times, produces masks good enough that the final image quality is essentially maintained—paired fidelity improves, at a small cost in FID.
That paired fidelity 
improves
 rather than degrades was not the expected outcome, and it deserves an explanation rather than a claim of superiority. We attribute it mainly to the second-stage finetuning of “
Second-stage finetuning
”. TPD’s second stage was trained on masks drawn from its own first-stage distribution; handing it masks from a different predictor is a distribution shift, and adapting only the first input block, some 1.2% of the parameters, is enough to absorb it. A second contributing factor is that our masks are more consistent: a convolutional network is deterministic, whereas TPD’s first-stage mask is the output of a stochastic sampling process that starts from a fresh noise draw, so the same person–garment pair can yield slightly different masks on different runs. Removing that variance removes one source of per-image error. The ablation in “
Ablation: how the mask predictor was chosen
” supports the same reading from the opposite side: a 
stronger
 mask predictor with higher IoU did not improve downstream FID, which indicates that mask quality is not the binding constraint once the mask is good enough, and that what the second stage actually needs is masks it has been adapted to.
The 0.26 FID we give up is small but real. The most likely cause is not mask accuracy in the average case but the tail: our threshold is set at the recall-favouring end of the plateau (“
The alpha mask predictor
”), so our masks tend to be slightly larger than strictly necessary, and the second stage must therefore synthesise a little more background and skin than it would with a tighter mask. The failure mode visible in Fig. 
3
, row 4, is a related but distinct issue: the intersection with 
\(M_g\)
 caps the mask at the worn garment’s extent, so a target garment with a longer hem cannot extend beyond it. That limitation is inherited from TPD rather than introduced by us, but our pipeline has no second chance to correct it, since there is no longer a stage that can propose a region outside 
\(M_g\)
.
It is worth being precise about what the speedup does and does not claim. The 2.05-fold figure is a comparison against the two-stage pipeline we accelerate, measured on identical hardware with preprocessing excluded from both sides. It is not a claim of being the fastest try-on method available: the warping-based baselines in Table 
3
 are cheaper still, at a clear cost in paired fidelity. The contribution is better read as removing a 
specific
 redundancy from a 
specific
 family of methods. It is also complementary to the other main axis of diffusion acceleration: faster samplers and distillation reduce the cost 
within
 a stage, whereas we remove a stage outright, so applying a few-step sampler to our remaining second stage would compound with our result rather than compete with it.
We conjecture, but do not demonstrate, that the same substitution could extend beyond TPD’s architecture: in principle, any try-on pipeline that spends a generative pass producing a binary or near-binary intermediate—a mask, a layout, a coarse region—might be a candidate, provided a feed-forward network can be supervised on that intermediate, which a segmentation-style target makes cheap. We have tested this only for TPD on VITON-HD, so we present it strictly as a direction for future work, not a validated conclusion.
Our evaluation is limited to VITON-HD, a single benchmark dominated by frontal, upper-body garments; we adopt it because TPD, the pipeline we accelerate, is itself benchmarked only on VITON-HD, so a controlled before-and-after comparison must use the same data. Our quantitative conclusions are therefore restricted to this setting and to the specific TPD-style pipeline. In particular, we do not claim that the alpha mask predictor generalises across datasets, garment categories, or larger garment–body shape variations: DensePose and the flat garment mask are reliable structural priors for frontal upper-body tops, but side or self-occluding poses, lower-body and full-body garments, and larger shape changes are untested and may require retraining or richer priors. As an ongoing extension we are training a single joint alpha predictor on DressCode
35
, which covers upper-body, lower-body and dress categories, to test cross-category generalisation; until then we present broader applicability as future work rather than a validated result. Our speedup also composes with faster diffusion samplers, since we still keep one full diffusion stage; applying such samplers to that remaining stage is a natural next step.
Methods
Background on diffusion models
Diffusion models are an advanced type of generative model. Recent models, such as the Stable Diffusion model
6
, consist of a variational autoencoder
18
 that encodes the input 
\(x \in \mathbb {R}^{3 \times H \times W}\)
 into a latent representation 
\(z = E(x) \in \mathbb {R}^{4 \times h \times w}\)
, whose decoder 
D
 maps a latent back to pixel space, together with a denoising network 
\(\epsilon _{\theta }\)
 that works in latent space: given a noisy latent, it predicts the noise.
Following the denoising diffusion probabilistic model formulation
19
, a forward process gradually adds Gaussian noise to the latent 
\(z_{0} = E(x)\)
 over 
T
 steps according to a fixed variance schedule 
\(\beta _{1}, \dots , \beta _{T}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} q\!\left( z_{t} \mid z_{t-1}\right) = \mathcal {N}\!\left( z_{t};\, \sqrt{1-\beta _{t}}\, z_{t-1},\, \beta _{t}{\bf I}\right) , \end{aligned}$$

                    (1)
                
where 
\(z_{t}\)
 is the latent after 
t
 noising steps, 
\(\mathcal {N}(\,\cdot \,; \text {mean}, \text {covariance})\)
 is a Gaussian distribution, 
\(\beta _{t} \in (0,1)\)
 is the amount of noise injected at step 
t
, and 
\({\bf I}\)
 is the identity matrix, so the noise added is the same in every latent dimension and uncorrelated across them. The factor 
\(\sqrt{1-\beta _{t}}\)
 shrinks the previous latent as noise is added, which keeps the overall scale of 
\(z_{t}\)
 constant instead of letting it grow with 
t
.
The process continues until the latent becomes isotropic Gaussian noise 
\(z_{T}\)
. Writing 
\(\gamma _{t} = \prod _{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta _{s})\)
 for the cumulative amount of the original signal that survives after 
t
 steps, any step is reachable in closed form as 
\(z_{t} = \sqrt{\gamma _{t}}\, z_{0} + \sqrt{1-\gamma _{t}}\, \epsilon \)
 with 
\(\epsilon \sim \mathcal {N}({\bf 0}, {\bf I})\)
 a sample of standard Gaussian noise. This closed form is what makes training cheap: rather than simulating the whole chain, one jumps directly to a single randomly drawn timestep, and the denoising network is trained to recover the noise that was used,
$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {LDM}} = \mathbb {E}_{z_{0},\, \epsilon ,\, t,\, e} \!\left[ \left\Vert \epsilon - \epsilon _{\theta }\!\left( z_{t},\, t,\, e\right) \right\Vert _{2}^{2}\right] . \end{aligned}$$

                    (2)
                
Here 
\(\epsilon \)
 is the true noise that was added, 
\(\epsilon _{\theta }\)
 is the denoising network with learnable parameters 
\(\theta \)
, 
t
 is the timestep it is told it is at, 
e
 is the conditioning signal, and 
\(\Vert \cdot \Vert _{2}^{2}\)
 is the squared Euclidean distance. The expectation 
\(\mathbb {E}\)
 averages over the training latents 
\(z_{0}\)
, over fresh noise draws 
\(\epsilon \)
, over uniformly sampled timesteps 
t
, and over conditions 
e
, so one training step amounts to picking an image, picking a timestep, noising it, and asking the network to name the noise.
The denoising network then reverses the forward process at inference: starting from 
\(z_{T}\)
, it iteratively predicts and removes the noise across the timesteps 
\(t = T, \dots , 1\)
, guided by a condition 
e
. This yields a latent 
\(z'\)
, which the decoder turns back into the final image, 
\(x' = D(z')\)
. For image inpainting, the denoising network is additionally conditioned on the region to fill: alongside the noisy latent it also receives the inpainting mask 
m
 and the encoded masked background 
\(E(m \odot x)\)
.
The TPD baseline
TPD improves virtual try-on by leveraging the denoising network’s self-attention to transfer garment textures. By spatially concatenating the reference garment and the masked person image, the model transfers textures directly through self-attention, offering an efficient alternative to cross-attention methods. TPD also proposes a decoupled mask prediction method that generates tailored masks for specific person–garment pairs, replacing the universal masks used in conventional methods.
Concretely, TPD places the person image (
\(512 \times 384\)
) and the garment image side by side on a 768-pixel-wide canvas, then applies two diffusion stages on the same denoising network. The first stage predicts the inpainting mask and the second paints the garment into that predicted mask. Figure 
1
 shows the full pipeline.
Formally, let 
x
 be the person image, 
P
 its DensePose
20
 and OpenPose
21
 maps, 
G
 the garment image, and 
\(M_{g}\)
 the parsing-based garment support. Write 
\(\Phi (z_{T}, c)\)
 for a full DDIM sampling loop of roughly 100 steps of the denoising network 
\(\epsilon _{\theta }\)
 from noise 
\(z_{T}\)
 under conditioning 
c
. TPD first runs the loop with a coarse box mask 
\(M_{\square }\)
 to obtain a tailored mask,
$$\begin{aligned} M^{s1} = \Phi \!\left( z_{T},\, c^{s1}\right) , \qquad c^{s1} = \left[ \, M_{\square },\; E\!\left( x \odot (1 - M_{\square })\right) ,\; P,\; G \,\right] , \end{aligned}$$

                    (3)
                
and then runs the 
same
 loop again, now conditioned on the refined mask 
\(M = M^{s1} \odot M_{g}\)
, to synthesise the try-on image,
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{x} = D\!\left( \Phi \!\left( z_{T},\, c^{s2}\right) \right) , \qquad c^{s2} = \left[ \, M,\; E\!\left( x \odot (1 - M)\right) ,\; P,\; G \,\right] . \end{aligned}$$

                    (4)
                
In both equations, 
\(z_{T}\)
 is a fresh draw of Gaussian noise, 
c
 is the list of conditioning inputs concatenated and fed to the network, and 
E
 and 
D
 are the encoder and decoder. 
\(M^{s1}\)
 is the mask the first stage produces, and 
M
 is that mask after it has been restricted to the parsing-based garment support 
\(M_{g}\)
 by the element-wise product 
\(\odot \)
. The term 
\(x \odot (1 - M)\)
 is the person image with the masked region erased—the masked person the model must fill in—and 
\(E(\cdot )\)
 compresses it into the latent space the network operates in. The superscripts 
s
1 and 
s
2 simply label which stage each conditioning set belongs to; the network 
\(\epsilon _{\theta }\)
 inside 
\(\Phi \)
 is the 
same
 network in both. The cost of the method is visible in the notation: 
\(\Phi \)
, the expensive term, appears 
twice
, once in Eq. 
3
 and once in Eq. 
4
, for a total of roughly 200 denoising steps per image.
Proposed method
TPD reaches high fidelity but is slow. Both stages are diffusion processes run on the same denoising network, so a single try-on needs two full DDIM runs, roughly 200 steps in total, which dominate inference time at roughly 15 s per image on an RTX A4000. The two runs, however, do very different amounts of work. The first stage decides 
where
 the garment goes: it takes the person under a coarse box mask, together with the pose and DensePose maps and the garment image, and denoises them into a per-pair inpainting mask that marks the pixels to be repainted—an output that is thresholded to a binary region, so all that survives the roughly 100 denoising steps is a shape. The second stage decides 
what
 goes there: given that mask and the same conditioning, it synthesises the garment into the erased region, inventing the fabric, folds, shading and boundaries that were never photographed on this person. This cost is justified for the second stage, which must produce photorealistic texture, but not for the first, whose only job is to output a near-binary inpainting mask. Using a heavy diffusion model to decide where a 
known
 garment shape should be placed on the body is wasteful; a single feed-forward pass predicts the same region far more cheaply.
We therefore keep TPD’s second stage and replace the first-stage diffusion mask predictor with a lightweight, single-pass convolutional network, the alpha predictor. From only structural cues, namely DensePose and the flat garment mask, it produces the inpainting mask in one forward pass instead of roughly 100 denoising steps, collapsing the pipeline to a single diffusion stage. We then finetune a small part of the second stage so it adapts to our mask distribution. Figure 
2
 gives an overview of the resulting pipeline.
Putting the two stages together, given a person image 
x
 with DensePose map 
P
, a garment image 
G
 and its flat cloth mask 
\(G_{m}\)
, our method computes
$$\begin{aligned} M \;=\; \underbrace{\textrm{LCC}\!\left( \left[ \sigma \!\left( f_{\alpha }(P, G_{m})\right) > \tau \right] \right) }_{M_{\alpha }\;:\;\text {one forward pass}} \;\odot \; M_{g}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (5)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{x} \;=\; D\!\left( \Phi \!\left( z_{T},\, c\right) \right) , \qquad c \;=\; \left[ \, M,\; E\!\left( x \odot (1 - M)\right) ,\; P,\; G \,\right] . \end{aligned}$$

                    (6)
                
Here 
\(f_{\alpha }\)
 is the alpha predictor and 
\(\sigma \)
 the sigmoid, which turns its raw outputs into per-pixel probabilities in [0, 1]; 
\(\tau \)
 is the threshold that converts those probabilities into a binary map and 
\([\,\cdot \,]\)
 is the Iverson bracket, which evaluates to 1 at every pixel where the enclosed condition holds and 0 elsewhere; 
\(\textrm{LCC}\)
 is the largest-connected-component filter, which discards all but the biggest blob; 
\(M_{g}\)
 is the parsing-based garment support and 
\(\odot \)
 the element-wise product, so 
M
 keeps only pixels that both the predictor and the parsing map agree on. In Eq. 
6
, 
\(\Phi \)
 denotes the DDIM sampling loop of roughly 100 steps over the finetuned second-stage network 
\(\epsilon _{\theta }\)
 starting from noise 
\(z_{T}\)
, 
c
 is the conditioning set described for Eq. 
4
, 
E
 and 
D
 are the encoder and decoder, and 
\(\hat{x}\)
 is the final try-on image. The single occurrence of 
\(\Phi \)
 is the point: TPD evaluates this loop twice, once to obtain 
M
 and once to obtain 
\(\hat{x}\)
, whereas our mask comes from a single evaluation of 
\(f_{\alpha }\)
.
The alpha mask predictor
Inputs
The predictor sees only structural cues: a four-channel, 768-pixel-wide canvas consisting of the DensePose RGB map (left half, 3 channels) and a flat garment cloth mask (right half, 1 channel). It never sees the person’s RGB photo, which avoids leaking the currently worn clothing into the prediction. DensePose was selected because it holds all the structural information of the person’s body, which is exactly what is needed to fit the garment.
Architecture
UNet++
22
 with a ResNet-34
23
 encoder (
smp.UnetPlusPlus
 from the segmentation-models-pytorch library
24
, approximately 26M parameters). The output is cropped to the left half to give a 
\(1 \times 512 \times 384\)
 mask.
Target and loss
The supervision target is the garment region (human-parsing labels 5 and 7, upper-clothes and coat, from the maps shipped with the dataset
25
), a binary map 
\(y \in \{0,1\}^{N}\)
 over the 
\(N = 512 \times 384\)
 pixels. Let 
\(p_{i} = \sigma \!\left( f_{\alpha }(\cdot )_{i}\right) \in [0,1]\)
 be the predicted probability for pixel 
i
. We combine two complementary losses. Binary cross-entropy,
$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {BCE}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum _{i=1}^{N} \Big [\, y_{i} \log p_{i} + \left( 1 - y_{i}\right) \log \!\left( 1 - p_{i}\right) \Big ], \end{aligned}$$

                    (7)
                
scores each pixel independently and keeps the output probabilities calibrated—which matters because the mask is later obtained by thresholding them at 
\(\tau \)
 (Eq. 
10
)—but it is weak under class imbalance, as the garment covers only a minority of the canvas. The soft Dice loss
26
,
$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L}_{\text {Dice}} = 1 - \frac{2 \sum _{i=1}^{N} p_{i} y_{i} + \varepsilon }{\sum _{i=1}^{N} p_{i} + \sum _{i=1}^{N} y_{i} + \varepsilon }, \end{aligned}$$

                    (8)
                
with 
\(\varepsilon \)
 a small constant guarding the empty-region case, instead scores the predicted and target regions as whole shapes: normalising by region size makes it insensitive to background and directly optimises the overlap (Dice or IoU) we later use for model selection, at the price of noisier gradients early in training. We use the two in equal proportion,
$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L} = 0.5\,\mathcal {L}_{\text {BCE}} + 0.5\,\mathcal {L}_{\text {Dice}}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (9)
                
so binary cross-entropy stabilises early training while Dice supplies the region-level signal that fixes the mask boundary onto the true garment.
Augmentation
We apply only geometry-preserving augmentation. The DensePose map and its ground-truth mask receive the 
same
 random horizontal flip and small geometric jitter (rotation 
\(\pm 10^{\circ }\)
, translation 
\(\pm 5\%\)
, scale 
\(\pm 10\%\)
) so they stay pixel-aligned; the flat garment mask, being a spatially unrelated photo, is flipped and jittered independently, and its boundary is randomly eroded or dilated to simulate imperfect upstream garment masks. All geometric operations use nearest-neighbour interpolation. We deliberately use no colour (photometric) augmentation, because DensePose encodes body-part identity as colour, so any change to hue, brightness or contrast—or bilinear blending during a geometric transform—would corrupt those colour-coded semantics and mislead the predictor.
Post-processing
Let 
\(\hat{M} = \sigma \!\left( f_{\alpha }(\cdot )\right) \in [0,1]^{512 \times 384}\)
 be the predictor’s soft output. At inference we threshold it at 
\(\tau = 0.2\)
 and keep the largest connected component,
$$\begin{aligned} M_{\alpha } = \textrm{LCC}\!\left( \left[ \hat{M} > \tau \right] \right) , \qquad \tau = 0.2, \end{aligned}$$

                    (10)
                
where 
\(\hat{M}\)
 is the soft probability map, 
\(\tau \)
 the decision threshold, 
\(\left[ \hat{M} > \tau \right] \)
 the binary map that is 1 at every pixel whose probability exceeds 
\(\tau \)
, 
\(\textrm{LCC}\)
 the largest-connected-component filter that keeps only the single biggest region of that binary map, and 
\(M_{\alpha }\)
 the resulting hard mask.
The threshold value is chosen deliberately: a high threshold yields a tight mask that may miss fragments of the person’s original garment, while a slightly lower threshold yields a larger mask that is successfully handled by the intersection with the garment support and by second-stage generation. We conducted a controlled experiment on 500 pairs sampled (seed 42) from a held-out validation pool carved from the training set and disjoint from both the test set and the alpha predictor’s training pairs, where the ground-truth inpainting region is unambiguous because the person is already wearing the exact garment being evaluated, so the true garment segmentation (parsing labels 5 and 7) is precisely the region the mask should cover. For each sample, the model’s raw probability map was computed once and swept across thresholds from 0.05 to 0.95, scoring the resulting hole against this ground truth via IoU, Dice, precision and recall.
The results show that overlap accuracy (IoU and Dice) is effectively flat across the threshold range 0.15 to 0.6, varying by only about 0.2 percentage points (Dice: 91.02 to 91.22%), meaning no threshold in this range is meaningfully more correct from a pure shape-matching perspective. What varies substantially in this range is the precision–recall trade-off: lowering the threshold increases recall, that is coverage of the true garment region, at the cost of precision, that is extra non-garment area included in the hole. At threshold 0.2, recall is 95.45% versus 93.83% at the Dice-maximising threshold of 0.4, a 26% relative reduction in missed-garment area (4.55% versus 6.17% uncovered). Figure 
5
 plots all four metrics across the sweep.
We argue that this trade-off is not symmetric in its visual cost. Under-coverage, that is low recall, leaves fragments of the original garment visible in the final generated image, a readily noticeable artifact commonly referred to as ghosting. Over-coverage, that is low precision, by contrast only enlarges the region handed to the second-stage diffusion model, which reliably reconstructs plausible body and background content in that area. Given this asymmetry, we select 
\(\tau = 0.2\)
, which sits at the recall-favourable end of the statistically flat Dice and IoU plateau, sacrificing a negligible 0.2-point Dice difference from the numerical optimum at 0.4 in exchange for a substantially lower risk of visible leftover-garment artifacts. The mask is then flipped to TPD’s convention, in which 1 means keep.

Intersection with the garment support
The final inpainting mask is the element-wise product of the predicted mask 
\(M_{\alpha }\)
 and the ground-truth garment support 
\(M_{g}\)
,
$$\begin{aligned} M = M_{\alpha } \odot M_{g}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (11)
                
where 
\(M_{\alpha }\)
 is the thresholded alpha mask from Eq. (
10
), 
\(M_{g}\)
 is the garment region of the person taken from the human-parsing map (upper-clothes and coat labels), 
\(\odot \)
 is the element-wise product, so a pixel survives only if 
both
 masks contain it, and 
M
 is the final inpainting mask. This confines the prediction to the region the person’s own garment actually occupies and removes stray activations. 
M
 is fed straight into the second stage. This step is inherited directly from TPD.

Second-stage finetuning
The second stage was trained on TPD’s own predicted masks, so feeding it the alpha masks directly introduces a small distribution shift, as illustrated in Fig. 
6
. We correct this by finetuning 
only the first input block
 of the second-stage network – its input convolution and first down-sampling block (
\(\theta _{\text {in}}\)
, approximately 11.36M of 944M parameters, or 1.2%)—while freezing the rest, using the standard latent diffusion objective
$$\begin{aligned} \min _{\theta _{\text {in}}}\; \mathbb {E}_{z,\,\epsilon ,\,t} \!\left[ \left\Vert \epsilon - \epsilon _{\theta }(z_{t}, c) \right\Vert _{2}^{2}\right] , \end{aligned}$$

                    (12)
                
where 
\(\theta _{\text {in}}\)
 are the parameters of the first input block, the only ones optimised, as indicated by the minimisation being taken over them alone; 
\(z_{t}\)
 is the noised latent at timestep 
t
; 
\(\epsilon \)
 the noise actually added; 
\(\epsilon _{\theta }\)
 the second-stage network; and 
c
 the second-stage conditioning, comprising the masked person, the garment, and our mask 
M
. The objective is identical in form to Eq. 
2
; the only difference is the restricted set of trainable parameters and the fact that 
c
 now carries our masks rather than TPD’s.
We keep TPD’s original finetuning recipe: learning rate 
\(10^{-6}\)
, a 200-step warmup, and the full latent-channel loss. Because validation loss does not reliably track image quality in this regime, we select the finetuning checkpoint by FID rather than by loss. This FID is computed on a held-out validation split carved from the training data (disjoint from the test set), not on the test set; the lowest validation FID is at epoch 8, which we adopt. The choice is not critical: across all finetuning epochs the test-set FID spans only about 0.03, so the pipeline is insensitive to the exact epoch. The alpha predictor itself is trained and selected on a held-out split carved from the training set that never overlaps the test images.
Dataset and splits
We evaluate on VITON-HD
4
, the standard benchmark for image-based virtual try-on. It contains 13,679 frontal-view pairs of an upper-body person and the corresponding in-shop garment (tops only), split into 11,647 training pairs and 2,032 test pairs at 
\(1024 \times 768\)
; following TPD and prior work we run at 
\(512 \times 384\)
. Each pair also ships with the preprocessing our pipeline consumes: a parsed person, from which we extract the ground-truth garment mask, a DensePose map, and a flat garment cloth mask, so no extra annotation is needed. Evaluation follows the two standard settings: paired, where each person is re-dressed in their 
own
 garment, so a ground-truth photo exists and SSIM and LPIPS can be computed; and unpaired, where each person is dressed in a 
different
 garment, so no ground truth exists and we report FID. All reported numbers use the full 2,032-pair test set.
Within the training half of that split, each trained component is selected on its own held-out validation set carved from the training data, with zero test overlap. The mask predictor—both alpha and the SegFormer of “
Ablation: how the mask predictor was chosen
”, on the 
same
 split—is trained on 10,482 pairs and selected by validation IoU on a 1,165-pair set; the second-stage finetuning uses a separate 500-pair set for early stopping.
Metrics
We report four metrics, chosen so that no single one can carry the argument alone: two measure fidelity to a specific target, one measures realism of the output distribution, and one measures cost.
The Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)
27
, for which lower is better, measures whether generated images look like real photographs 
as a population
. Both sets are embedded by an InceptionV3 network
28
, each modelled as a Gaussian with mean 
\(\mu \)
 and covariance 
\(\Sigma \)
, and the two Gaussians are compared:
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{FID} = \Vert \mu _{r} - \mu _{g} \Vert _{2}^{2} + \textrm{Tr}\!\left( \Sigma _{r} + \Sigma _{g} - 2\left( \Sigma _{r}\Sigma _{g}\right) ^{1/2}\right) , \end{aligned}$$

                    (13)
                
with subscripts 
r
 and 
g
 for the real and generated sets. As a distribution-level measure it needs no image-to-image correspondence, so we use it on the unpaired order, where no ground-truth image exists.
Structural similarity (SSIM)
29
, for which higher is better, compares a generated image to its ground-truth photo over local sliding windows in terms of luminance, contrast and structure rather than raw pixel differences:
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{SSIM}(a, b) = \frac{\left( 2\mu _{a}\mu _{b} + C_{1}\right) \left( 2\sigma _{ab} + C_{2}\right) }{\left( \mu _{a}^{2} + \mu _{b}^{2} + C_{1}\right) \left( \sigma _{a}^{2} + \sigma _{b}^{2} + C_{2}\right) }, \end{aligned}$$

                    (14)
                
where 
\(\mu \)
, 
\(\sigma ^{2}\)
 and 
\(\sigma _{ab}\)
 are the local means, variances and covariance of the two windows 
a
 and 
b
, and 
\(C_{1}, C_{2}\)
 stabilise the ratio; the score is averaged over all windows.
Learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS)
30
, for which lower is better, also compares against the ground-truth photo, but in the feature space of a pretrained network, which tracks human judgement better than pixel differences:
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{LPIPS}(a, b) = \sum _{l} \frac{1}{H_{l}W_{l}} \sum _{h,w} \left\Vert w_{l} \odot \left( \hat{\phi }^{l}_{hw}(a) - \hat{\phi }^{l}_{hw}(b) \right) \right\Vert _{2}^{2}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (15)
                
where 
\(\hat{\phi }^{l}\)
 are the unit-normalised activations of layer 
l
 (AlexNet
31
 backbone) and 
\(w_{l}\)
 are learned per-channel weights. Both SSIM and LPIPS need a ground-truth image, so both use the paired order.
For cost, we report wall-clock latency to generate one image, measured on a single idle RTX A4000 and averaged over the test set. Timing covers only the model’s own inference: for our method, the alpha predictor’s forward pass plus the second-stage DDIM loop and the decode; for TPD, both DDIM loops. All preprocessing time is excluded.
Evaluation protocol
Having defined the metrics, we state how they are computed. FID is reported on the unpaired setting (person plus a different garment) and SSIM and LPIPS on the paired setting (person re-dressed in their own garment, so a ground-truth photo exists). FID is sensitive to JPEG quality: generated images are saved at quality 75, the library default used by TPD, so we compute FID against a reference resized to the same quality 75. Using quality 95 inflates FID by roughly 3.3 points.
We reproduce the two-stage TPD on the same machine and protocol so all comparisons are directly equivalent. We report against both the paper’s published numbers and our reproduction; the latter isolates the cost of our method from environment and seed drift.
Implementation details
The alpha predictor is trained for 25 epochs with batch size 8 and learning rate 
\(2 \times 10^{-4}\)
 on a single RTX A4000; its training and validation curves are shown in Fig. 
7
, and we select the epoch-23 checkpoint by validation IoU. Second-stage finetuning is run on eight RTX A4000 GPUs and reuses TPD’s original training setup, with three changes: we lower the learning rate from 
\(10^{-5}\)
 to 
\(10^{-6}\)
, a smaller step that lets the network adapt to the new mask distribution without overwriting the garment-synthesis ability learned in pretraining, that is to avoid catastrophic forgetting; we add a 200-step warmup with early stopping; and we restrict updates to the first input block (“
Second-stage finetuning
”). At inference, the second stage uses DDIM with 100 steps; timing is measured on a single idle RTX A4000.

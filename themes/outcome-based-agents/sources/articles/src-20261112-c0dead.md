    Commuters say Central Street CTA station remains inaccessible, call for ADA compliance

 

Daily File photo by Emma Richman
 
Of the nine CTA Purple Line stations between Linden and Howard, only three are ADA accessible.
 

George Koutrouvelis
 and 
Clara Xi
 

November 12, 2026

Despite undergoing two major renovations since its construction in 1908, the Chicago Transit Authority’s Central Street Purple Line station still lacks accessible entrance options, presenting challenges for residents and those visiting the nearby Evanston Hospital. 

Of the nine CTA Purple Line stations between Linden and Howard, 
only three are ADA accessible
: Linden, Davis and Howard.

Even with recent renovations to its staircase, the Central Street station still does not comply with the Americans with Disabilities Act, forcing commuters like Chicago resident Christa Shields to climb stairs to reach the platform.

“There does need to be elevators, or at least escalators or something, because you do have a lot of people that come that be on canes and walkers, and it takes them a long time to get downstairs,” Shields said.

Shields travels through the Central Street station during her daily commute from Chicago’s Bronzeville neighborhood. After 26 years of riding to and from work, Shields said she would “love” if Evanston improved accessibility for senior citizens and those with disabilities.

Shields, who works at Evanston Hospital, said that the station’s lack of accessibility causes difficulty when physically impaired hospital visitors can’t access the platform.

“I know of some people that go to this hospital where they was like, ‘I would have to catch an Uber’ or wait for they siblings or someone to bring them here because of the (lack of an) elevator and because they can’t get down the steps,” Shields said.

The Central Street station falls along Chicago resident Clarence Hubbard’s commute to Highland Park Hospital.
 

Hubbard said he frequently sees hospital patients at Evanston Hospital requiring assistance to get up or down the station’s stairs.

“An elevator would help for people with disabilities,” he said. “As we know, CTA doesn’t have that right now, but it would be perfect for it to be implemented.”

In 2019, the CTA launched its Red and Purple Modernization, a station renovation initiative spanning the entirety of the area’s Red and Purple Lines. The RPM is the second largest capital improvement project in CTA history, amounting to $2.1 billion in funding at both the federal and local level.

The RPM renovation efforts tie into the CTA’s broader goal to make all stations ADA-compliant by 2038, according to a 2024 policy plan. 

According to a 2023 public meeting about the RPM’s next phases, the project hopes to add elevators for ADA accessibility, modify tracks for smoother rides and lengthen platforms to fit larger eight-car trains.

While initial RPM renovations began seven years ago, the Trump administration’s 
October 2025 freeze of federal CTA funding
 stifled promises of ADA accessibility.

The CTA filed a lawsuit against the U.S. Department of Transportation and the Federal Transit Administration in March seeking to reinstate its funding. While District Judge Thomas M. Durkin ruled later that month in favor of the CTA, funding for future RPM phases, including Central Street station renovations, remains uncertain.

Residents have also noted gaps in past CTA construction efforts.

“They put the new steps and stuff,” Shields said. “Everybody thought there was gonna be escalators.”

The Regional Transportation Authority announced its Access to Transit program in June 2026. The program allocated $810,000 for new signs and information displays, as well as a warming shelter on the Central Street station’s east platform for passengers waiting for inbound trains to Chicago. 

Chicago resident Rachelle Santos said she noticed the new heat lamps, but noted the station’s inaccessibility meant “the people who can’t get up here can’t take advantage of that benefit.”

She added that differences in accessibility among the city’s CTA stations directly affects commuters.

“I’m aware that Evanston Davis has ramps and elevators, so I think people who need those accommodations would have to go further if they live closer to this station,” Santos said.

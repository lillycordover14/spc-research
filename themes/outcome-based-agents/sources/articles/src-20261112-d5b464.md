On the Mountain

Horstman Glacier at Whistler Blackcomb, June and July 2026: Shaun led and James co-tested the 158 cm off the T-bar. Float was judged in deep, soupy afternoon slush, which shows how the shape handles heavy snow and nothing more.

In that slush the wide shovel nose and the tapered tail kept the front of the board riding high without anyone having to lean back hard.

It scored 9.0 for float, which is good but not the best here: the Fish 3D, Mind Expander and Orca floated better.

Firm snow is where it earned its place. It scored 9.5 for carving, the highest of the ten. The camber under the feet and the carbon inlay in the tail held long, laid-out carves on early morning hardpack, and Shaun saw zero tail wash.

Brand marketing classifies this alongside playful powder shapes, but it rides like a stiff, aggressive freeride charger. It lays down incredible trenches on firm morning snow, but if you relax your technique, it will overpower you.
Shaun

Rough snow was its other strength, with the best score of the ten at 9.0. The stiff flex and solid camber blasted straight through refrozen ruts and uneven chunder without being deflected off line.

Where It Asks Something of You

The Excavator wants an athletic rider. James noticed the wide footprint on firm morning transits, and tight, slow bumps took active leg work because the board is torsionally stiff and wide underfoot.

Starting a turn needed a deliberate push through the ankles, which is why agility is its lowest score at 8.5. Once the board was on edge, the tapered tail snapped out of tight turns with authority. Damage was minor: the sintered base took faint hairline scuffs from rut debris and the edges stayed true.

Spec Sheet vs Our Test

Volume shift.
 Wide waist first, claim second: on firm morning transits the footprint was obvious, and in slush the nose rode high, which is what the maker's volume shift (surface area moved from the tip and tail into the waist, to cut swing weight and toe and heel drag) is meant to buy. We didn't test toe or heel drag. For you, that means a board that wants to be ridden on edge rather than flat, with float that's good rather than the best.

Carbon Power Fork.
 The maker describes a pre-cambered carbon inlay in the tail for energy in and out of turns and extra pop for takeoffs and landings. Energy held up in Shaun's carves, which loaded and released with no tail wash. Pop didn't: James found the tail stiff and said it demands serious leg drive to generate air from small rollers. Buy it for carving energy, not playful pop.

Flex 6/10.
 A 6 out of 10 flex is the maker's number, listed alongside freeride and powder. Shaun found a stiff, aggressive charger rather than a playful powder shape, and James called the tail stiff, so on the glacier the board rode firmer than the rating sounds. That firmness is likely part of why rough snow scored the best of the ten, and why a relaxed rider will tire.

Should You Buy the K2 Excavator?

Don't buy the Excavator for its label. Shaun's point stands: the maker lists it for powder, and it rides like a freeride charger. It scored 90 in the mid-price tier, level with the Jones Mind Expander 2.0, and it carved harder than any other board here. For aggressive freeriders and powerhouse carvers who want soft-snow float and firm-snow grip in one deck, it's our pick.

A playful, forgiving ride isn't what this board sells. The Mind Expander 2.0 has a looser nose and almost the same float and carving (9.5 and 9.0, against the Excavator's 9.0 and 9.5), and the Dancehaul is the better pick for slush slashes and side hits. The Excavator's nose stayed up in slush, so we'd expect it to carry over to deeper snow, but that's our judgment, not a test.

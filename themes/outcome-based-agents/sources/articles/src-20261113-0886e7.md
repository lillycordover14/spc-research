“My friends and I have been in the same fantasy football league for 20 years. We’re all really close and we have a fantasy football trophy. Currently, I am the winner and I keep the trophy on the fridge in the garage. Coincidentally it’s right next to my dad, who is in an urn. When my mom died I had to take the urn and I just didn’t have the heart to just throw it away. Personally, I don’t even know why we have urns. That’s not them in there. Anyway, we decided next year we wanna get a new trophy. I thought of the wackiest most insane idea. What if my dad’s urn is the new trophy? I told my buddies and half of them laughed hysterically and the other half thought I was demented. What say you? Every time I think about it, I laugh so hard. Am I demented? Am I crazy? Is it hilarious? Is it all of the above? The idea of my dad traveling each year to someone else’s home as a fantasy football trophy is just gold.”

Most people eventually end up spreading their loved one’s ashes somewhere he or she loved.

Which is why, and this is a real thing, some people’s ashes end up getting poured out at college football stadiums around the country. Really. It’s also enough of an issue at Disney World that they’ve had to institute protocols related to this move. Why do people do that? Because they want to ultimately release their loved one’s ashes at a place that was important to them.

Which brings me back to your situation.

The holder of the urn basically has two questions to answer: what would make your loved one happy, in this case your dad, and what would make you happy as the holder of the ashes?

Right now, your dad’s ashes are in the garage, which doesn’t seem like a place of particular prominence.

So a couple of questions for you: did your dad have a good sense of humor? What would he think about becoming the fantasy football trophy?

This also then raises another question: who wants your dad’s ashes in their house? No way I want this. Everyone has kids, someone could knock this thing over and the next thing you know the ashes are a mess and have to get vaccummed up. And your dad’s remains end up in a garbage dump somewhere.

So here’s what I would suggest: take your dad’s ashes somewhere special for him and release them there. Then if you want to use his empty urn as the next fantasy football trophy, so be it.

By the way, is it old school to want a gravestone?

I get cemeteries aren’t necessarily great uses of land, but I like cemeteries. I think it’s cool to visit old cemeteries. They connect historically in ways the modern era doesn’t.

In fact, I actually think family cemeteries should make a come back.

We have a farm in 
Perry County, Tenn
., and there’s a family cemetary in the front yard of the house. The family still comes and takes care of it and people have been buried there for over 150 years. It’s still an active cemetary, every now and then a new funeral takes place. I don’t know how many people still have family plots or what the legality is now. Could I get buried in my backyard in 
Williamson County, Tenn
.? We have several acres. Does this violate Nashville area codes? What if you just buried someone in the front yard of your subdivision? Do HOAs forbid this? Do they come and dig up the body if you do it? Can you make your own coffin like back in the day?

I’m just picturing someone with a shovel doing the digging for the grave — this is brutal, hard work, by the way — people are out walking their dogs and you’re just busting your ass with a shovel working on the hole. There’s a tombstone propped up next to you on the front porch. Has anyone done this in a hundred years?

Anyway, good luck with picking a place for your dad’s ashes.

And with your fantasy football season.

Once you get married and have kids, especially multiple kids, no one has the time to be that social any more. If you’re around my age, scheduling a dinner or drink with friends can take months. We actually have an easier time sometime meeting up with friends from Nashville down on Florida’s 
30A

Toss in aging parents and there’s a stage of life where a lot of us have young kids and old parents, especially now when so many people wait until their upper 30s to have kids.

My kids are 18, 16 and 12 now and my parents and my wife’s parents are all 80 or over. Some of you reading this right now have really young kids and really old parents and that’s a tough, stressful combo to manage. I was 28 when my first kid was born so in six years my youngest kid will be off to college and I’ll have no idea what to do with all my time. But in the meantime that 24-year window, from when I was 28 and became a dad to 52 years old, when my kids will all be off to college, it’s really hard to have a serious full-time job, a spouse, and multiple kids and maintain friendships the same way you did before you had kids, especially if you have multiple kids.

I do know some guys are totally different people when they have girlfriends compared to when they don’t have girlfriends. They go all in with the girl, vanish into the relationship, and then suddenly show up again when the relationship inevitably falls apart. They want to hang out with the guys until they find another girl and then vanish all over again. That’s lame and it’s hard to be friends with guys like this.

But I would show him some grace.

Your friend may feel like you’re the dude who disappears when he gets a girlfriend and he probably thinks you’re way more fun without a girlfriend.

He’s probably right.

Have you ever met a guy who is more fun after he gets a controlling girlfriend who sucks up all his time? I haven’t.

I think most men overvalue our capabilities in situations like these. I also think on the dating apps most men figure being overconfident rather than underconfident likely leads to you finding them more attractive. So I think they probably exaggerate for attention and to try and impress you.

Truth be told as well, airline pilot is kind of a badass job. So you might be intimidating lots of guys. Most guys deep down still want to be the primary breadwinner in a household so when they hear what you do they may feel compelled to act like they are more capable than they actually are.

plane crash in Palm Beach

“I love what you have done for standing up for the anti-woke crowd and sports media.

But, I strongly disagree with you supporting the 24-team college football playoff

So why do you think it is a good idea for the playoff to expand considering what makes college football great and popular is the regular season and as you have pointed out ratings for the postseason don’t jump a lot?”

I’m surprised that anyone around my own age — those of us old enough to remember 1997, for instance, when Nebraska and Michigan both finished the season undefeated and we just voted them co-champs — is upset about any aspect of a playoff. For most of my youth and young adulthood, the idea we might ever have a true playoff in college football felt like a complete pipe dream. Now we’ve got it and we have it at a time when parity has never been greater in college football, there are more teams today that might win a college football playoff than ever before given how well distributed talent is, and people are upset by this?

I just don’t get it.

Worst-case scenario: it doesn’t work and they dial it back after five years. Best-case scenario: it’s wildly successful and people spend all of December obsessed with the College Football Playoff.

Let me explain why I feel this way.

First of all, the most successful sports league in the United States, by far, is the NFL.

And they play 17 regular season games to eliminate 20 teams, advancing 12 teams to the playoff, which is roughly 38% of the league.

A 24-team college football playoff will represent roughly 35% of the 68 teams in the power four conferences plus Notre Dame. If you toss in the non-power conference teams and count them too — that takes us to 128 — it’s an even smaller percentage overall, roughly 19% of FBS teams would make the playoff.

That’s a lower percentage of college football playoff teams than every pro sports league in America.

Every round of the NFL playoffs, the overall ratings increase, culminating in over 120 million viewers for the Super Bowl.

Major League Baseball, the NBA, the NHL, every pro sports league in the country experiences an increase in audience during the course of its playoff, often doubling, tripling or even quadrupling the opening round viewership.

College football doesn’t see this.

The highest rated college football game of all time is USC-Texas back in 2006. 35.6 million people watched this game, no other college football game in the 21st century has reached this viewership level.

In fact, the top-rated game can even occur before the title game.

Why is this?

I think it’s because many college football fans have actually tuned out by November. I’ve seen the evidence as someone who owns a media company. Every year the audience is super excited for the start of college football in August and then that enthusiasm continues in September and October before falling off a cliff in November. Why is that? I think it’s because by November most fans have seen their teams lose so many games that their optimism from September has vanished. Once you know your team can’t win a title, you lose interest.

With a 24-team playoff at least 50 fan bases, maybe more, will enter November thinking if their team runs the table they have a chance to make the playoff.

This also means that far from devaluing the regular season, many more games, by far, will matter in late November than matter now.

Right now there might be seven or eight games that have a real impact on the playoff by the final week of the season.

Next year, there will be 25 or more games that matter. That seems good to me.

Let’s see if I’m right.

The biggest issue I see with sports is cost.

This is what I hammered in my congressional testimony this past summer.

The games we watch have gotten insanely expensive while the televisions we watch them on have actually gotten cheaper. Credit to the 
New York Times Business
 section from a couple of weeks ago for this data. A mid-size Sony television in 1986 was $1,156 in today’s dollars. Now it’s $399.99. So TVs have become 70% cheaper over the past 30 years.

That’s extraordinary and a testament to capitalism.

But how about the games themselves? Well, a ticket to the 1986 Super Bowl was $213 in today’s dollars. Now it’s $3,316. So the markup there is 15.6x as expensive.

They actually included tuition at the University of Tennessee too. UT cost $1,105 a semester in 1986. Now it’s $7,012 a semester. That’s an increase of 6.3x in the past 30 years.

My question would be, how do we make the rest of society more like TVs and less like the Super Bowl?

Now to be fair Super Bowl tickets are a limited commodity and TVs are a large quantity business so these aren’t directly comparable goods. If they could make a Super Bowl with a million-seat stadium capacity they could probably sell it out, but the price would be way cheaper on average. But the rate of increase of the ticket price is crazy compared to the rest of society. This is also probably a function of largely eliminating scalpers from the ticket market. When I was a kid, when you went to a game, there were scalpers lined up everywhere around the stadium with huge books of tickets. Now you almost never see them. All tickets have moved online and there basically is never a sellout, if you have the money you can always buy a ticket to pretty much any event, sports, concert or otherwise.

It’s impossible to be a normal person with a normal salary and be able to take your family of four to most sporting events without paying an arm and a leg.

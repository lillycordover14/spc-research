Abstract
Traffic congestion has major social, environmental, and economic consequences, and adaptive traffic signal control is one approach for improving intersection performance under changing demand. This study presents a reinforcement-learning-based controller for a single isolated intersection and compares its performance with a firefly-based heuristic. The main contribution is a normalized composite reward that combines queue length and vehicle waiting time. These measures are correlated, but they describe different operational aspects of intersection performance: queue length reflects spatial occupancy and potential spillback, whereas waiting time reflects the temporal delay experienced by vehicles. A weighting parameter (β) is used to vary the relative emphasis assigned to the two measures. The proposed reward was compared with queue-only and waiting-time-only rewards under the tested simulation conditions. The results indicate that the composite reward can provide a tunable compromise between the two measures, although it does not consistently outperform the single-metric rewards for every performance indicator and traffic-demand level.

Introduction
Private car ownership and usage are rising extremely quickly in metropolitan areas. The organization of motor vehicle makers reports that the sales of various vehicle kinds are on the rise, increasing by roughly 3% annually

2

Therefore, researchers and practitioners are always seeking a solution to the traffic congestion problem. One of the ways which can be used to control the congestion on the roads, is to control the timing of traffic signals at intersections. In this work, the aim is to find a good solution to the traffic congestion problem based on controlling the timing of traffic signals, which is defined as the urban traffic signals scheduling problem. In the urban traffic signal scheduling problem, it is desired to get the optimum schedule for each considered traffic signal to maximize or minimize a specific objective function(s); these schedules determine the active and inactive traffic phases during each cycle time. Different strategies have been proposed to schedule traffic signals, typically, a static strategy is implemented. A predetermined fixed duration for each signal in an intersection or network is implemented in such a strategy. Although this strategy is typically applied in most traditional traffic systems, it is inappropriate in the case of dynamic traffic flow. That is why many adaptive and intelligent traffic signal control strategies have been developed to overcome these limitations
3
.
Traffic signal control systems
Traffic signal control is one of the most logical approaches to tackle congestion through measures affecting it. The traffic signal control techniques aim to control the flow of vehicles at intersections and connected networks. This control is achieved by finding the best signal control strategy. Some of the goals of traffic signal control techniques are increasing the throughput of an intersection, decreasing delays, and ensuring the safety of people by preventing conflicts that may cause accidents and disturbances at the intersection
4
.
The traffic light controller must make two sequence control decisions, selecting the current phase among the possible phases and the duration. The function of the controller is to serve all the users of the intersection at the roads by organizing the movements and preventing accidents and disturbance on the roads. The control system model is based on historical data, if it is a fixed time system or based on a responsive model in the adaptive control mechanism model in which the controls system can automatically select the phases. The classical method is the fixed time controller, which applies a fixed schedule with fixed phases sequence and durations. This method is not applicable to fluctuating changes in the traffic flow, so the most practical approach nowadays is adaptive traffic control. The adaptive traffic control works on finding the adaptive optimum schedule, which optimizes the traffic control metrics. These metrics will be discussed in the following section.

Real-time traffic signal control strategies
Two major traffic control strategies could be used to tackle the intersection traffic signal control problem, the fixed-time and adaptive or responsive control strategy. In the fixed-time control system, the system parameters such as the cycle length, the phase splits, and the phases sequence are specified in advance based on historical data. This type of control system is suitable when the flow of an intersection shows no or low changes over time. On the other hand, all the previously mentioned parameters are not fixed in the traffic-responsive control system. All these parameters change, and update based on the change in the traffic flow behavior, and it is widely used nowadays. The scope of this work is the adaptive traffic signal control, which represents a real-time dynamic control system.
The current study does not propose a new DQN algorithm. Its contribution is the evaluation of a normalized composite reward that combines queue length and vehicle waiting time. Although the two measures are correlated, they are not operationally identical: queue length is associated with approach occupancy, storage use, and spillback risk, whereas waiting time represents the duration of delay experienced by vehicles. The parameter β controls the relative emphasis assigned to these two measures rather than implying that they are statistically independent objectives.

Approximate and metaheuristic methods
The optimal scheduling of traffic signals has become increasingly crucial in addressing traffic congestion. Despite its importance, the literature on adaptive traffic control systems is limited, and many existing studies rely on approximate solution methods. Metaheuristics offer a promising alternative, providing high-quality near-optimal solutions at a reasonable computational cost compared to traditional optimization techniques. Population-based metaheuristics are primarily categorized into Evolutionary Algorithms (EA) and Swarm Intelligence (SI) algorithms, both of which share a similar framework for optimization. Initially, all potential solutions in the population are generated either randomly or through simple greedy heuristics, with each solution’s decision variables confined to their respective search domains. These initial solutions are then evaluated using an objective function specific to the problem. An iterative process follows, generating new solutions through various nature-inspired mechanisms. Over the past two decades, these algorithms have been applied to a range of smart city applications, including traffic signal scheduling problems

In the era of Industry 4.0 and the expansion of information and communication technology, transportation systems have become a critical component for the success of Industry 4.0 initiatives. Reducing travel times is essential to enhance system performance. Machine Learning (ML) serves as a fundamental technology for developing Industry 4.0 applications. Within the realm of traffic signal scheduling, ML is crucial for realizing the advantages of Industry 4.0. Reinforcement learning has emerged as a particularly promising technique for traffic control due to its effective performance in managing traffic systems

Machine learning is a branch of artificial intelligence that enables systems to learn from data, improve their performance, and adapt to new situations without explicit programming. It encompasses algorithms that can recognize patterns, make decisions, and predict outcomes based on experience. Machine learning techniques are categorized into three main types: Supervised Learning, which involves training algorithms on labeled data to map inputs to outputs and includes methods like Linear Regression and Neural Networks; Unsupervised Learning, which deals with unlabeled data to uncover hidden patterns or structures, employing techniques such as K-Means Clustering and Principal Component Analysis (PCA); and Reinforcement Learning, where algorithms learn to make decisions by interacting with an environment and receiving rewards or penalties, with methods including Q-Learning and Deep Q-Networks. These techniques are particularly effective in solving complex problems and performing well in dynamic and unstable environments where traditional approaches may falter
39

Classification and regression models are two fundamental types of supervised learning. In supervised learning, algorithms are trained using labeled data, where both input and output pairs are provided, enabling the algorithm to learn the relationship between them and make predictions. Classification involves categorizing data into predefined classes, such as spam versus non-spam emails, while regression predicts continuous values, such as forecasting house prices. In contrast, unsupervised learning deals with unlabeled data, where the algorithm must identify and describe underlying patterns or structures within the data without predefined labels. For example, clustering algorithms group similar data points together to reveal hidden structures or segments, such as customer demographics in market analysis

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a machine learning technique that controls an environment based on its feedback. An adaptive intelligent approach maintains an agent targeting system to maximize a cumulative long-run reward function. For a problem to be controlled using the RL technique, an agent repeatedly observes the state of the environment (S). It takes action (A) based on its behavior policy (π); after that, it receives a feedback reward (R) for the action taken, which will be accumulated to its long-run reward value (V). The RL agent optimizes the policy (π) during the learning process, it keeps adjusting its policy by maximizing the expectation of the long-term reward until it converges to the optimal policy (π∗). Q, the state-action value function or the function for a given policy π, measures the expected cumulative reward so that the optimal policy gives the optimum Q value

Methodology
Problem statement
This study aims to develop and evaluate an adaptive traffic signal control strategy for a single isolated intersection. The controller coordinates the signal phases to support safe and efficient vehicle movement under changing demand. A deep Q-learning agent is applied with three alternative reward definitions: a waiting-time reward, a queue-length reward, and a normalized composite reward. The composite formulation is used to examine whether changing the relative emphasis between two correlated but operationally distinct traffic measures influences the learned control policy.

Reward functions
The action that has been chosen should improve the traffic situation (i.e., State) by enhancing the traffic performance metrics (e.g., waiting time, delay, and queue length). The reward function is used to estimate the feedback from the environment (i.e., traffic metrics) as a result of taking a specific action.
Vehicle waiting time and queue length are both widely used measures of signalized-intersection performance. They are positively related because longer queues often lead to longer delays; however, they describe different operational consequences. Queue length is an instantaneous spatial measure that is relevant to approach storage and spillback, whereas waiting time is a temporal measure experienced by individual vehicles and can reveal prolonged delay or phase starvation. The purpose of combining the two measures is therefore not to treat them as independent objectives, but to investigate how the learned controller changes when greater emphasis is placed on spatial queue management or temporal delay performance. Queue length and waiting time were included because they represent related but operationally different measures of traffic performance. Queue length indicates the number of stopped vehicles and the use of available approach storage, while waiting time represents the accumulated temporal delay experienced by vehicles. Because the measures have different units and magnitudes, each term was normalized before combination. Q_ref denotes the reference or expected maximum queue length, and W_ref denotes the reference or expected maximum waiting time. These reference values were selected from the expected operating range of the simulation environment and remained fixed during training. The parameter β specifies the relative emphasis assigned to the normalized queue term, while (1 − β) specifies the relative emphasis assigned to the normalized waiting-time term. The weighting parameter is used to vary the relative contribution of the two normalized performance measures in the composite reward.
Deep Q-network agent
The controller uses a deep Q-network (DQN) to approximate the action-value function Q(s, a;θ), where s is the observed traffic state, a is one of the four signal-phase actions, and θ denotes the neural-network parameters. For each observed state, the network produces four Q-values, and the action-selection policy chooses an action according to the exploration strategy used during training. Experience replay is used to sample previously observed transitions during network updates.
The implemented network is fully connected, with an 80-neuron input layer, five hidden layers containing 400 neurons each, and a four-neuron output layer with linear activation. Each output corresponds to the estimated Q-value of one signal-phase action. The discount factor γ is 0.75 and the learning rate is 0.001. Table 
2
 summarizes the neural-network architecture and training configuration used in the experiments.

version

Queue length is widely used as a reward signal in traffic-signal control. For the benchmark comparison, the RL controller was evaluated against the Firefly-based heuristic proposed by Abohashima et al.

The agent was trained for 100 episodes. Each episode represented 1.5 h of simulated traffic and contained 5,400 one-second simulation steps, giving 540,000 environment steps and 150 simulated hours across training. These quantities describe simulated exposure rather than wall-clock computing time. Experience replay was used during training so that transitions were stored and sampled in mini-batches rather than being used only once in chronological order.

Limitations and future work
This study has some limitations, and it will be considered in our future work. First, the proposed reward formulation was evaluated at one isolated intersection, so the findings cannot yet be generalized to coordinated networks or different intersection geometries. Second, the experiments considered vehicle movements only and did not include pedestrian phases, cyclists, emergency vehicles, or public-transport priority. Third only a limited set of action durations and three β values were tested. Future work should address these limitations through multi-seed experiments, denser sensitivity analyses and network-level and pedestrian-aware extensions.
Conclusion
This paper developed a reinforcement-learning model to adapt traffic signal control in an isolated intersection. It controls the traffic flow dynamically with the change of traffic conditions. The RL agent is implemented using Python script as a controller with many options.
This paper proposed a comparative study between three adaptive traffic signal control methods. The three methods were developed to control the traffic signals. First, a proposed mathematical model that considers the problem’s physical constraints was developed. The model objective was to minimize the number of vehicles waiting at the intersection. Second, a firefly algorithm was adopted to solve the problem effectively and efficiently, overcoming the mathematical model’s large computational time. The third model was formulating the traffic signal control problem as a reinforcement-learning problem by representing the system’s state, determining all possible actions.
In the reinforcement-learning model, two commonly used single-metric rewards were evaluated together with a normalized composite reward that combines vehicle waiting time and queue length.
The proposed formulation uses the weighting parameter β to vary the relative emphasis assigned to normalized queue length and normalized waiting time. These two measures are correlated, but they reflect different operational concerns at a signalized intersection. The sensitivity analysis therefore investigates how the learned policy changes when the controller places greater emphasis on approach occupancy and spillback-related queue performance or on vehicle delay. The results show that the composite reward does not consistently achieve the best value for every metric and traffic-demand level. Its main benefit is the ability to provide a tunable compromise between average and extreme queue and waiting-time measures under the tested conditions.
The study uses a fixed deep Q-learning architecture to evaluate alternative reward definitions, with the principal contribution focusing on composite reward design. Further work should validate the findings using additional demand patterns, multiple random seeds, statistical uncertainty estimates, real traffic data, and multi-intersection environments.

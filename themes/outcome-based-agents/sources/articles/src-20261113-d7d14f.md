Choose your own agent, says Zendesk - and pay us on outcomes&nbsp;&nbsp;diginomica

[page text]
Shashi Upadhyay, Zendesk, speaks at Relate 2026

It's barely six months since 
Zendesk's last major set of product announcements
. This week brings another big launch, adding new support for connections from external AI agents — including agentic support queries on behalf of consumers — and the roll-out of an outcome-based pricing model for 
what it now calls the Outcome AI Platform
, giving a new context for its recently introduced specialized agents.

One of the most significant announcements is Zendesk Bridge, a new platform through which customers can connect to Zendesk from external agents such as ChatGPT and Claude, rather than having to fire up the application to find information or get things done. This reflects the broader market trend towards headless access to SaaS applications from AI-native model providers, but Shashi Upadhyay, President of Products Engineering & AI at Zendesk, says this goes further:

I think the way people talk about headless, it's often like, 'I'm just going to repackage all my APIs and call it headless.' That is not what we have in mind. What we have in mind is, it's actually a full-on development platform.

Zendesk Bridge, he explains, is a platform where customers can build their own apps on top of Zendesk, such as a customer support app, or where agents or people using AI assistants can access information or progress actions. He goes on:

It's three things. It's individual users using ChatGPT. It is builders building applications or agents, and it's AI agents trying to get their work done.

As well as APIs that agents can call, it manages what they can see and do, and provides access to the application’s underlying workflows and context. He explains:

At a very base level, it's all our APIs packaged together in a secure and managed way. It's the MCP interface. It's the A2A interface. It's also a CLI or command line interface, which allows you to program it. And the most important thing about it is that it's completely managed. Whatever permissions that you have inside a company — what you're allowed to see, what you're not allowed to see — all of that inherits. It inherits that directly, and it makes every workflow, all the data inside of Zendesk, all the analysis that may happen, all of that available through any interface that you want.

Agent capabilities

Providing agentic access doesn't mean that everyone is moving away from the classic interface. Intensive users of Zendesk, such as people in call centers, will still find a lot of value in using the app, he explains:

We believe the Zendesk interface, it's highly optimized for the human agent, especially since we have added a bunch of AI capabilities to that interface in a very visual, easy-to-use form.

I'll just give you an example. If you're on a call, as you're interacting with the user, it can auto recommend the response, and then you can edit it if you want to. You can have conversations where the agent and the end user are speaking totally different languages. Those things are highly optimized for the agent's workday, the kind of things that they work on. If there's a ticket, it auto creates like, 'Hey, here are 10 other customers that had a similar problem, and this is how it was solved.' I think that there is so much value embedded in that, that I don't believe the interface is going to go away for the human agent. I actually think AI supercharges it, superpowers it.

The trend towards using agents to find information and get things done has reached the consumer space, too. Personal AI agents such as Instinct and Meta Muse can handle tasks on behalf of consumers such as booking travel, sending emails, shopping — and getting support. Upadhyay comments:

We believe that these agents will actually become the primary way in which people will consume support. Meaning, instead of calling for support or calling a contact center and saying, 'Hey, can you change my flight, etc., etc,' you're just going to tell your personal agent to go take care of it, and it'll do that.

To help these agents find the right support resources, Zendesk is promoting Open Service Directory (OSD), an open specification and public directory that helps AI assistants find a brand’s verified support channel. Businesses register their channels and control what information they share and when human review is required. Alongside Zendesk, initial participants include Betterment, ElevenLabs, Flex, TeamSystem, TBA Auctions, Telekom Payment SA and Wireless Logic. Upadhyay explains:

It's a way for you to find the company that you want to talk to, where the company has themselves pointed you to that particular thing...

We actually want bot-to-bot service to happen, but let's keep the system clean. If we don't do something like this, then all the fraud, the phishing, all that stuff enters the space and mucks it up, and then it doesn't achieve the potential it's meant to achieve.

The recent launch of specialized industry and custom agents
 introduces agents that come with pre-built expertise in sectors such as e-commerce, banking, healthcare and technology. These use Zendesk's experience of working in these markets and shortcut the process of developing and training agents with the specific domain expertise and service workflows of these industries. Upadhyay comments:

If you don't provide the right context, the right guardrails, the right constraints, what the agent is allowed to do and not allowed to do within a specific context, it doesn't work for the customer. We have, now, almost 20 years of data that we have collected over time across industries, across geographies, across verticals, because we have the ticket histories. We found that was very useful to train the model one vertical at a time, so that it became like an expert on that, and then build the context, the guardrails around that are tied to that industry.

Then what you get is like you get an employee who already has a banking experience, you get an employee who already has insurance experience, on day one. We don't have to spend like a year, a year and a half training them to do the basic job.

Outcome-based pricing

He adds that the shift to outcome-based pricing has given the vendor an extra incentive to introduce ready-made agents that can lift resolution rates. He explains:

When SaaS was horizontal, and we were not on the hook for the outcomes, we left all that the problem of verticalization, specialization, to our customers to go solve. They would write custom code, do custom integrations, etc. But now that we are on the hook to provide the outcomes — because we don't get paid if we don't provide the outcomes — it's our problem... It's totally in my interest now as a SaaS provider to verticalize these solutions.

Six months ago, the talk was all about Zendesk's 'Resolution Platform', a product rebrand that had been introduced a year prior 
to emphasize the shift away from simply closing tickets to actually resolving customer issues
. That emphasis hasn't changed, says Upadhyay, but talking now about an 'Outcome Platform' allows it to speak to functions where 'resolution' may not be the right measure of success. He explains:

As our platform has expanded beyond customer service to include adjacent back-office, mid-office problems, and employee service, we found that customers don't always think in terms of resolution. A good example is, now we have customers who are using our product to do collections. In collections, resolution is not really the right measure. That's not how they think about success. They think about success in terms of how many dollars have I collected of the amount that was due?

This of course creates a new challenge — how to define the right outcomes to price on in each individual case? He explains this is often a case of asking the customer what KPIs they themselves use to measure success.

How do you define an outcome? We have found it useful to just go to the customer and say, 'Okay, how are you measured?' Whatever their metrics are, they become our metrics...

The way people think about outcomes in a human setting is the way we think about outcomes in a machine setting.

My take

The rapid evolution at Zendesk reflects the pace at which the technology continues to evolve. There's a growing eagerness among enterprises to use general-purpose agents to access enterprise functions, but vendors need to do more than simply set up an MCP gateway if they want to continue providing value from the underlying application infrastructure. If they can't expose the specialized understanding of how enterprises use their capabilities — increasingly down to the specific requirements of individual industry verticals — then they lose their differentiation. Zendesk clearly understands this.

I was intrigued to hear Upadhyay argue that the move to outcome-based pricing is incentivizing the vendor to maximize the effectiveness of agents. I can remember back in the day when SaaS vendors used to argue that the need to earn a subscription renewal every year meant they were more attentive to customer success than the client-server software vendors they replaced. Now AI's outcome-based pricing has moved the goalposts even closer to truly understanding what success means for the customer and both demonstrating and measuring value delivered. It's a welcome development that aligns with 
my long-term views on the true meaning of customer success
.

Talking of true value for customers, I'm also intrigued by the notion of consumer AI assistants that will actually take over the hassle of chasing down answers from vendor support teams. Why should I sit on hold for half an hour to speak to someone in the support call center, when my agent can do it for me? If this actually works, it could really transform the support function. But I can also imagine a dystopian future in which my AI assistant only half-understands what I'm trying to achieve and is autonomously interacting with a support agent that doesn't have access to the resources it needs to resolve this particular edge case. So let's see how this works out in practice.

The number of U.S. newborns diagnosed with congenital syphilis rose from an estimated 597 in 2012 to 3,325 in 2022, a nearly sixfold increase, according to a Johns Hopkins University analysis of 14.6 million birth hospitalizations published in 
Clinical Infectious Diseases
. The rate climbed from 16.0 to 94.7 per 100,000 birth hospitalizations, 
CIDRAP reported
.
Congenital syphilis happens when a 
pregnant person
 with untreated syphilis passes the infection to the baby. It can cause miscarriage, stillbirth, 
premature birth
, low birth weight, lasting health problems, and death shortly after birth.
Most cases are preventable. A blood test during pregnancy and timely penicillin treatment can stop transmission, which makes nearly every case a missed chance to protect a baby and a question every expectant parent can raise at the next prenatal visit.
Sharpest Increases in Low-Income and Native Communities
Rates rose in every racial and ethnic group the researchers studied, as well as across income levels and hospital settings. The steepest climb was among American Indian and Alaska Native newborns, from 22.7 to 486.0 per 100,000, about a 21-fold increase, though the estimate is imprecise because the group is small.
By raw numbers in 2022, Hispanic newborns accounted for about 1,010 cases, Black newborns for 970 and White newborns for 785. Rates were highest among babies from the lowest-income ZIP codes, at 186.8 per 100,000, and among newborns with public insurance, at 177.4 per 100,000.
Geography mattered too. The West had the highest rate in 2022, at 126.0 per 100,000, while the South accounted for more than half of affected birth hospitalizations that year, about 1,682.
The researchers pointed to limited access to prenatal care, especially in rural areas, along with federal funding cuts to family-planning programs and shortages of the drug used to treat syphilis in pregnancy. The authors wrote that it is 
"critically important to reverse the rise"
 in congenital syphilis.
The Count Likely Misses Some Babies
The study has important gaps, and most of them point toward a larger toll. It counted only in-hospital births, so stillbirths and other pregnancy losses caused by syphilis were excluded.
It also left out Indian Health Service hospitals, which handle about 10% of American Indian and Alaska Native births. That likely means cases among Native newborns were undercounted even as their rates rose fastest.
The data stop in 2022 and come from four snapshot years: 2012, 2016, 2019 and 2022. More recent federal surveillance shows the problem has not reversed. Provisional CDC data show about 4,000 congenital syphilis cases in 2024, a nearly 2% rise from 2023 and the 12th straight annual increase, according to a 
CDC release on newborn syphilis
.
The two counts are not directly comparable. The Hopkins team used hospital discharge records, while the CDC tallies cases reported by health departments.
"While the STI epidemic may be turning a corner, we must accelerate progress and stop its most tragic consequences," 
said Dr. Bradley Stoner, director of the CDC's Division of STD Prevention, when the 2024 figures were released.
Testing and Treatment That Stop Transmission
Most cases trace back to a missed test or missed treatment. In 2022, lack of timely testing and adequate treatment during pregnancy contributed to 88% of U.S. congenital syphilis cases, according to a 
CDC Vital Signs report
.
The CDC recommends syphilis testing at the first prenatal visit, with repeat testing at about 28 weeks and at delivery for people at higher risk, those in areas with high syphilis rates and anyone not tested earlier. The 
American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists goes further
, recommending testing at all three points for everyone.
Many people with syphilis have no symptoms, or a painless sore that goes unnoticed. That is why testing matters even for people who feel healthy or are in a long-term relationship.
The recommended treatment in pregnancy is benzathine penicillin G, sold as Bicillin L-A, given as one to three shots depending on how long the infection has been present. Supplies have been strained for several years, a shortage the study authors cited among the reasons for the rise. The FDA has extended the use dates on 21 lots of Bicillin L-A to help supply, according to its 
list of extended use dates
, last updated Nov. 4.
Partners need testing and treatment too, ideally at the same time as the pregnant person. A person who is treated during pregnancy can be reinfected by an untreated partner, putting the baby at risk again.
Steps for Expectant Parents and Families
Anyone pregnant can ask their clinician whether they have been tested for syphilis, when the test was done, and whether they need another one later in pregnancy. Checking the result in a patient portal, or asking for a copy, helps if care moves to a new clinic or hospital.
People who start prenatal care late, or have none, can still be tested at urgent care, an emergency department, or a local health department. Many health departments offer free or low-cost syphilis testing and treatment, and Medicaid covers prenatal care, including testing, for eligible pregnant people.
A baby born to a parent with syphilis may look healthy at birth. Pediatricians may test and treat the newborn, and follow-up visits are important even when the baby seems well.
Signs in a pregnant person that need prompt care include a new sore on the genitals, mouth or anus, a rash on the palms or soles, or swollen glands. Anyone diagnosed with syphilis should finish the full course of treatment and tell recent partners so they can be tested.
The researchers called for expanded prenatal screening, better access to timely treatment and a reliable drug supply. MedicalDaily will report when the CDC releases its 2025 congenital syphilis figures.

Key Questions Answered
How much did congenital syphilis rise?
 From an estimated 597 newborns in 2012 to 3,325 in 2022, a nearly sixfold increase in hospital records.
Who was most affected?
 American Indian and Alaska Native newborns had the steepest rate increase. Babies from low-income areas and with public insurance had the highest rates.
Can congenital syphilis be prevented?
 Yes. Testing during pregnancy and timely penicillin treatment can prevent transmission to the baby.
When should pregnant people be tested?
 At the first prenatal visit, and again at about 28 weeks and at delivery for those at higher risk. ACOG recommends all three for everyone.
Is the count complete?
 No. The study excluded stillbirths and Indian Health Service hospitals, so the true number is likely higher.
Where can I get tested?
 Through a prenatal clinician, urgent care, or a local health department, many of which offer low-cost testing.

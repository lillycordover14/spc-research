A name like a photocopier, but a definite bargain. Why is this cut-price SUV flying under the radar?
15 November 2026
 | By 
Patrick Jackson
Join the conversation
1
Is this budget-friendly Mahindra a bargain hiding in plain sight? Photo: Patrick Jackson.

Unless you’ve been living under a rock all year, you’ll no doubt be aware that Australia is currently facing a cost-of-living crisis.

After all, the only thing the TV news can talk about right now is the cost-of-living crisis or 
the collapse of David Jones
. Or about why the collapse of David Jones is because of the cost-of-living crisis.

You’d think, then, that people in need of a new set of wheels would flock to buy more affordable cars at a time like this – and certainly, the numbers suggest that they are.

Just not this one, it seems.

Maybe it’s because this tiny SUV has a name like a photocopier. Maybe it’s because it still has a petrol engine. Maybe because it’s one of the few cars at this end of the market that 
isn’t
 from China.

Whatever the case, this car is an absolute bargain hiding in plain sight.

Diminutive proportions also promise a diminutive price tag, with the XUV 3XO being one of the most affordable SUVs on the market right now. Photo: Patrick Jackson.

What is it?

This is the most affordable model from Indian manufacturer Mahindra, the XUV 3XO, although the one you see here is the upscale variant.

That makes this the XUV 3XO AX7L. I told you it had a name like a photocopier.

Yet it’s closer in price to a big office photocopier than most SUVs. The entry-level AX5L version normally runs out the door for $23,990 driveaway, while the AX7L is $26,990 driveaway. There’s an all-black Anniversary Model for a little extra spend, too.

However, until the end of December 2026, Mahindra will sell you 2025-built AX5L models for as little as $22,490 driveaway, increasing to $25,490 for a 2025-built AX7L like this one.

Even if it’s a technicality, that base model deal makes it the cheapest SUV you can buy in Australia right now, beating the MG ZS Vibe ($22,990 driveaway) and its chief rival, the Chery Tiggo 4 Pro ($23,990 driveaway). It’s also considerably less than the 
Kia Stonic
 or 
Suzuki Fronx
.

That Mahindra has enough 2025-plated stock to offer such a deal says a lot about how the XUV 3XO is faring against its successful competition, though.

Mahindra doesn’t report its sales figures to the Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries (FCAI), but it announced that in 2025, it sold 4653 vehicles across its entire Australian range.

By comparison, FCAI figures show the Chery Tiggo 4 Pro alone sold 20,149 units in 2025. This year, that number was already beaten by the end of October, with year-to-date sales of 22,043 units. The MG ZS isn’t too far off those marks, either.

On the surface, it’s hard to see why the Mahinda is so overlooked in comparison. It’s quite a smart-looking thing, aside from the lurid Citrine Yellow paintwork of this example.

It’s ideally sized for city driving, able to fit in any carpark with ease. The extra ride height also makes it ‘so easy to get in and out of, you know’ (one for the 
Top Gear
 tragics out there).

It isn’t spartan, either. Cars are far from the most successful part of the Mahindra empire; Mahindra’s main success is as the world’s largest tractor manufacturer by volume, but don’t think that means it feels agricultural inside.

This AX7L version has a panoramic sunroof, leatherette upholstery, a cooled glovebox, a 360-degree camera with a handy blind-spot angle, and even a Harman Kardon premium audio system.

It’s starting to sound like pretty good value now, right?

The interior is basic, which for plenty of people is perfectly fine. Photo: Patrick Jackson.

What’s it like to live with?

There’s something to be said for the relatively analogue approach Mahindra has taken with the XUV 3XO.

Almost every key function is still controlled by a physical button, but there’s still just enough tech integration.

There are two 10.25-inch screens, one for the driver’s instrument cluster and one for the infotainment display. Both displays are impressively crisp and modern in design.

What’s great is that to change the volume or temperature, you don’t have to interact with either of them. Dials and buttons, yes please! Put anyone behind the wheel of this car, and they’ll find every control exactly where they’d expect it.

That said, the downside of this car’s budget price tag is that the fit and finish leave a little to be desired. Some of the trim wasn’t as tidy as you’d like, particularly on the dashboard. The exterior passenger-side window seal seemed to be disintegrating. Even the badges on the back aren’t quite straight.

It’s also a bit of a mixed bag on the road, though highly promising for how well suited it is to city driving.

Powered by a tiny 1.2-litre three-cylinder engine, mercifully fitted with a turbocharger, its 85 kW power output is meagre, though 200 Nm of torque is handy.

It’s no surprise Mahindra doesn’t quote a 0-100 km/h time, although it does quote how quickly it gets from 0-60 km/h: 4.6 seconds. That tells you exactly what this car is suited to.

Like all three-cylinder engines, it has plenty of character and makes a nice thrum. It can definitely get moving at city speeds, but runs out of puff pretty quickly beyond that.

There might be three drive modes with funny names – Zip, Zap, and Zoom – but none make much of a difference either way.

The six-speed automatic transmission is a bit of a highlight, though, being a brand-name Aisin unit which performs excellently. It’s a shame the same can’t be said about its suspension, as its rudimentary torsion-beam rear suspension somewhat spoils an otherwise decent drive.

Still, were you really expecting the ultimate driving machine for this tiny sum? It’s perfectly serviceable as a city runabout, which is all it really needs to be.

The verdict

Although the XUV 3XO is far from perfect, it’s quite surprising to me that I haven’t seen more of them out on the road – and that’s after spending time across most Australian cities in the past year.

What it lacks in build quality, it makes up for with simplicity and value. If you just want a car to be a car – here you are, your answer.

All I hear from people these days are complaints about how complex many modern cars are getting, from the overbearing safety systems that are hard to switch off through to the lack of clearly marked buttons.

The buttons here are big, and it’s easy to figure out which ones turn any beeps and bongs off – not that it beeps and bongs as much as many new cars.

For the sort of customers who turned to the old Mitsubishi ASX for well over a decade, this is one of the few true modern equivalents.

Quite simply, it’s four metres of car. Simple, straightforward, shameless. There’s nothing wrong with that.

Simple, straightforward, shameless… and small. Photo: Patrick Jackson.

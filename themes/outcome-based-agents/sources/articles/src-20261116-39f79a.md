(The Center Square) - New listings in higher-priced ZIP codes around the Seattle area were 20.1% above the average for those same periods several years ago, based on overlapping 90-day periods in February through May 2026 compared to that window in 2022-23, as listings fell 3.9% in lower-priced ZIP codes.​
An 
analysis
 
from The Center Square’s chief economist, Orphe Divounguy, ranked Seattle second among 88 eligible metro areas for the size of the gap in new listing growth between higher- and lower-priced ZIP codes.
Compared with the corresponding periods in 2022–2023, inventory increased 155.1% in higher-priced ZIP codes and 104.4% in lower-priced ZIP codes, while completed sales declined 11.2% and 23.8%, the analysis shows.
During the 2026 legislative session, state lawmakers passed a new 9.9% tax on income above $1 million for an individual, or above a combined $1 million for spouses or registered domestic partners, starting January 1, 2028. It followed a capital-gains tax enacted in 2021, with the first payments due in 2023 and historic tax increases in 2025, but Divounguy said it's hard to tell exactly what’s causing the trend.
The shift in higher- versus lower-priced ZIP code listings was already visible in January 2024, he said.
“Are Washington’s tax increases pushing wealthy homeowners to sell?” Divounguy wrote in the report.​
“Taxes, layoffs and immigration restrictions remain plausible contributors,” he wrote of what could be causing the broader buildup of homes for sale, “but this analysis cannot establish how much each contributed.”​
Democrats and Republicans say several things could be to blame, with the legislative majority pointing to the Trump administration and federal policy and conservatives arguing Democrats should bear some blame. Real estate brokers who spoke with The Center Square say it’s more nuanced than either side claims.
State Rep. Sam Low, the ranking Republican on the House Housing Committee, said some of his voters left Washington state after Democrats filled two multi-billion-dollar general fund deficits with tax hikes.​
State spending has more than doubled, outpacing population growth, in the last decade as Democrats expand control in the Legislature and executive branch. Despite massive tax hikes, Gov. Bob Ferguson says lawmakers will face additional budget pressures in 2027 as the income tax faces legal challenges.
“The pendulum has been stuck on one side for the majority of the time,” Low told The Center Square, referring to Democratic majorities in the statehouse. “Those policies are now coming home to roost.”​
What's driving the trend?
According to Northwest Multiple Listing Service’s October 
snapshot
, 13,538 homes were actively listed across King, Snohomish and Pierce counties, up about 31.2% from the year prior. Active listings rose in King County by about 32.8%, in Snohomish County by 41.7% and in Pierce County by 19.7%.​
“With new listings unchanged from a year ago, the growth in inventory is coming from slower sales rather than an increase in homes coming to market,” according to NWMLS’ snapshot.
“At the October 2026 pace of sales, it would take approximately 4.41 months to sell every home currently listed in the NWMLS service area if no new homes came on the market, up from 3.02 months in October 2025.”​
The increase in active listings comes not only amid increasing tax pressure, but also a wave of layoffs.
Starbucks announced plans to move its sourcing operations from Seattle to Nashville in March and, in April, said the move would include an additional support office with up to 2,000 jobs over five years. It comes amid a wave of Starbucks locations closing around Seattle, as Microsoft also cuts headcount.​
In July, the Washington-based tech giant announced it would cut 
605
 
positions in the state, and in September it announced another 277 Washington job cuts, scheduled to take effect Nov. 21.
Microsoft also offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 people across several nations. However, those developments fall outside of the timeline in Divounguy’s report.
Steve Bourassa, a real estate professor at the University of Washington, told The Center Square in an email that the first thing he thought of was tech layoffs. He said the income tax could also be causing people to leave Washington state, but argued that estimates of its potential impact can “vary widely.”
“I don't think it is possible to nail down the reasons for the increase in listings in expensive relative to lower-priced neighborhoods,” Bourassa wrote in an email responding to Divounguy’s housing analysis.
Microsoft announced more than 2,800 Washington job cuts in May and July 2025 after lawmakers authorized what business groups estimated would be more than $12 billion in state and local tax increases, though they later pulled back some.​
In January, Amazon issued a layoff notice affecting nearly 2,200 employees beginning in April; more than 1,400 positions were in Seattle, and more than 600 were in Bellevue. Those were part of larger global layoffs, with Meta also laying off 1,395 Washington workers in a round of job cuts effective July 22.
One broker's perspective
Shawna Ader, a Windermere real estate broker in Seattle’s luxury market, pointed to employment and life changes as what she sees among her clients. She said some downsize as their children leave home, moving to Bainbridge Island and other parts of the state, though some folks leave altogether.
She said if any tax is pushing individuals out of the state, she thinks it’s the estate tax. Washington raised its highest estate-tax rate from 20% to 35% in 2025, then reversed the rate increases for deaths on or after July 1, 2026.
“I had a luxury seller with a $10 million-plus home, and that all happened during the time we were listing their house,” Ader said, referring to Democrats reducing the rate. “And we asked them, ‘Does this matter to you?’ And they said, 'No, the 20% [rate] was still enough for us to move to Nevada.'”
She said some in the ultra-luxury market are redomiciling in other states to avoid state taxes.
When asked about the inventory sitting on the market, Ader explained that owners with low mortgage rates might hold on to their house until interest rates drop rather than take on a more expensive loan.
She said housing prices around Seattle outpace the national average at the entry and luxury levels.
“The only correction that we can have here is price correction,” Ader said, unless interest rates drop.
What lawmakers have heard
Rep. Julia Reed, a Seattle Democrat who sits on the House Housing Committee, wrote in an email to The Center Square that she hasn’t heard much from her voters about leaving the region. She said she has heard about people moving away because of federal immigration enforcement and tech layoffs.​
“I hear more about the concern about unregulated AI driving job losses and about high fuel costs,” she wrote. “I hear about how President Trump and the Republican Congress’ decision to cancel healthcare subsidies has driven up their healthcare premiums and concerns about what will happen to people.”
Sen. Jessica Bateman, a Democrat who chairs the Senate Housing Committee; Rep. Janice Zahn, a Democrat who sits on the House Housing Committee; Senate Majority Leader Jamie Pedersen, who led the income tax to passage; and Councilmember Dionne Foster, who chairs the Seattle Housing, Arts and Civil Rights Committee, didn’t respond to requests for comment on Divounguy’s market analysis.
Rep. Cyndy Jacobsen, the assistant ranking Republican on the House Housing Committee, wasn’t available for an interview, but emailed comments her office has received from voters leaving the state.
“This will likely be my last email to you, as my house closes next week. After 30 years here, I am leaving Washington,” according to one email her office forwarded to The Center Square. “The political climate and trajectory have ruined what good was left, and I am not sure the state can recover.”

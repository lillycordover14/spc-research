David Crosby
 spent his entire career trying to make the most sophisticated side of rock and roll more palatable. 
There was no sense in him making the same bluesy rock and roll that everyone else was making, and when he broke free from The Byrds, he felt that a lot of his greatest songs needed to have something else going for them than just being a bunch of catchy tunes like Roger McGuinn. He was already trying to test the waters of what he could do, but Crosby, Stills and Nash always relied on all the members working in conjunction with each other every time they played.
That’s easier said than done when all of them disagreed on where their music should go, but when you listen to them harmonise, you would have sworn that they had been playing together their whole lives. 
‘You Don’t Have to Cry’ was the first time
 that most people got to hear what they sounded like outside of their respective outfits, and if they could sound that heavenly on one of their records, Crosby was going to teach a clinic in the mystical ways of playing rock and roll.
The cascading harmonies on ‘Déjà Vu’ were the perfect example of him arranging every note to sound perfect, but when Neil Young was brought into the mix, that’s usually a lot more to manage in one band. Young was willing to go with the flow with what everyone wanted to play for a brief moment in time, but whenever he made his own songs, he was going to do everything his way or no way whenever the band jammed.
While that worked perfectly well when he was strumming away during ‘Helpless’, ‘Ohio’ was a much different can of worms when Young first played it. The tragedy of the Kent State shooting was still heavy in the air, and from the moment that he stumbled upon that drop-D guitar lick, everyone in the band knew that they needed to make the record as soon as they could to comment on the tragedy.
There was only one problem: they already had a single out. ‘Teach Your Children’ was clearly a frontrunner to become one of the most commercially successful singles that the band would ever have, but once Nash heard the song, they all voted to kill their own single to make sure they pushed a song like this over the line. It wasn’t the most complicated song in the world, but that didn’t matter to Crosby.
This was the sound of Young reporting the horrors of the world, and he was proud to be associated with anything adjacent to a song like that, 
saying
, “We got it out right away. The president of our record company said, ‘I want this out now.’ We pulled all the stops. We had a song already climbing the charts, ‘Teach Your Children,’ that was going to be a big hit probably, but we pulled it. Nash pulled it. Then we put out ‘Ohio’ right away. It was immediate, it was topical, and it was the truth. You could feel that we were angry.”

And while Crosby and Young had more than their 
fair share of ups and downs
 while the former Byrd was still alive, he never lost respect for this song. This was one of the most poignant messages that they could have sent around that time, and up until the day he passed away, the song never failed to put a lump in Crosby’s throat whenever he had to sing the tune live.
Because while anyone could have related to a traditional pop song like ‘Teach Your Children’, there’s a more visceral feeling that comes from hearing a tune like ‘Ohio’. It might seem like ancient history for people who weren’t there to hear about the tragedy, but the reason why songs like this last is because they’re documenting the truth, and that’s something that no one can take away from CSNY.

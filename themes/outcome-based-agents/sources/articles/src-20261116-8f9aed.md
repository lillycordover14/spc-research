OBITUARY
Jason W Clay
February 28, 1951
 – 
November 16, 2026
IN THE CARE OF
Joseph Gawler’s Sons, LLC
Dr. Jason W Clay, of Arlington, Virginia, a global food expert and human rights advocate, passed away at Johns Hopkins in Baltimore, Maryland on November 16, 2026, surrounded by his family after complications from a stem cell transplant. As Senior Vice President at World Wildlife Fund and the Executive Director of the Markets Institute, Jason worked tirelessly to reshape global-food systems to enhance sustainability and economic security.
Jason grew up in a rural community in King City, Missouri with his six siblings living on less than a dollar a day, sleeping on a screened porch and hunting and fishing for the family dinner table. When Jason was fifteen, his father died in a tractor accident, and overnight Jason took responsibility for managing the family farm. He credited the scarcity he experienced as a child with the determined and resourceful approach he brought to his life’s work.
Jason was the second student in his town to take the SAT. He struggled with dyslexia in his youth, wasn’t in the top quartile of his graduating high school class and was advised to avoid public speaking. He went on to receive a scholarship to Harvard and graduated at the top of his class, attended the London School of Economics, and later earned his PhD in Anthropology from Cornell University. He would go on to deliver thousands of talks and keynotes around the world.
His early work focused on human rights and supporting “people without a nation.” He worked with refugees, indigenous people, famine victims, and ethnic minorities to ensure their voices were heard. Jason set up some of the first fair-trade supply chains and coined the phrase “rainforest marketing.” He created Rainforest Crunch with Ben and Jerry’s and worked on more than 200 products with more than 50 other companies. He raised awareness for the Amazon Rainforest by partnering with The Grateful Dead on a benefit concert at Madison Square Garden in 1988. In 1980, he founded the award winning Cultural Survival Quarterly, which he edited until 1992.
During the height of his work in constructing fair-trade supply chains, Jason was a guest of Dame Anita and Sir Gordon Roddick – founders of the Body Shop – at their castle in the Scottish Highlands where he met Mary Ann Mills, the brand’s first American franchisee. From the moment that Jason opened the castle door for Mary Ann, a great love story began. In their 34 years together, they raised three children in Arlington, Virginia (two from Jason’s prior marriage to Dominique Callimanopulos) while traveling the world, playing cards, hosting dinner parties, and weaving together a vibrant social tapestry.
Jason had an ability to bring people together from all walks of life, working with producers, multinational companies, non-profits, farmers, donors, activists, and low-income communities to find common ground. Jason helped establish global production standards for palm oil, sugar, soy, beef, and 12 aquaculture commodities. His theories of market transformation influenced the work and priorities of WWF, other NGOs and partners, and many of the largest companies on Earth. Many of the approaches we now take for granted—partnering with the private sector, working through supply chains, focusing on system-level change—reflect Jason’s energy and influence. Most recently he had been working on Codex Planetarius to set minimum environmental performance levels for products to enter global markets and a “1% Fund” to introduce an insurance premium on key food commodity exports to help support the least efficient producers. Throughout his career, Jason authored 18 books, more than 1,000 articles, 500 blogs and has given more than 2,000 presentations.
Despite his Horatio Alger life story, Jason was quick to note that his success owed much to others and governmental “Great Society” programs that helped lower-income and rural children access higher education. He noted in a recent correspondence, “I’m not under any illusion that every poor kid in the US could have done what I did. But many could have if given half the opportunity and some would have done much, much more.”
Jason was an accomplished, passionate and adventurous cook. Food was central not only to his family life but to many of his professional meetings, which he often conducted in his kitchen, at his dinner table, or by the fireplace.
During his illness, Jason redoubled his efforts to guide WWF’s food and markets work. True to his nature, he continued to hold meetings from morning through night, pushing out original ideas and raising capital for the initiatives that were so important to him and form the enduring legacy he wished to impart to the world. Through it all, Mary Ann was his supreme advocate, caregiver, and beloved companion, befriending all of Johns Hopkins with her characteristic love, devotion and cheer.
In addition to Mary Ann, Jason is survived by his three children, Alexa (Adam Sobolew), Zale (Stacy Du Clos) and Hawkins (Emily Pelstring), his two grandchildren, Ava and Theia, five of his brothers and sisters, Dreanna, Jennifer, Donna, Eric, and Chris, along with sixteen nieces and nephews and their children.

Taiwan Tops Asia-Pacific in Cybersecurity Threats as Cyber Insurance Premiums Soar Nearly Sixfold in Seven Years to Top NT$500 Million

The density of cyberattacks facing Taiwanese companies continues to climb. According to Microsoft's latest Microsoft Digital Defense Report, in the first half of this year Taiwan ranked fourth globally and first in the Asia-Pacific among countries and regions most frequently affected by cybersecurity threats, with cyberattacks, ransomware and data breach risks all rising in tandem. The escalation of cybersecurity threats is also directly reflected in the insurance market: premium income from cyber insurance in Taiwan's property insurance industry grew from NT$89 million (approximately $2.7 million) in 2018 all the way to NT$512 million (approximately $15.5 million) in 2025, expanding nearly sixfold over seven years; cumulative premiums in the first half of this year had already reached NT$319 million (approximately $9.7 million), with a chance of setting a new full-year high.

Taiwan's Financial Supervisory Commission is calling on companies to strengthen cybersecurity risk management, sharpen the resilience of their cybersecurity operations, and assess taking out cyber insurance at the appropriate time to transfer potential risks away.

Premium structure concentrated, semiconductor industry accounts for nearly half

Looking at the distribution of policyholders by industry, demand for cyber insurance is highly concentrated among sectors with deep digitalization that sit at the core of global supply chains. According to statistics, premiums from the semiconductor industry totaled about NT$156 million (approximately $4.7 million), accounting for 48.71% and firmly ranking first among all industries; the financial and insurance sector ranked second at about NT$39.78 million (approximately $1.2 million), while the computer and peripheral equipment sector ranked third at about NT$25.83 million (approximately $780,000).

Such a structure is hardly surprising. Once the information systems on a semiconductor fab's production lines grind to a halt, losses are counted by the hour, and customers are imposing increasingly strict requirements on supply chain security; taking out cyber insurance has become part of maintaining orders and supply chain trust.

Losses from cybersecurity incidents no longer confined to the "information" layer

Mega Insurance analyzes that the cybersecurity risks facing modern enterprises long ago transcended the purely information-security domain. A single cybersecurity incident often triggers multiple loss vectors at once: legal liability arising from the leakage of customer personal data or trade secrets, disruption of order-taking and shipments caused by the shutdown of critical systems, forensic and crisis-management costs after an incident, and the enterprise's own direct property losses.

The company notes that when an enterprise suffers a cyberattack, it may face heavy legal liability due to data breaches, and if information systems shut down, order-taking, production, shipments and customer service may also be affected, generating business interruption losses. In response to such compound risks, Mega Insurance has launched "E-commerce and Information Security Liability Insurance," allowing enterprises to flexibly plan coverage for third-party liability, business interruption losses, crisis management costs and their own direct losses according to their operational characteristics and risk appetite, addressing both external liability and internal operational impact.

A number of property insurance companies in the market have already launched different types of cybersecurity products. In addition to the comprehensive policies mentioned above, there are also data protection liability insurance policies that specifically cover legally owed compensation to third parties for personal data breaches, as well as information system wrongful act insurance, which mainly covers losses of funds or other property caused by third parties unlawfully intruding into computer systems.

Underwriting terms depend on cybersecurity posture

It is worth noting that cyber insurance is not something that can be bought at standard rates simply by paying. When underwriting, insurers conduct risk assessments through interviews and make comprehensive judgments based on factors such as the client's industry risk profile, the level of cybersecurity controls and past loss records. Industry players suggest that companies can proactively provide third-party audit reports, business continuity plans and crisis notification mechanisms when applying for coverage to secure more favorable underwriting terms. In other words, the more solid a company's cybersecurity defenses, the more bargaining power it has over premiums and coverage scope.

Taiwan's Financial Supervisory Commission also singled out that, in addition to the semiconductor and financial sectors, industries such as information services, trading and department stores, and education and cultural industries — which make extensive use of internet services and hold large amounts of personal data — are likewise high-risk groups for cyberattacks; once hacked, they may face the dual damage of business interruption and data breaches at the same time, and transferring risk through cyber insurance should be regarded as an important strategy for corporate sustainability.

Significance for the market and industry

The nearly sixfold growth in cyber insurance premiums over seven years reflects the convergence of two forces. The first is the substantive deterioration of the threat environment: Microsoft's report ranks Taiwan as the most frequently attacked region in the Asia-Pacific, shifting corporate risk awareness from "whether it will happen" to "when it will happen." The second is policy guidance from the competent authority: Taiwan's Financial Supervisory Commission continues to encourage companies to use insurance tools to reinforce their risk management frameworks, gradually turning cyber insurance from an optional add-on into a component of operational resilience.

For the property insurance industry, this is a niche market with a low base and rapid growth, but underwriting is difficult and tests insurers' ability to price cybersecurity risk; for enterprises, insurance is ultimately only the last line of defense for risk transfer. Industry players caution that cyber insurance cannot replace cybersecurity protection measures, and companies must continue to strengthen information security management and incident response mechanisms, combining pre-incident protection, in-incident response and post-incident recovery in order to maintain sound operations in the digital age.

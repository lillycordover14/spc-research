EQT Ventures, the investment arm of Swedish investment group EQT, has invested about $10 million in Edra, which develops artificial intelligence solutions for enterprises, lifting the company's total funding to $40 million — a move that reflects investor appetite for technology that takes AI from experimentation and digital assistants to running operational processes inside companies.
The investment followed a four-week trial in which EQT Group, EQT Ventures' parent, used Edra's platform within its IT services division. The group kept using the technology after the trial ended and plans to extend it to other parts of its business.
The deal draws added significance from the nature of the relationship: EQT's role went beyond financing, with an actual test of the product inside its own operations preceding the investment. The group is also giving Edra access to a portfolio of more than 330 companies across the Americas, Europe and Asia to explore using the platform in their businesses.
New investment lifts Edra's funding to $40 million
The new investment from EQT Ventures is worth about $10 million and extends a Series A round in which Edra raised roughly $30 million earlier in 2026, led by Sequoia Capital with participation from 8VC and A*.
That brings the company's total funding to $40 million, according to a report by The Wall Street Journal.
The new funding is intended to support Edra's expansion in the enterprise AI software market and to widen the platform's reach among companies seeking to automate internal workflows, particularly processes that depend on accumulated operational knowledge spread across employees, systems, documents and work records.
The company operates from offices in New York and London and targets organizations that need to connect their existing business applications to AI systems able to understand and carry out their procedures.
Edra's premise is not to build a digital assistant that answers employees' questions, but to build a software layer that can study how an organization works, document the procedures its teams carry out, and turn that knowledge into instructions AI agents can use to complete tasks.
The company's two founders come from Palantir
Edra was founded in 2024 by Eugen Alpeza and Yannis Karamanlakis, who previously worked at Palantir, where they helped lead advanced AI engineering teams working directly with enterprises to deploy technology in real operating environments.
The founders' background rests on tackling a challenge companies face when they move from testing AI tools to using them in day-to-day operations: models may be able to analyze data and generate answers, but they need to understand each organization's internal procedures before they can carry out tasks correctly.
Those procedures vary from one company to another, even when both operate in the same sector or use the same software. How a company handles support requests, approves a given step, or escalates an issue to a specialist team may depend on internal rules and accumulated experience that are not fully documented in any single manual.
That is where the idea for Edra came from: the platform aims to turn operational expertise that already exists inside the organization into structured knowledge that employees and intelligent systems can use.
Platform connects corporate systems and understands their processes
Edra's platform works by connecting to the systems and applications the organization already uses, then analyzing the operational data they hold, including work logs, requests, correspondence and technical tickets.
After gathering this information, the platform seeks to reconstruct how teams carry out their work and identify the recurring steps and the rules governing how different cases are handled, then turn them into documented instructions that AI systems can refer to when carrying out tasks.
The approach differs from relying on feeding a set of general instructions into an AI model, because it aims to build an understanding more closely tied to the organization's actual operations, based on what happens in its systems and records.
Platform uses include IT service management, technical support and compliance — areas that require handling large volumes of requests and repetitive procedures while adhering to specific rules in carrying out each process.
This architecture allows AI agents to handle business processes according to instructions drawn from the organization's own reality, rather than relying on general knowledge that may not align with internal policies or the way teams operate.
Four weeks proved enough to test the platform
EQT Ventures' investment followed a pilot that EQT Group ran on the Edra platform inside its own IT services department.
The trial lasted four weeks and focused on using the platform to support the internal IT service team, which handles employee requests and issues related to devices, software and technical services.
According to what EQT Ventures published in explaining the investment, the team handles more than 3,000 tickets a month, a volume that provides a suitable environment for testing the platform's ability to analyze recurring requests and extract the procedures the team relies on to handle them.
During the first week of the trial, Edra was able to identify more than 130 patterns for resolving issues that had not previously been documented in clear operating instructions, according to EQT Ventures.
The result suggests that some of the knowledge organizations need to get their work done may already exist in records of past requests and conversations, but it does not necessarily become written guides or procedures that employees and other systems can use.
By the second week, the platform had begun proposing solutions for actual tickets reaching the team, and by the fourth week the trial had reached a stage where the team was increasingly accepting the solutions proposed by the system, according to EQT Ventures' account of the trial.
After the pilot period ended, EQT Group kept using the platform in its IT operations and announced plans to expand it into other parts of its business.
EQT portfolio companies begin evaluating the technology
Edra's access to EQT's portfolio companies is one of the most important routes that could support the company's expansion over the coming period.
EQT Group owns a portfolio of more than 330 companies across the Americas, Europe and Asia, giving Edra a chance to reach organizations operating in different sectors, with varied needs in operations management, knowledge documentation and process automation.
The group said a number of its portfolio companies will begin assessing use of the platform, following the trial it ran inside its own operations.
Among the companies that have already begun testing the technology is Keywords Studios, which provides game services, translation, localization and art production for the gaming and digital entertainment industry.
The expansion lets the company test how well its platform holds up outside IT services and reach other enterprise use cases, such as operational support, compliance and internal process management.
It also gives it a chance to test the product in different operating environments, rather than relying on a single trial inside an investment firm.
Even so, portfolio companies entering the assessment stage does not mean they have all adopted the platform or begun using it at scale; the step remains tied to the results of the tests each company runs according to its own needs.
The ContextOps concept addresses the enterprise knowledge gap
Edra's model rests on a concept the company calls ContextOps, which is built on organizing operational knowledge inside the enterprise and turning it into instructions that can be tested, approved and continuously updated.
The idea stems from the premise that deploying AI in companies does not depend on model capabilities alone; it requires precise context that explains how the organization works, the rules its teams follow, and the exceptions they handle while carrying out tasks.
When a support team receives a new ticket, there may be specific steps for diagnosing the problem, different levels for escalating it, and certain permissions that allow one employee but not another to carry out an action.
These details may sit in records of past tickets, in employees' messages or in their hands-on experience, without ever being gathered into a single guide.
Edra extracts this information and converts it into instructions that can be reviewed and approved, so that intelligent systems can use them when carrying out operations.
The approach involves a workflow that begins with monitoring operations and the available data, then extracting patterns and potential instructions, after which they are reviewed and approved by the experts and managers responsible for each process, before being published for use within the organization's systems.
This method allows the instructions to be updated when procedures change or new cases arise, rather than relying on static manuals that may fall out of step with how the work is actually done over time.
Why do companies struggle to deploy AI agents?
Organizations face a challenge when deploying AI agents in real operations, because completing a task requires more than understanding the question or generating a suitable answer.
Agents need to know the steps that allow them to carry out the procedure, the systems they must interact with, the permissions available to them, and the cases that require an employee's intervention or a manager's approval.
The absence of this information can lead to inaccurate solutions or to steps that conflict with the organization's internal procedures, even when the model being used is highly capable of processing language and analyzing data.
The problem is compounded at large organizations that run multiple systems, operate through geographically dispersed teams and have accumulated different procedures over the years.
Turning this knowledge into usable instructions usually requires experts who understand how the business works and who work with technology teams to document procedures and build the right solutions.
Edra aims to reduce reliance on this manual process by analyzing operational data and extracting knowledge automatically, while preserving the experts' role in reviewing and approving the instructions.
That balance is a core part of the company's model: the idea is not to give the AI agent authority to carry out any action based on its own inference, but to equip it with instructions that have been reviewed and approved by the people responsible for the processes.
EQT bets on shift from experimentation to operations
EQT Ventures' decision to invest in Edra is tied to a broader vision of companies moving from experimenting with AI tools to using them in real operations.
Sven Tornkvist, global co-head of EQT's technology and digital sector team, said there is strong interest in the company within the group's portfolio, as organizations move beyond the experimentation phase into practical applications.
He noted that solutions that help AI agents understand and execute complex business processes have significant opportunities, as many organizations face the same challenge.
The investment reflects venture capital firms' interest in companies developing technology layers that support the deployment of AI within enterprises, particularly those addressing the problems associated with integrating models with existing systems and managing operational knowledge.
These companies are trying to provide the infrastructure that allows organizations to benefit from AI agents without having to rebuild their systems entirely or document every procedure manually before they can begin using them.

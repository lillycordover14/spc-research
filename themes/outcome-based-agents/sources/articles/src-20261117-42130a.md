Jason Clay, who persuaded big business to help save nature, has died at 75&nbsp;&nbsp;news - Mongabay

[page text]
Jason Clay grew up in rural poverty, earned a scholarship to Harvard and began his career working with Indigenous communities and refugees before joining WWF in 1993.
Clay helped create Ben & Jerry’s Rainforest Crunch and other products that generated roughly $100 million in sales. He later acknowledged that the initiative had failed to address the principal drivers of deforestation.
At WWF, Clay sought to reduce environmental damage by influencing major agricultural commodity buyers and establishing sustainability standards. He became increasingly critical of voluntary certification, arguing that it often failed to reach the least responsible producers.
Clay died on November 16th, 2026, aged 75.

In 1988 
Jason Clay attended a Grateful Dead concert
 at Madison Square Garden and met a man who wanted to help save tropical rainforests. Clay, an anthropologist who had worked with Indigenous communities, asked what he did for a living.

“I make ice cream,” the man replied.

It was Ben Cohen of Ben & Jerry’s. Clay suggested developing a flavor using nuts gathered from tropical forests. He thought higher payments for forest products might give people a reason to keep the trees standing.

Within a year, Rainforest Crunch was on supermarket shelves. Clay set up a trading company and went on to work with more than 50 businesses on some 200 products, generating roughly $100 million in retail sales. At one point, he said, his operation was buying around a fifth of the world’s Brazil nuts. It paid gatherers two or three times the going rate, and other buyers raised their prices to compete.

The people gathering the nuts weren’t generally the ones clearing the forests. Ranchers and loggers had little reason to change what they were doing, and Clay eventually called Rainforest Crunch a failure. “We were attacking the wrong driver,” he explained in 
a 2010 TED talk
. He should have been working on beef, lumber and soy.

  

Clay, 
who died on November 16th aged 75
, spent more than three decades at the World Wildlife Fund (WWF), where he worked to change how major companies bought agricultural commodities linked to deforestation and other environmental problems.

Born in 1951, he grew up near King City, Missouri, one of seven children on a small farm. The family lived on less than a dollar per person per day. Clay said he drove a tractor at three and was hunting and fishing for food at seven. His mother tended a large garden and taught the children to save what they couldn’t eat. They were poor, 
he recalled
, though there was nearly always enough to eat.

When he was 15, his father died in a tractor accident. Clay helped his mother manage the farm. He had dyslexia and wasn’t among the top students in his high-school class. He’d also been advised against public speaking. A scholarship took him to Harvard. He later studied at the London School of Economics and earned a doctorate in anthropology from Cornell.

“I went to college to get away from farming,” he recalled in 
a 2016 interview
.

In 1985, 
he interviewed Ethiopian refugees
 in camps in eastern Sudan. They described forced resettlement, imprisonment, rape and killings. He calculated how much food the camps would need and how many people were likely to die. Eventually, he said, he became good at predicting how many body bags would be needed.

“This is God’s work, but it’s not my work,” he later said of his refugee research. He wanted to address the policies that had driven people from their homes.

Through Cultural Survival, where he founded the 
Cultural Survival Quarterly
 in 1980, Clay worked with Indigenous peoples whose livelihoods depended on forests.

At WWF, which he joined in 1993, Clay initially worked on developing markets for sustainable products. WWF helped set up certification programs, but Clay grew skeptical of how much they could achieve. He didn’t expect shoppers to figure out how something was made in the few seconds it took to grab it off the shelf.

In his 2010 TED talk, he calculated that just 100 companies handled a quarter of the trade in 15 commodities WWF had identified as major threats to biodiversity. The same company names kept appearing across different commodities. “There’s Cargill here, there’s Cargill there, there’s Cargill everywhere,” he told his audience.

He wanted rivals like Coca-Cola and Pepsi, or Cargill and Bunge, to agree on environmental rules and keep fighting over everything else, an idea he called precompetitive collaboration.

When WWF helped convene discussions to establish sustainability standards for farmed salmon, three of the eight original participants were suing one another. The meetings eventually expanded to include producers accounting for about 60% of the world’s farmed salmon, according to Clay. WWF went on to help establish similar standards for sugar and soy, among other commodities.

He learned to talk to executives in their own terms. A chocolate company that couldn’t get cocoa had a problem, whether or not anyone in the building cared about rainforests.

The Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil, which WWF helped establish, 
was accused by environmental campaigners
 of allowing companies to claim environmental responsibility while deforestation continued. Even certified producers sometimes had difficulty finding buyers willing to pay for their products.

Clay had grown dissatisfied with the certification programs he had helped create. Standards often became “Christmas trees,” he complained, as more requirements were added, driving up costs. Better producers found themselves being undercut by competitors who made no such effort. Voluntary schemes also depended on companies wanting to do the right thing. “Most probably don’t,” Clay said in 
a 2024 WWF interview
.

In his final years, he was promoting 
Codex Planetarius
, a proposal to set minimum environmental standards for food entering international trade. He also proposed a 1% insurance premium on exports of key food commodities, with the money used to help the least-efficient producers change their methods. Neither had been fully implemented when he died.

Clay often held meetings at home, in his kitchen or around the dinner table. He loved to cook, something he’d learned from his mother. Asked in 2016 what ordinary people could do to improve food systems, he advised them to eat their leftovers.

He met his wife, Mary Ann Mills, a Body Shop franchisee, at a castle in the Scottish Highlands belonging to Anita and Gordon Roddick, the founders of The Body Shop. According to his family, their relationship began when he opened the castle door for her. They spent 34 years together and raised three children in Arlington, Virginia.

In May, 
WWF’s board honored him
 for more than 30 years of service. He spoke about ideas that had spent years waiting for support: 13 years for Codex Planetarius and 33 for the 1% Fund. He recalled a board member once asking how much progress his program could make in a few years. Clay suggested that its impact might double in a year. The board member wanted to know how he could achieve ten times the results in three.

During his illness, Clay continued holding meetings and raising money for WWF’s food and markets work. 
He died at Johns Hopkins Hospital
 in Baltimore following complications from a stem-cell transplant. Both his sons had been stem-cell donors.

When Clay discussed his rise from rural poverty, he pointed to the government programs and scholarships that had helped him. “I’m not under any illusion that every poor kid in the US could have done what I did,” he wrote late in life. Plenty could have, he added, given the chance, and some would’ve done better than he did.

His family established a scholarship in his name to help a low-income graduating senior from King City High School attend college.

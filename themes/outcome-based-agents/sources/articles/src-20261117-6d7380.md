After a spectacular win on opening night in which the Pittsburgh Penguins trounced their arch rival Philadelphia Flyers 7-0 in front of a national TV audience, it seems everything has gone wrong for the Penguins. 

The push to integrate young players into the 
lineup
 hasn’t happened. The veteran free agents have suffered 
injuries
 and ineffectiveness. 
Sidney Crosby
 is scoring well below career norms. And the trade talk is persistent, though no action has occurred.

The only trade thus far was a surprising boon to the club when they had to sacrifice forward 
Filip Hallander
 to the Edmonton Oilers for depth defenseman Spencer Stastney. But, as the Penguins found, the incompatible construction of their blue line was helped by Stastney.

Fast forward 19 more games and the Penguins have just four regulation wins and seven total. The losses have been marked not by strong efforts that came up short but instead marred by continuing and egregious mistakes. 

The long-worn line following some losses of “there was a lot to like” has not been applicable. Nay, the team can’t insult their own intelligence with a blistering fib worthy of the political campaign trail.

And the ready, set, go of the regular season has now become a hurry up and wait for whatever comes next. 

3 Thoughts

1. Sidney Crosby 

Is age catching Crosby? The answer to that question is a split decision, but in the metric many fans will measure, the stat sheet is belied by reality. Crosby is on pace for about 55 points, which is well below par for his 21-year running point-per-game streak, but for the full picture, don’t stop looking there.

Crosby has 13 points (6-7-13) in 20 games, which puts him within striking distance of extending his record, but more importantly, his underlying offense metrics are strong. 

To wit: His shooting percentage (9.6%) is his lowest since a wonky 2017-18 in which the Penguins offense got fat on power-play points. His Corsi (53%) is in line with career norms, while his expected goals-for (57%) is the best in eight years. And his scoring chance rates are exemplary (58%). All stats are from 
NaturalStatTrick.com
.

So, what’s happening? 

Well, age is catching Crosby as evidenced in some parts of his game. He’s not defending as well because teams are able to get on their toes and bury the Penguins in a rush and transition game. Crosby excels in a hard game on the walls and corners, but not chasing the likes of Logan Cooley up and down the ice.

Additionally, Crosby has not played with a puck-retrieving winger for most of the season. While the advanced numbers when playing with Malkin are well above board, the eyes show that over the course of the entire season, Crosby simply isn’t receiving the puck in good areas. The overall Penguins game is a reflection of the Penguins roster construction: some talented players but almost no grit. The team is stuck with a rush attack, which is not to their benefit, but they push ahead anyway rather than settle into an ugly chip-chase-forecheck game because, well, that’s not to many of the players’ benefit either.

The Penguins are a puck possession team without enough players who can establish initial possession. And Crosby is just one of several players paying the price. 

Age? Sure. But that’s not why his stats are down. 

2. Muse Ain’t to Blame

Losing breeds 1000 opinions on what needs to change. If you’re not fully clear on the Penguins roster construction, double-check the portion on Crosby because it also applies to coach 
Dan Muse
.

Muse has been trying in vain to find combos and stability in his forward lines, but he’s stuck patching holes in a leaky boat. Find one combo, such as 
Tommy Novak
 and 
Egor Chinakhov
, and it opens a black hole on the third line. Move 
Bryan Rust
 back to Crosby’s line, but then he suffers a six-week (or more) lower-body injury.

The offensive production from 
Nick Robertson
 and 
Hendrix Lapierre
 has also slowed dramatically. Robertson is an army of one, and Lapierre is trying to remain defensively responsible with mistake-prone linemates, so much so that he often removes himself from the play to the high F3 out of obligation, and it simply creates more imbalance. 

The cohesion the Penguins enjoyed last season with some big bodies and puck getters is gone, replaced by players who play with the puck, but only after it is given to them. 

Muse is trapped with a team that isn’t good at either attack, isn’t fast, and isn’t physical. As a reader pointed out, neither Toe Blake nor Scotty Bowman could fix this. 

The Muse criticism is an attempt to simply explain something that cannot be simply explained, and it is simply off base.  

3. Goalies!

A little stick tap to Penguins goalie coach Andy Chiodo. The bright spot of the Penguins season has been the play of their goalies. Admittedly, 
Sergei Murashov
 had a rough go for a few games. 
Arturs Silovs
 has subpar statistics. But the process is a positive.

Murashov has rebounded well, very well, after those rough games and has a couple of wins. And Silovs has held the fort against an onslaught of high-danger scoring chances that would cause a lesser man to destroy his stick over the crossbar and let rip on teammates. 

To judge Silovs on his statistics would be akin to judging a golfer’s scorecard after playing in a tropical storm. The Penguins’ goalies have kept the Penguins in games despite a turnstile of breakaways, two-on-ones, and uncovered chances at point-blank range, which are usually the result of awful turnovers or coverage breakdowns.

Silovs has allowed the third-most high-danger goals against (18), joining goalies from Toronto, Chicago, Vancouver and Winnipeg in the top five. It’s not that those teams are giving up tons of high-danger chances; it’s that those teams excel in the unmeasured statistic that could be called “ridiculously high-danger chances.”

Murashov is fairing a little better on the high-danger chances, posting an .813 save percentage against them vs. Silovs .700, but both rank highly in terms of short distance of shots faced. 

One note for Silovs: for the 40 goalies who have played at least 400 minutes, he is tied for the league lead in fewest rebound chances given up (29). Given his rough go last season, that’s just another step forward for goalies, even if the team has not done its part.

Building blazing trails: UK Land-grant engagement project creates adaptive biking experience in Kentucky

The Blazing Trails project introduced a new adaptive mountain biking trail in Central Kentucky for people with disabilities to enjoy, many for the first time.

							            Morgan Turner (pictured) was one of the adaptive riders at the Blazing Trails event. Photo provided by UK Human Development Institute.
							        

By
											
Christopher Carney

Published on
											November 17, 2026
																					

VERSAILLES, Ky.—

An adaptive mountain biking trail was recently designed and constructed at the 
Falling Springs Center
, giving people with disabilities the opportunity to ride a bike in ways that match their physical abilities. In Woodford County, this popular park and recreation center offers a myriad of outdoor activities for thousands in the local community to enjoy. 

According to the 
Bluegrass Chapter of Kentucky Mountain Bike Association
 (KYMBA), adaptive mountain biking programs serve amputees and adaptive riders —
 
providing access, skills and confidence for people with disabilities who typically cannot ride a standard mountain bike and require adaptive equipment. 

An adaptive mountain bike. Photo provided by UK Human Development Institute.

The Blazing Trails project, funded by the 
University of Kentucky Office of Land-grant Engagement
, is designed to give people with disabilities increased access to nature in the Commonwealth through adaptive mountain biking by reducing some common barriers.  

John C. Hill
, Ph.D., adjunct faculty member at the 
UK College of Education
, survey project coordinator at the UK 
Human Development Institute
 (HDI) and one of the Blazing Trails project leads, said that access to nature can be challenging for people with disabilities.

“
For people with disabilities, affording and accessing adaptive mountain biking experiences is extremely difficult,” Hill said. “Adaptive mountain bikes are expensive, costing around $14,000 on average. With Blazing Trails, we wanted to introduce a trail that they could access, ride safely and with confidence, and get the same enjoyment as anyone else.” 

An avid mountain biker in Central Kentucky for many years, Hill noticed biking trails in the Commonwealth are inaccessible. 

“I realized there’s no way in Kentucky for people with a disability to ride a mountain bike on some of these trails,” Hill said. “They don’t have the equipment, or the trails might not be accessible, appropriate or enjoyable for people with disabilities.” 

Hill turned to HDI colleagues 
Laura Butler
 and 
Darrell Mattingly
 to begin collaborating on a solution. 

Butler and Hill were able to secure initial funding, provided by 
HDI’s Fund for Excellence
 (FFE), to get the adaptive mountain biking project rolling. 

In 2025, Hill and Butler 
secured additional funding from the UK Office of Land-grant Engagement
, which supports proposals for innovative projects that increase university engagement with communities.  

“Access to the outdoors here in Kentucky is not there for everyone,” Butler said. “Your community isn’t complete unless everyone has access to it. This is why the Blazing Trails project was so important to us.” 

Hill and Butler began working with 
Jeb Gorham
, Ph.D., Versailles resident, avid cyclist, KYMBA board member and associate dean and sports management Botkin Professor at Midway University. According to Hill, Gorham and his research team were vital in helping to fund and construct the 1.5-mile trail at the Falling Springs Center, with permission from the Woodford County Parks and Recreation.  

An adaptive mountain biker participant gripping the handles and smiling. Photo provided by UK Human Development Institute.

“KYMBA wanted to bring an adaptive element to this trail in Woodford County,” Gorham said. “John and Laura partnered with us to support the funding for the trail’s expansion. It takes a lot to build an adaptive mountain biking trail, so we were extremely excited and grateful for this partnership with UK.” 

The Blazing Trails team, which included faculty and students from both UK’s 
Community Innovation Lab
 (CIL) and Midway University, spent more than 100 hours building and creating the trail at Falling Springs Center. 

The team designed the trail to meet adaptive mountain biking guidelines developed by the Kootenay Adaptive Sports Association and the International Mountain Bicycling Association, which specify the appropriate width, surface, grade and cornering requirements. 

Bryan Hains
, Ph.D., CIL co-director at the 
Martin-Gatton College of Agriculture, Food and Environment
 and resident of Woodford County, said his lab being part of the planning process of Blazing Trails was “incredibly special” and will do great things for the local community. 

“Blazing Trails happening in my home county made this a 'passion project' for me,” Hains said. “We wanted to do our part of making adaptive mountain biking more accessible and enjoyable, right here in our backyard.” 

Blazing Trails ribbon cutting
 

In Spring 2026, Blazing Trails hosted a ribbon cutting and adaptive mountain biking workshop for people with disabilities at the Falling Springs Center. The community was invited to enjoy the new adaptive mountain biking trail and go riding on the adaptive bikes provided by 
Catalyst Sports
. 

Approximately a dozen adaptive riders with a variety of disabilities came to the workshop. 

One of the adaptive riders was Lexington native Morgan Turner, who has been competing in 
Special Olympics Kentucky
 since he was in elementary school.  

For Turner, highlights of the experience were riding an adaptive bike for the first time and meeting new people who were also new to adaptive mountain biking. 

Morgan Turner riding an adaptive mountain bike. Photo provided by UK Human Development Institute.

“I wanted to try something new, and I really loved that I stepped out of my comfort zone,” Turner said. “It was fun to see all of the adaptive bikes. You could use your hands and arms to pedal, and I really liked that I could use my upper body strength. There was also a button on the bike that could help me out when going up a hill. I got to meet so many people like me, which sparked conversations, new relationships and friendships. I enjoyed seeing other people with disabilities overcoming their fears and conquering the trail with me.” 

Turner believes that these types of experiences bring communities together and raise more awareness for people with disabilities who also want to enjoy the outdoors.  

“People with disabilities are just people with different abilities,” Turner said. “Bringing more people together and getting everyone outside to have fun with each other would be huge for communities.” 

Blazing Trails is just the beginning for Hill and Butler.  

“Going forward, we hope to see even more civic participation to help people with disabilities access adaptive trails in Kentucky,” Butler said. 

Butler said the team also hopes that new policies, funding and structural changes will encourage the inclusion of adaptive design when initially constructing trails.  

“We are proud of the Blazing Trails project,” Hill said. “We are looking forward to more collaborative opportunities that create access to outdoor recreation for people here in Kentucky.” 

The Blazing Trails project included the following partners, sponsors and collaborators: Bluegrass Berms Composite Team; Broomwagon Bikes; Catalyst Sports; the Commonwealth Council on Developmental Disabilities; Community Innovation Lab; Kentucky Mountain Bike Association; Martin-Gatton CAFE; Midway University; UK College of Education; and UK HealthCare – Healthy Kentucky Initiative.

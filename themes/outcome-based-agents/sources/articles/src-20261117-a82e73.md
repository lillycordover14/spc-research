EMA Expands Prevention Options for Two Pediatric Infections&nbsp;&nbsp;Medscape

[page text]
The European Medicines Agency’s Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP) has recommended expanded pediatric protection against meningococcal group B disease and severe 
respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infection
. 
The changes would lower the minimum age for Bexsero (
meningococcal group B vaccine
, GlaxoSmithKline Vaccines) from 2 months to 6 weeks and extend use of the RSV monoclonal antibody Enflonsia (
clesrovimab
, Merck Sharp & Dohme Limited) to children up to 24 months of age who remain vulnerable to severe RSV disease through their second RSV season. 
Protection Against Meningococcal B Disease 
The Bexsero update addresses a disease with a substantial burden in early life. Invasive meningococcal disease can cause serious infections such as 
meningitis
 and septicemia, and serogroup B became the leading cause in Europe after the introduction of serogroup C vaccines. Young children face the highest risk, followed by adolescents. 
Bexsero is a multicomponent protein vaccine that targets surface antigens of group B meningococci to stimulate protective immunity.
Real-world effectiveness of Bexsero has also been evaluated in a Spanish nationwide matched 
case-control study
, which showed substantial protection against invasive meningococcal disease.
The vaccine is administered as an intramuscular injection, with age-based dosing schedules. Clinicians should consider age-specific disease burden and regional serogroup B antigen variability when planning vaccination.
Extending RSV Protection 
RSV is a major cause of lower respiratory tract infection in infants, accounting for an estimated 2.2 million hospitalizations annually worldwide among children younger than 1 year. The disease burden is highest during the first 6 months after birth, and most RSV-related hospitalizations occur in otherwise healthy, full-term infants. 
Enflonsia contains clesrovimab, a long-acting monoclonal antibody that binds the RSV fusion protein and helps prevent the virus from entering host cells.
Clinical evidence supporting Enflonsia includes the CLEVER and SMART studies, with SMART providing direct evidence for use in children who remain vulnerable through a second RSV season.
In the 
phase 2b/3 CLEVER trial
, clesrovimab reduced medically attended RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infection and hospitalization vs placebo in infants during their first RSV season.
In the 
phase 3 SMART trial
, 997 infants at increased risk for severe RSV disease received either clesrovimab or 
palivizumab
 during their first RSV season. Rates of medically attended RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infection were comparable between the groups through day 150. During the second RSV season, 276 children who remained at risk received clesrovimab 210 mg, with a cumulative incidence of medically attended RSV-associated lower respiratory tract infection of 7.3% through day 180.
Enflonsia is administered as a single 105-mg intramuscular injection into the thigh for first-season prevention. The CHMP noted limited data in infants weighing less than 1.1 kg.
Safety Profile and Regulatory Considerations 
Common adverse reactions with Enflonsia include injection-site pain, erythema, swelling, and rash. In CLEVER and SMART, serious adverse events and solicited local and systemic reactions were broadly comparable to placebo or palivizumab, with no new safety signals identified.
Both preventive therapies should be used according to official recommendations. Following European Commission approval of the latest indication changes, an updated Summary of Product Characteristics for each therapy will provide detailed guidance on their expanded use. Safety data will continue to be monitored, and suspected adverse effects will be evaluated to determine whether further action is needed to protect patients.

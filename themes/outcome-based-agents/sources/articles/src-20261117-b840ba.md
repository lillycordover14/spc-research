Political Philosophy on three tabs: A course review

Coffee wasn't going to cut it. Neither was my usual dusting of psilocybin.

Features

Colourful figure (Kaelem Tingate / The Muse)

Published:

November 17, 2026
 

Reading time: 
4
 min.

Author:
 
Kaelem Tingate

Most people drink coffee in the morning. There were two weeks till finals. Between papers and nights on George Street, it had been weeks since I’d had a proper night’s sleep. Coffee wasn’t going to cut it. Neither was my usual dusting of psilocybin. So, I tore three tabs from a strip and hopped in the shower. 

Acid is unpredictable; you never know when it’s going to hit — 20 minutes, an hour, two hours. Walking out of my house, I felt nauseous, unsure whether it was the drug or the absolute fear of what was to come. 

The salt-streaked sidewalk weaved with rainbow streaks, quickly whirling beneath my feet as I marched; the trees and houses along the sides arched, forming what felt like a tunnel for my walk to class. 

I arrived at class early; the room was empty — this was my chance. Run! I still have time. To do what? Study? Work on my overdue papers? Too late. Who would I be if I left now? 

The acid had long since taken hold, and I was moments away from turning into a jabbering beast more focused on the patterns in a blank wall than the world around me. 

The classroom pulsed like a beating heart coming to life, but it was slowly shrinking — I couldn’t look anywhere but the corner of the room. 

Then it happened: the classroom filled, one person after another, until there were four. Four students and a professor — five people, including me, the most recent victim of the Tour of Babel. 

As class began, the professor was disappointed by the turnout. I don’t blame her, but it’s not the students’ fault; the class was boring. Political Philosophy — exciting in theory, but the average person excited by political philosophy is best described as unbearable. Maybe today would be different? 

The professor was young — she was out for blood, wanting to put students through the same ringer she experienced herself. Somehow, you’d leave class feeling bad that you came, but worse if you missed it. 

She began handing out sheets of paper. 

Once she got to me, I reached for the paper, and she looked at me with sheer horror, as if I tried to stab her. Had I? Did I say something? Does she know? Or maybe I simply reached for the paper with the spatial awareness of a half-blind toddler. Compose yourself.

When she returned to the front, I took a moment before she stared directly at me — this was a conversation class. 

“What did you think of the reading?”
 she asked. She couldn’t be talking to anyone else; I was on my own, no one behind me. 

Shuffling through the papers, I couldn’t make out a word—they looked like Sanskrit, with blue streaks and blotches of purple whirling about the page. I would have had more luck had it been written in Klingon. 

“That’s the wrong reading.”
 There were two sheets of paper in my hands, melting and slipping into one. 

My heart sank. 
“Sorry, I’m just not really here right now,”
 I muttered. 

From that point on, she didn’t look at me once. Was it out of fear, pity, or annoyance? No one wanted to attend her class, and one of the four that showed up had a head full of acid. 

Maybe she didn’t know, or maybe she didn’t care. I was probably paying more attention than usual — at least I wasn’t sleeping. Maybe I’d have more luck listening to the conversation. The voices sounded warped, distorted, and just about impossible to understand; all I knew was that it was pretentious. 

This class was a write-off; I was better off sitting back and enjoying the ride. 

As I settled into my seat, the dust on the chalkboard reached out, like fog in a 3D movie. The suspended ceiling’s grid-like pattern meshed and moved, layering with the dots of its panels. The walls, the people, the furniture had all become cheap plastic left in the sun for too long. 

What was supposed to be an hour and fifteen-minute class became a day. Time slowed to a crawl, as I was surrounded by the most uninteresting and unintelligible conversation imaginable — not that I usually understood this class. 

By the end of class, I had both of my jackets back on. I was cold, but sweating profusely—a shivering sensation like electricity formed beneath my skin, it was as though my whole body was asleep — for a moment, I thought I was having a seizure. 

The papers in front of me were covered in scribbles, but one thing stood out: circled, underlined, and emboldened at the top was the title: 
“The Commons.” 
I tenderly folded the handout and eased it into my pocket — I’d need it for later, that was certain; it was more notes than I‘d taken all semester.

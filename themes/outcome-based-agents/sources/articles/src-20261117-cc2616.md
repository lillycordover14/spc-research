Builder confidence hits a 14-year low

The 
National Association of Home Builders/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index
 fell four points to 25 in November. Any reading below 50 means more builders see conditions as poor than good.

November’s builder survey by the numbers

Overall index:
 25, down from 29, the lowest since April 2012

Builders cutting prices:
 46%, the highest share since the pandemic

Average price cut:
 6%, unchanged for an eighth straight month

Builders offering incentives:
 68%, also a post-pandemic high

Buyer traffic index:
 16, its lowest in 15 years outside the start of the pandemic in 2020

High rates and costs are keeping buyers away

Builders pointed to high mortgage rates, rising material and labor costs, and hesitant buyers. The average 30-year fixed rate was 7.339% on Nov. 17, up from 7.021% a week earlier, according to 
Mortgage Research Center data reported by Fortune
.

“Mortgage rates above 7%, softer consumer confidence and an uncertain economic outlook are holding buyers back.”

Robert Dietz, chief economist, National Association of Home Builders

NAHB Chairman Bill Owens said 
“with fewer buyers in the market, more builders are scaling back construction.”

How buyers can use builder price cuts

When builders struggle to sell, you can often negotiate on more than the sticker price. What a given builder will offer still varies widely by market, community and how long a home has been sitting.

Price cut vs. rate buydown: get both offers in dollars

Many builders will either lower the price or pay to 
buy down your mortgage rate
. They help you in different ways.

A price cut
 lowers your loan amount, your down payment and often your property tax bill, and it lasts for as long as you own the home.

A rate buydown
 lowers your monthly payment, sometimes by more than a price cut would. But a 
temporary buydown like a 2-1
 only lowers the rate for the first year or two.

Illustrative example:
 On a $450,000 home with 10% down, a 30-year loan at 7.3% has a principal and interest payment of about $2,777 a month. A 6% price cut, with 10% down on the new price, brings that to about $2,610. A permanent rate 1 percentage point lower (6.3%) on the original price brings it to about $2,507.

Those two offers cost the builder different amounts, so ask for each one in writing and compare the payment, cash to close and how long you plan to keep the loan. If you might refinance in a few years, a permanent price cut keeps paying off after the buydown’s value ends.

Closing costs: ask the builder to cover them

Builder-paid closing costs reduce the cash you need at the table. Your loan program limits how much a seller can contribute, so check with your lender before you agree to terms. Our guide to 
getting sellers to pay your closing costs
 explains how the limits work.

The builder’s lender: compare before you commit

Builder incentives often require you to use the builder’s affiliated lender. That can be a good deal, but not always. Get at least one outside quote and compare the full Loan Estimate, not just the rate. Read more on 
why builder incentives can cost more in the long run
.

Don’t skip the inspection on a new home

A builder trying to move inventory may be stretched thin. An independent inspection, before closing and again before your warranty ends, can catch problems early. See our 
guide to buying a new construction home
.

Bottom line: New-home buyers have leverage

Builders are cutting prices and offering incentives at the highest rates since the pandemic. If you’re shopping for a new home, ask for both a price cut and a rate buydown in writing, compare the numbers, and shop your mortgage with more than one lender. Rates and terms vary by lender and borrower.

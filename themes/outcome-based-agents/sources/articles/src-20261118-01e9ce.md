Fed Governor Waller Turns Hawkish: Either a 50-Basis-Point Hike in December or Signal January Tightening First

US Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller has once again sounded an inflation alarm. In an interview on Tuesday, he said bluntly that inflation is still moving in the wrong direction and that price increases are spreading from a handful of items to a broader range. If the Fed truly wants to bring prices down, it must adopt tougher measures; if policymakers do not intend to signal more tightening in the coming months, then the December meeting should deliver a 50-basis-point rate hike in one move.

Waller noted that recent annualized inflation has breached 3% and could even be heading toward 3.5%. He specifically pointed out that roughly 70% of items in the core services basket have risen more than 3%, "and this has nothing to do with oil," indicating that price pressures are not coming from temporary factors such as energy. He worries that corporate management has come to view price increases above 3% as normal and is passing them on to consumers. Once that mindset becomes entrenched, suppressing inflation later will come at a higher cost.

In his view, the market's acceptance of current price levels exceeds expectations, and that is precisely the problem. The Fed needs to use "shock" tactics to make clear that a 3% inflation rate is not the authority's target, and to overturn the expectation that the Fed will hike only once more and by just a quarter point. He stressed that communication itself is a policy tool: the Fed can use the post-meeting statement or press conference to clearly lay out its stance on further rate hikes, or otherwise it must act directly with a 50-basis-point hike at the December meeting.

Year-End Projections Summary Poor as a Signal; Officials Turn to Verbal Guidance

Waller also poured cold water on the Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) due in December. He believes the projections, based on year-end data, have limited reference value, and the Fed needs other means to convey a more aggressive anti-inflation stance. His remarks contrast with Chair Kevin Warsh's preference for avoiding "forward guidance." Waller argues that precisely because the chair dislikes giving guidance, market communication should be used all the more as a weapon to suppress inflation expectations.

Notably, Waller's position has been gradually escalating. On November 10 in London, he said that even if October inflation cooled, he would still lean toward supporting a quarter-point hike in December; but if October core Consumer Price Index (CPI) rose 0.3% or more month-over-month, a 50-basis-point hike should be considered, because raising rates by only a quarter point in that scenario could damage the Fed's credibility. Data subsequently released showed October core CPI rose 0.3% month-over-month, holding the same pace for a third consecutive month and landing exactly on the threshold he set.

The Fed's next rate decision meeting is scheduled for December 8-9. At the October 27-28 meeting, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) left rates unchanged at 3.75%-4.00%, with Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissenting in favor of a hike. Since that meeting, most policy signals about the next step have been conveyed to the market through officials' public remarks.

Hawkish voices within the policy circle are indeed growing louder. In addition to Waller's call for a 50-basis-point hike in December, Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan wants at least two more quarter-point hikes, possibly at a faster pace than this autumn; Hammack, Anna Paulson and John Williams support continuing at a smaller pace. Market observers note that after the October CPI release, the hawkishness of FOMC voting members has risen to its highest level of this tightening cycle, and unlike past cycles, the gauge has continued to climb after the first hike in September, meaning members see the terminal rate as further away than at the start.

Payments Innovation Speech Coincides; Central Bank Money's Role Unchanged

Even as his rate stance turned hawkish, Waller also delivered a speech focused on payments innovation on the 17th at The Clearing House annual conference in New York. He said that as stablecoins and asset tokenization enter traditional financial markets, existing payment systems will still play a key role, and central bank money remains the foundation of trust in the payment system.

He analyzed that tokenization of financial assets has extended from crypto asset trading into traditional finance; blockchain can operate around the clock and smart contracts can automatically execute some processes, helping shorten settlement times; stablecoins, tokenized bank deposits and tokenized money market funds can coexist to meet different transaction needs. However, he cautioned that operators must still clarify asset ownership, transaction liability, cybersecurity, and legal protections in the event a platform fails.

Waller stressed that central bank money need not be involved in every transaction, but remains indispensable to the overall payment system. The Fed will continue to provide core payment and settlement infrastructure, monitor financial stability risks, and work with private-sector operators to support innovation.

Market Reaction and What to Watch Next

Traders continue to bet on consecutive Fed rate hikes, and the US Dollar Index rose intraday to its highest level since April 2025, trading near 103.82. Market focus is shifting to the upcoming minutes of the October meeting, to see whether most members already expected more hikes at the time.

For investors, the key to this signal is not whether the Fed hikes a quarter point or 50 basis points in December, but whether it formally bids farewell to the old framework that "3% inflation is acceptable." If the post-meeting statement or press conference clearly lays out a path for further hikes in January, short-term US Treasury yields and the dollar will react first, and valuation pressure on growth stocks and highly valued assets will rise in tandem; conversely, if communication is vague and only a quarter-point hike is delivered, the market may read it as the Fed conceding on inflation, and once inflation expectations become unanchored, the future policy cost will be even higher. The December 8-9 meeting will be the most telling communication test since Warsh took office.

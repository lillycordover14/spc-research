Quick Read

Using net unrealized appreciation on $1 million of employer stock taxes only the $150,000 basis as ordinary income, cutting the federal bill from roughly $279,000 to $149,000.

Selling all the shares in one year triggers top-tier Medicare surcharges, the 3.8% investment income tax and the 20% gains rate, so spreading sales helps.

A 65-year-old engineer retiring from a chipmaker holds $1 million of company stock in his 401(k). He is married and files jointly, and the couple draws $211,000 from a combination of pension and Social Security income. In this example, his plan statement shows a cost basis of $150,000 on those shares. The choice he makes before signing a rollover form is worth tens of thousands of dollars.

If he rolls everything into an IRA, every dollar eventually comes out as ordinary income. If he uses 
net unrealized appreciation (NUA)
, he pays ordinary rates only on the cost basis now. Everything above that waits for 
capital gains treatment
.

How NUA Turns $850,000 of Growth Into Capital Gains

The tax code excludes employer share appreciation from income. This happens when they leave the plan in a lump sum distribution (26 U.S.C. § 402(e)(4)(B)
[
1
]
). A lump sum pays out the entire plan balance within one taxable year
[
1
]
. This occurs after a trigger such as separation from service or reaching age 59½
[
1
]
. The shares move in kind to a taxable brokerage account, and the cash and funds roll to an IRA.

If he takes the distribution in January, the $150,000 basis shows up on his 2027 return as ordinary income. The remaining $850,000 is taxed as a long-term capital gain whenever he sells, even if he sells the next day.

Here is how the two paths compare. Taken from an IRA, that growth stacks on top of his other income and climbs through four brackets, from 24% to 37%. Add it up, and the federal bill comes to roughly $279,000.

The 15% capital gains rate covers joint taxable income up to $613,700, but $850,000 stacked on his other income, including the $150,000 basis from the same year, pushes part of it into the 20% bracket above that. The real NUA bill totals approximately $157,000 and still saves him roughly $122,000.

Selling Everything in One Year Sets Off the Medicare Surcharge

The exclusion ends when he sells. Capital gains count toward the income Medicare uses to set its surcharge, called IRMAA, and Medicare looks back two years. His 2027 sales would set his 2029 premiums.

Under 2026 brackets, selling all the shares in one year pushes joint income past $750,000
[
2
]
, the top level. Each spouse would pay $689.90
[
2
]
 a month for Part B instead of the standard $202.90. That adds $11,688 a year for the couple.

Part D adds up to $91
[
2
]
 a month per person on top of that. The same sale also crosses the $250,000
[
3
]
 joint threshold for the 
3.8% net investment income tax
, and gains above the 15% ceiling are taxed at 20%.

The year of the distribution needs planning too. His $211,000 in other income is inching close to the $218,000 joint line, where the standard premium applies. So adding $150,000 pushes him into IRMAA turf that year, with the tier yet to be determined. Waiting until January, after his final paycheck, keeps his salary out of that year’s income. A 30% drop on a $1 million position wipes out $300,000, far more than the worst-case annual Medicare surcharge. The stronger plan is to choose NUA and sell a large portion in year one. He should keep a limited stock position. It should be an amount he could watch fall by half without changing his retirement, and accept one year in a higher IRMAA level. He can then sell the rest over two or three years while staying inside the 15% bracket.

Three Moves to Make Before the Plan Cuts a Check

Ask your plan administrator for the cost basis of each lot of shares, since NUA pays off most when the basis is a small share of market value. Some plans let you take low-basis lots as shares and roll high-basis lots into the IRA, which reduces the ordinary income bill.

Keep the full balance intact until the lump-sum distribution is sent. A payout in an earlier tax year after you separate from service can disqualify the distribution under the “one taxable year” requirement. Schedule the full payout for January so salary lands on a separate year’s return.

Before each sale, check it against two lines: the $218,000 IRMAA threshold and the $250,000 investment income tax threshold and if your final year of salary pushes up your 2028 premiums, file Form SSA-44 and cite the work stoppage. That asks Social Security to use your lower income after retirement.

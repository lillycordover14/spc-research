Built to last: Casper carpenter carries artisan woodworking craft into the future&nbsp;&nbsp;Oil City News

[page text]
CASPER, Wyo. –– The carefully organized tools lining most available wall space in Atlas Cabinet Shop number in the hundreds, each one covered in well-earned patina.

Together they might tell a story, at least for someone who values the art and craft of woodworking. There’s one modest set above a doorway, however, with perhaps the most interesting background.

“Yeah, those are his tools right there, up in that walnut case,” said Travis Wade. “That was when he was working out of the back of his Buick; he just had all these tools and hand saws.”

The man in question was the late Martin Sheldon, who spent his life chasing perfection, suffering splinters and carving out a living in carpentry. Martin passed away in 2018 after battling cancer, but his tools live on. Most importantly, the decades of knowledge and experience also live on in Travis.

Tools once owned and used by Martin Sheldon hang over a doorway at Atlas Cabinet Shop in Mills. Sheldon mentored current owner Travis Wade before Travis purchased the building and business after Sheldon’s passing. (Dan Cepeda, Oil City News)

Travis, a Casper native, had wanted to create with his hands for as long as he could remember. “I went to college to be a music major,” he said. That was before he found out he’d soon be a father. “I was like, ‘Ooh, I got to get a job.'”

He opened up the newspaper classified section and almost immediately zeroed in on a carpentry job. “I always thought carpentry was cool,” he said. The ad was looking for labor to help build the C’Mon Inn in Casper, expanding a brand known for its woodsy, rustic hotel design. With no real building experience, he was hired to schlepp drywall and other supplies around the site and assist the crews with their daily duties. It wasn’t long before he was recruited by one of the framers, though — one who would have a key influence on his eventual career.

“They were a group of guys who traveled all over the country to build these hotels,” he said. “The one guy I happened to luck out with picked me up and said, ‘I can make you a competent carpenter in three months if you just shadow me.'”

The man was in charge of hanging the doors in every room, and he was good at it. “He was methodical,” Travis said. “He’d take time to meticulously set everything up, and it would take him almost a whole day to hang a door because he was so particular about it. He did everything with intention and care.”

Tools line the walls at Atlas Cabinet Shop in Mills. (Dan Cepeda, Oil City News)

Travis’s inner perfectionist was hooked. “When that job was done, I went and got a job framing houses, and that’s all we did.” 

It paid the bills, but it didn’t scratch the creative itch. Travis recalls one job building a restaurant during which he was tasked with the exterior trim. He pored over vintage Popular Mechanics woodworking books for a technique and design ideas requiring real skill, coming up with some ideas he presented to the owner. “He was like, ‘Just do whatever’s cheapest and fastest,'” Travis recalled, “and I decided I had to get out of this ‘cheaper and faster’ thing.”

He started popping into local cabinetry shops before eventually running into Martin, quietly concentrating in an aging Quonset Hut in Mills. “I walked in and he was sitting in the corner, this big and imposing old-timey sort of guy,” he said. Martin was just looking at boards propped up against the wall. “I asked him what he was doing, and he’s like, ‘Studying the grain,’ and I was like, ‘All right, this is the place.'”

Martin was a hard sell, however. As soon as Travis told him he was a framer, Martin said, “You’re already ruined.”

Travis Wade works on a project at Atlas Cabinet Shop in Mills. (Dan Cepeda, Oil City News)

“He got up and left, and I just followed him around and finally got him to talk to me,” Travis said. With steady persistence, Martin warmed up and gave him a part-time gig. He kept his steady framing job to support his family for a while before assisting Martin turned into his full-time job. “I’ve been here ever since.”

He credited Martin for reinforcing his work ethic and love of detail, and — perhaps most importantly — for taking pride in the work. “Martin would say, if you’re a ditch digger, you should try and build the best ditch you possibly can,” he said. “If you’re a gas station attendant, grocery clerk, whatever you’re doing. That’s the same with all trades, arts, crafts, professions.”

After Martin died, Travis decided it was up to him to keep the craft and business alive. “It was scary; he was in debt and he didn’t own the property at the time,” he said. He ended up being a good fit, though, and the business keeps growing.

The selection of projects Travis takes on is nothing to shake a chisel at. Most are kitchens and bathrooms, but recently, the main shop was taken up with a massive frame and false door for what will become a custom Murphy bed. He and his son, Michael, worked to meticulously measure and place pieces of wood onto the main bed door, ensuring everything was cut properly, all the while studying the grain, the fit and the geometry.

Michael started working at the shop after school about five years ago, and has essentially become Travis’s right-hand man.

Michael Wade collects wood from the warehouse at Atlas Cabinet Shop in Mills. (Dan Cepeda, Oil City News)

“It’s mentally stimulating,” Michael said. “It’s nice to be able to change up what you’re doing day to day, week to week.” Learning on the job and trying not to screw anything up can be stressful, he admitted, but added that the work is increasingly rewarding.

“It could be really difficult at times, but more and more it’s obvious how important this can be in my life,” he said. 

The wood speaks its own language, something Travis learned long ago from Martin. He can go on about the grain, feel and pliability of various woods. “Cypress is beautiful and it’s got a lot of really cool characteristics, but it’s also soft so it’s not very forgiving,” he said. “You start out with an ideal when you’re building something, but there are always little things that pop up, but the goal is to be as close as possible to that ideal.”

Travis Wade checks wooden dowels on a door for a Murphy bed at Atlas Cabinet Shop in Mills. (Dan Cepeda, Oil City News)

A recent project involved huge wooden doors, commissioned by St. Mark’s Episcopal Church to replace some ’50s-era doors that had weathered beyond repair. The technically demanding job came their way after other carpenters turned it down. “Those doors weighed about 280 pounds each,” he said. “These were nuts, dude. They had all this tracery; hand-carved mahogany; solid, stave-core laminations. If you don’t have the practice, you can’t take it on.”

It took about four months of solid work to get those doors finished, he said, and many more hours than expected to get them moved and installed at the historic building. “We could’ve built eight kitchens by that time and made a lot more money, but if you did that you’d have just built a bunch of kitchens. These will be there possibly for centuries,” he said.

In some ways, those massive church doors owe their very existence to Travis’s mentor. While Martin’s oldest tools are retired, resting quietly above a doorway in that aging Quonset Hut in Mills, his craftsmanship and experience will live on for decades.

Travis Wade, center, works with his son Michael on a door for a hidden Murphy bed at Atlas Cabinet Shop in Mills. (Dan Cepeda, Oil City News)

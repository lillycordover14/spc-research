Cataract Surgery With Anterior Vitrectomy Raises Intraocular Pressure Spike Risk&nbsp;&nbsp;Ophthalmology Advisor

[page text]
Patients who require an anterior vitrectomy during phacoemulsification frequently experience early postoperative intraocular pressure elevations that typically resolve within 2 weeks.

Nearly 1 in 10 eyes undergoing cataract surgery with concurrent anterior vitrectomy experience a postoperative intraocular pressure (IOP) spike, with glaucoma, male sex, and worse baseline visual acuity identified as independent risk factors, according to results of a study published in 
Ophthalmology Glaucoma
.

In this retrospective cohort study, investigators analyzed data from the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s Intelligent Research in Sight (IRIS) Registry to evaluate postoperative IOP changes among eyes undergoing phacoemulsification with concurrent anterior vitrectomy between January 2013 and September 2019. Daily postoperative IOP measurements were compared with baseline values through postoperative day 90. An IOP spike was defined as pressure exceeding 30 mm Hg and increasing by more than 10 mm Hg from baseline within the first postoperative week. 

The analysis included 1811 eyes, of which 573 (31.6%) were classified as having glaucoma or related conditions. The median patient age was 70 years, and the median baseline IOP was 16.0 mm Hg.

Postoperative IOP spikes occurred in 164 eyes (9.1%), nearly 3 times the 3.7% rate previously reported following uncomplicated cataract surgery. Median IOP initially increased to 18.0 mm Hg and remained generally elevated above baseline through the second postoperative week. IOP subsequently stabilized, with final mean pressures of 16.57 mm Hg across all eyes, 17.69 mm Hg among eyes with glaucoma, and 16.1 mm Hg among eyes without glaucoma.

There is an initial IOP spike most frequently the day following surgery, but then IOP returns to baseline approximately two weeks postoperatively.

Most IOP spikes occurred on postoperative day 1, accounting for 82.3% of observed spikes. Among eyes experiencing a spike, those with glaucoma had significantly higher IOP than those without glaucoma on postoperative day 7 (
P
 =.046).

In the overall cohort, glaucoma was associated with 71% higher odds of an IOP spike (odds ratio [OR], 1.71; 95% CI, 1.17–2.50; 
P
 =.005). Men were also associated with increased odds (OR, 1.64; 95% CI, 1.15–2.37; 
P
 =.007), as was worse baseline visual acuity (OR, 1.07 per 0.2 logMAR increase; 95% CI, 1.01–1.13; 
P
 =.02).

Among eyes with glaucoma, male sex was associated with more than twice the odds of an IOP spike (OR, 2.36; 95% CI, 1.32–4.32; 
P
 =.004). Each 10-year increase in age was associated with 45% higher odds (OR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.08–1.97; 
P
 =.02), while Hispanic ethnicity was associated with increased odds compared with non-Hispanic White patients (OR, 2.30; 95% CI, 1.07–4.80; 
P
 =.03). Among eyes without glaucoma, worse baseline visual acuity was the only significant predictor (OR, 1.09 per 0.2 logMAR increase; 95% CI, 1.00–1.18; 
P
 =.04).

Study limitations include the retrospective registry-based design, reliance on historical rather than contemporaneous matched comparisons with uncomplicated cataract surgery, and potential misclassification resulting from variations in clinical documentation and procedural coding.

“The current study furthers our understanding of IOP changes following cataract surgery requiring AV. There is an initial IOP spike most frequently the day following surgery, but then IOP returns to baseline approximately two weeks postoperatively,” according to the study authors.

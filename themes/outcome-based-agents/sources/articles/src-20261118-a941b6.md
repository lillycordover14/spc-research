Mixed with water and sprayed as a fine particle film on plants, kaolin clay powder is well known in Florida for protecting citrus orchards from insects and heat stress, but how would it work on a row crop like soybeans in the Mississippi Delta?

Two recently published studies by researchers with the Arkansas Agricultural Experiment Station and a U.S. Department of Agriculture research station in Mississippi showed that it worked quite well, providing protection from insects and heat stress.

"We knew kaolin worked well in other cropping systems, but we had never really studied it in row crops such as soybean," said Justin George, research entomologist with the USDA Agricultural Research Service's Southern Insect Management Research Unit in Stoneville, Mississippi, and corresponding author of the studies. "We wanted to know how it affected insect behavior, pest populations and plant physiology, and whether it could fit into an integrated pest management strategy."

The 
first study
, published last June in 
Pest Management Science
, examined how kaolin particle films affected egg-laying behavior, feeding and development of insects.

The 
second study
, published in July in 
Field Crops Research
, expanded the work to field plots, evaluating pest populations, plant performance and economic impacts.

                Credit: Rahul Debnath
            
 

Deterring pests before damage begins

The research focused on soybean looper moths and southern green stink bugs, two common soybean pests with different feeding habits.

Adult soybean looper moths avoided laying eggs on soybean leaves treated with kaolin. The offspring of looper moths that did manage to lay eggs on the treated plants had difficulty feeding.

"Even when some eggs were laid, the early instars were not able to feed and develop successfully on treated surfaces," George said.

Results differed for stink bugs, said Rupesh Kariyat, a co-author of the studies and a professor of crop entomology and Clyde H. Sites Professor of International Crop Physiology in the Department of Entomology and Plant Pathology for the experiment station, the research arm of the University of Arkansas Division of Agriculture, and the Dale Bumpers College of Agricultural, Food and Life Sciences at the University of Arkansas.

Stink bugs feed by inserting specialized piercing and sucking mouthparts directly into plant tissue.

"When an insect is essentially drinking from the plant's plumbing, it can sometimes feed despite the coating," Kariyat said. "The different feeding styles explain much of what we observed."

But that's where the insecticide comes in.

A tool for integrated pest management

George and Kariyat emphasized that kaolin is intended to complement, not replace, conventional insecticides as part of integrated pest management, or IPM, programs.

However, reducing insecticide use may help slow the development of insecticide resistance while reducing potential impacts on beneficial insects and surrounding ecosystems.

"We're not saying insecticides are bad, but we need to have a balance," George said. "We can reduce the amount of insecticides that we put out there, which is a major expense for growers. If producers combine kaolin with insecticides, they might be able to reduce costs and get better results."

The research found that treatments combining kaolin with half-rate insecticide applications provided pest control comparable to full-rate insecticide applications.

The field trials also showed that plots treated with kaolin alone generally supported fewer pest insects than untreated plots.

"The goal of integrated pest management is to identify additional tools that can reduce the burden of chemical inputs while maintaining effective pest control," Kariyat said. "Any strategy that safely reduces the amount of an active ingredient applied over time can have benefits both within and beyond the field."

A bonus is that the kaolin clay powder did not gunk up the tank sprayers during the research.

"You can just add the powder to the tank mix with insecticides and spray it," George said. "The only thing is, if sprayers don't have the tanks with agitators, it can get settled."

With a high-pressure sprayer and no tank agitator, however, George's crew had no issues spraying during 30- to 45-minute sessions.

Benefits beyond insect control

Another aspect of the research was examining how kaolin clay alone affected soybean plants, regardless of insect presence.

The studies noted that kaolin-treated soybean plants showed reduced heat stress and increased chlorophyll content compared with untreated plants. As it does in citrus orchards, the thin, reflective clay coating on soybean leaves helped moderate temperature stress while allowing normal plant function.

Regarding the clay's impact on photosynthesis, Kariyat said the physiological measurements were included primarily to ensure the treatments were not negatively affecting the plants, and that additional research is needed to better understand how the coating influences the host plant traits throughout the growing season.

"Physiology was part of the study just to make sure that we were not affecting the ability of these plants to intercept light, and that clearly doesn't happen," Kariyat said. "If you look at different photosynthetic parameters, is there something else going on? We don't know that yet. This would be a great place to use the information we collected and understand that in more detail."

Reducing costs while preserving effectiveness

While the studies suggest kaolin can help protect soybean plants without disrupting normal plant function, the researchers said its practical value for growers may ultimately come from its potential to reduce pest management costs.

Cost analyses showed kaolin-only treatments had the lowest costs, followed by kaolin-plus-insecticide treatments, while insecticide-only programs had the highest costs.

"Use of half insecticide and half kaolin may help reduce insecticide costs by about $20 to $25 per hectare when compared to insecticide alone," George said. "That's based on four to five applications per season."

That translates to roughly $8 to $10 per acre in reduced insecticide costs.

The researchers cautioned that the study was conducted in small research plots and that larger-scale field trials are still needed to confirm economic benefits under commercial production conditions.

Kaolin clay is widely available in the United States through agricultural suppliers and is 
approved by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency for use as a biopesticide
. The clay is also listed by the 
U.S. Food and Drug Administration
 as "GRAS," or generally recognized as safe, for specified indirect food-contact uses.

The recommended application rate used in the study was about 10 pounds per hectare, or approximately 4 pounds per acre. The researchers used Surround WP kaolin clay from NovaSource in the experiments, which sells for less than $1 per pound when bought in bulk.

Given the millions of soybean acres grown across the United States, including 3 million acres (1.2 million hectares) in Arkansas alone, George said even modest improvements in pest management efficiency could have significant impacts. He said the findings suggest kaolin clay has potential as a practical addition to integrated pest management programs in soybean production.

Clay figurines from the Middle East suggest that artistic traditions were already being shared across generations before farming, possibly with children taking part in their creation.
Long before agriculture transformed life in the Middle East, people were already shaping clay into figures that reflected the world around them. Some resembled birds, mammals, and humans, while others carried traces of red or orange coloring. One fragment may even depict the facial markings of a mountain gazelle.
These small artifacts, originally excavated from three sites in present-day Israel, date back roughly 15,000 to 11,500 years. Researchers believe they provide the earliest known evidence of a clay figurine-making tradition in the region, suggesting that people were using the material for symbolic expression much earlier than previously thought.
Hunter-gatherers worked with clay before farming
At the time, the region was home to Natufian communities, hunter-gatherers who had begun living in permanent settlements before the development of agriculture. Archaeologists already knew that Natufian people made figurines, although most previously discovered examples were fashioned from bone or stone.
Natufian figurines depicting waterfowls, a bird of prey and a human-bird (woman-goose) interaction, modelled in clay 15,000 years ago (left) and 12,000 years ago (right), discovered at the sites of Eynan-Mallaha and Nahal Ein Gev II (Upper Jordan Valley). Credit: Laurent Davin, 
CC-BY 4.0
Clay figurines, by contrast, were generally believed to have become common during the Neolithic period, when the agricultural revolution began around 11,500 years ago. The newly identified objects suggest that the practice had much deeper roots, revealing a wider range of artistic expression among these early communities.
Children may have helped shape ancient art
The findings also raise questions about how cultural traditions developed before farming. The researchers suggest that Natufian artistic practices were more varied and complex than previously recognized, and that some aspects of the region’s later Neolithic culture may have originated thousands of years earlier.
The research, published November 11, 2026, in the open-access journal 
PLOS One
, was conducted by Dr. Laurent Davin of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem in Israel and colleagues. Beyond revealing an earlier history of clay figurines, the study offers clues about how creative practices and cultural knowledge may have passed between generations.
A Natufian figurine depicting a stylized human body modelled in clay 15,000 years ago, discovered at the site of Eynan-Mallaha (Upper Jordan Valley). The back of the clay figurine bears the fingerprint of an adult, covered by a child’s fingerprint. This sequence of gestures in the wet clay, when the figurine was being modelled, is a rare direct testimony of inter-generational knowledge transmission in a hands-on apprenticeship. Credit: Laurent Davin, 
CC-BY 4.0
“These discoveries change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in the first villages of humanity. Thousands of years before agriculture and pottery emerged, people were already using clay to create figures, as part of their beliefs and knowledge of the natural world. What is particularly fascinating is the evidence that children were involved in making these objects, suggesting that creativity and cultural knowledge were shared across generations,” said Davin.

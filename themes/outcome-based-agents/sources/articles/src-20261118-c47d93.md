As its customer base extends beyond Texas, Araya Artisan Chocolate is working through the precise timing, varied recipes, and freshness requirements that make handmade chocolate difficult to scale.

The Houston company is experiencing a familiar challenge for artisan food businesses: demand can grow faster than the production methods built to serve it. Increasing output requires attention to the individual steps that determine how a chocolate looks, feels, and tastes.

Founded in 2010, 
Araya Artisan Chocolate
 makes its confections in small batches at its Heights chocolate laboratory. Its reach includes three Houston-area boutiques, Central Market stores across Texas, select luxury hotels and corporate customers nationwide.

That combination brings a practical question into focus for owner Giusse Sicoli: how can the business accommodate more orders while giving each batch the attention its products require?

Chocolate Has Its Own Timetable

Handmade chocolate depends on a sequence of steps that cannot all be accelerated together.

Tempering, the controlled heating and cooling of chocolate, helps establish the cocoa butter structure responsible for a firm, glossy finish. A chocolatier then has to work with that chocolate under conditions that allow it to form properly and release cleanly from its mold.

The filling introduces another set of requirements. In its 
guidance on filling molded chocolates
, Callebaut explains that a filling can melt the shell if it is too warm. The amount deposited also matters: too much makes sealing difficult, while too little leaves unwanted space inside.

These details explain why an increase in orders involves more than multiplying a recipe. A faster filling step is useful only when enough shells are ready, and additional molds need somewhere suitable to cool and set.

For a growing artisan operation, the challenge is getting the stages to support one another. Otherwise, increasing the speed of one task can simply create a queue at the next.

This is the challenge Araya is working through as demand grows across its stores, retail partners, and corporate accounts: increasing the number of chocolates it can make while preserving the time and attention each batch requires.

Courtesy of Araya Artisan Chocolate

An Assortment Is Several Products Made Together

Variety adds another layer of work.

A box containing different flavors draws on multiple preparations, each of which must be available in the quantities needed for packing. Having plenty of one chocolate does little to complete an assortment that needs several others.

Araya’s 
Diwali collection
, for example, draws from twelve alcohol-free flavors, including chai spice, blueberry cardamom, ginger, pistachio and passion fruit. The collection combines ingredients ranging from tea and spices to nuts and fruit fillings.

For the customer, those differences create a tasting experience. From a production perspective, an assortment requires coordination across recipes, ingredients, and finished pieces.

As order quantities increase, planning the mix becomes as relevant as planning the total. A producer must consider which varieties are needed together and how their preparation fits into the available working day.

This is one reason a small-batch business can become more complicated as it grows even when its recipes remain familiar. The individual chocolates may be established products, but supplying more combinations creates additional scheduling demands.

Freshness Makes Inventory a Different Calculation

Making everything far in advance would appear to simplify production. Fresh confections impose limits on that approach.

Araya makes its chocolates without preservatives. Its Diwali collection advises customers to enjoy the chocolates within 14 days, an indication of the relatively short enjoyment window around which these products are sold.

That puts timing alongside quantity as a production consideration. More finished stock is useful only when it can reach customers in suitable condition and within the intended freshness window.

Delivery creates further constraints. Araya’s shipping policy limits dispatches to Monday through Wednesday to reduce the likelihood of packages spending a weekend in a carrier warehouse. The company also monitors weather and routes, adding insulation and cold packs when needed.

Those practices show how far the work extends beyond the chocolate laboratory. A batch has to fit into a dispatch schedule, and a packed order has to withstand the conditions along its route.

For an artisan chocolatier with customers outside its home market, production planning and delivery planning are closely connected. A larger output must still fit the calendar for getting a delicate product to its destination.

Finding Capacity Within the Craft

Araya is exploring how equipment could support this work, including a recent trip to Italy to meet chocolate machinery manufacturers.

The practical value of any investment will depend on where it fits into the existing operation. Equipment that helps maintain working conditions or handle repetitive tasks could give a production team more room to manage the preparations and finishing that require close attention.

The evaluation also has to account for the range of products being made. A machine’s output figure alone cannot explain how well it will work across different recipes, batch sizes and production schedules.

For Araya, the goal is to support a business that already reaches customers through several channels while retaining small-batch preparation, high-quality ingredients and preservative-free recipes.

Its growth brings the less visible work of artisan chocolate into view. Behind an attractive assortment is a sequence of decisions about temperature, timing, quantities, and transport, repeated across every order.

The next stage of expansion will depend on making that sequence work for more customers while preserving the care inside each box.

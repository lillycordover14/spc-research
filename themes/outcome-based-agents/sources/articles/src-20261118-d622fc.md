Abstract
Mammalian nitric oxide synthase (NOS) generates nitric oxide (NO), an essential signaling molecule in neurotransmission, inflammation and cardiovascular regulation. NOS dysregulation contributes to neurodegeneration, septic shock and ischemia. The structural basis of NOS function remains unclear, likely because of its dynamic, multicomponent architecture. Catalysis requires electron transfer across five cofactors, with the oxygenase (Oxy) domain converting 
l
-arginine to NO after receiving electrons from the reductase (Red) domain through calmodulin (CaM) activation. Here, we report cryo-electron microscopy structures of the human inducible NOS:CaM holoenzyme, revealing an architecture in which the Red:CaM arm spans the Oxy dimer in an apparent active state for cross-monomer electron transfer. The flavin mononucleotide subdomain is rotated ~90° relative to the Red core, positioning its cofactor ~9 Å from the heme, adjacent to a conserved tryptophan residue within a narrow Oxy domain tunnel. Red–Oxy flexibility in the active state may facilitate electron shuttling among cofactors, defining how NOS cofactors are transiently aligned to enable catalysis.

These mechanistic gaps prompted us to focus on the structural characterization of human iNOS, which binds constitutively to CaM and lacks additional flexible regulatory or autoinhibitory elements found in other NOS isoforms

,

,

With these efforts, we determined structures of the iNOS:CaM complex to an overall ~3.0-Å resolution that capture the interdomain electron transfer state, revealing that the Red domain adopts a CaM-mediated, FMN subdomain-rotated, deshielded conformation that contacts Oxy and positions the exposed FMN cofactor directly over the heme of the adjacent protomer. Together, this work provides the long-sought structural basis for the interdomain electron-transfer-competent state of NOS enzymes, offering fundamental insights into the catalytic mechanisms conserved across the cytochrome P450 superfamily.

Crosslinking captures iNOS:CaM Red−Oxy interaction state for cryo-EM

The Red:CaM adopts an FMN subdomain deshielded output state to engage the Oxy dimer for electron transfer from FMN to heme

CaM, FMN and FNR interaction network supports the deshielded Red state
Upon inspection of the CaM interaction, we identify that the FMN subdomain rotation is supported through the previously characterized ‘hinge region’ (ref. 
27

). We additionally observe potential electrostatic contacts between the FMN subdomain residues D573, K574 and R576 and Oxy2 residues K155 and E156 at the tip of a helix–turn–helix (HTH) motif formed by helices H3 and H4 (Fig. 
4b(Ⅳ)
).
To assess the functional importance of these interactions, we performed mutational analysis and measured NOS activity (Fig. 
4c
). We note that, for all mutants, including double and triple mutants, the overall integrity of the iNOS:CaM complex remained comparable to that of the wild type, as evidenced by similar purity following tandem Ni-NTA and ADP resin purification and retention of bound CaM and heme (Extended Data Fig. 
9c,d
). Disrupting the interaction between Oxy2 and CaM (interaction I) by substituting both R83 and H84 of iNOS to alanine reduced the activity of the iNOS:CaM complex by ~25%, indicating this interaction, despite being distal from the cofactor sites, is important for iNOS activity, likely through positioning CaM on the Oxy dimer (Fig. 
4c
). Notably, neutralizing CaM residues D119A, E120A and D123A by alanine substitution, which appear to interact at the Oxy1–Oxy2 interface (interaction II) (Fig. 
4b
), nearly abolishes iNOS:CaM activity (Fig. 
4c
), indicating these residues are indeed critical for CaM-promoted NOS function. Consistent with previous studies of CaM activation
28
,
30
, these results reveal an interaction mechanism in which these charged residues contact a complementary site at the Oxy1–Oxy2 interface, likely stabilizing the Red arm over the Oxy dimer for FMN–heme electron transfer. Individual charge-reversal substitutions of the E120(CaM)–K497(iNOS) residue pair, previously untested, significantly reduced iNOS:CaM activity by approximately half (Fig. 
4c
). Moreover, combining the two substitutions (CaM(E120K) and Oxy1(K497E)) restored activity, establishing the functional importance of this predicted salt-bridge interaction. Interaction III was tested by FNR(Y812A), which reduced the activity by nearly half, supporting this site in stabilizing the FNR–Oxy1 interaction (Fig. 
4b,c
). Lastly, the HTH motif (interaction IV) was tested by substituting residue K155 in Oxy2, but neither K155D nor K155A substantially affected activity (Fig. 
4b,c
). Conversely, the adjacent E156R charge-reversal substitution reduced the activity of iNOS:CaM by ~25%, supporting that this residue may help coordinate the FMN subdomain. Overall, no single-site substitution abolished iNOS activity, consistent with multiple interactions collectively stabilizing the engaged state.
Conformational flexibility enables FAD–FMN and FMN–heme cofactor alignment
In our iNOS:CaM structure refined from a single well-resolved class, the Red domain remained at a lower resolution compared to the Oxy dimer, indicating flexibility between these regions (Extended Data Fig. 
3a
). Indeed, comparison of Red-containing classes shows differences in Red domain position relative to the Oxy dimer, with 8–9 Å shifts along the dimer surface, either lengthwise (class 1 versus class 4) or laterally (class 1 versus class 3) (Extended Data Fig. 
2e
 and Supplementary Fig. 
1

2e
 and Supplementary Video 
3

3b,c
 and 
10a,b
). While the Red domain remained at lower resolution compared to the Oxy dimer, these structures importantly reveal two distinct positions of the Red domain involving r.m.s.d. changes of up to ~12 Å for regions of the FNR and FMN subdomains and CaM that are adjacent to the Oxy dimer surface (Fig. 
5a,b
 and Extended Data Fig. 
10c
).

Discussion
Here, we determined structures of iNOS:CaM complex in an apparent cross-monomer electron-transfer-competent arrangement, revealing distinct states that illuminate how FAD, FMN and heme cofactors dynamically align during NO synthesis (Fig. 
6a
). We propose that flexible interactions between the Oxy dimer and Red:CaM arm enable the FMN cofactor to adopt multiple positions relative to FAD and heme for distinct electron transfer steps while minimizing solvent exposure. Strikingly, we identify a conformational state (proximal state) with an interdomain and interprotomer tunnel in which the FAD, FMN, heme and BH
4
 cofactors are aligned along with key residues (including W372, Y631 and P632) for efficient electron transfer across ~5-Å distances (Fig. 
6a
). In this state, the Red domain’s interaction with Oxy protects the FMN cofactor from solvent despite the large rotation away from FAD. Overall, these findings provide a structural basis for NO generation and establish general principles for electron transfer from the Red domain to heme in NOS enzymes.

From this work, we propose a functional model in which iNOS undergoes CaM-mediated, stepwise conformational changes that enable multiple cycles of electron transfer without dissociation of the Red–Oxy interface (Fig. 
6b
). CaM binding to the CaM-binding motif induces the FMN subdomain deshielding from FNR. This promotes CaM interaction with the Oxy dimer and FNR engagement with the Oxy domain on the same monomer (Oxy1), positioning the FMN subdomain atop the Oxy domain of the neighboring monomer (Oxy2) to form the engaged state. Interestingly, the Red remains flexible in the engaged state, enabling the FMN cofactor to shuttle between FAD-aligned (distal) and heme-aligned (proximal) states. Although it is widely believed that NOS must undergo repeated transitions between fully shielded and deshielded states for electrons to flow from FAD to FMN to heme, the discovery of distal and proximal states for iNOS:CaM suggests that these states may enable multiple electron transfers in a more rapid manner without large conformational changes. Such a mechanism seems advantageous for tightly CaM-bound iNOS
47
,
48
,
62
. It remains to be seen whether eNOS and nNOS have similar proximal and distal states as these isoforms are more dynamically regulated by CaM binding
47
,
63
.
In our iNOS:CaM structure, CaM binding to the CaM-binding motif of NOS induces deshielding of FMN subdomain from FNR, leading to anchoring of the entire Red
DS
, primarily through the Y812 residue at the tip of the β-hairpin finger (or CD2A) of the FNR (Fig. 
4a,b
). This β-hairpin finger motif may serve two roles: (1) as a motif that perturbs CaM interaction under low Ca
2+
 conditions, as previously suggested
64
, while conversely promoting deshielding of FMN subdomain under high Ca
2+
 conditions, and (2) as a structural element that enables interaction of FNR with Oxy and, thus, facilitates formation of the engaged state. Notably, the β-hairpin finger of nNOS and eNOS is longer than that of iNOS, potentially explaining their lower CaM-binding affinity and higher Ca
2+
 dependency
47
,
48
. Consistent with this, truncation of the hairpin was shown to reduce the Ca
2+
 dependence of CaM-bound activity of eNOS
64
. Interestingly, we identify a minor class of the monomeric iNOS:CaM complex that adopts a shielded state, indicating that this conformation is compatible with CaM binding (Extended Data Fig. 
8
). In this state, the FNR β-hairpin motif interacts with the CaM C-lobe. Thus, this conformation may restrict formation of the engaged state by limiting the availability of the CaM C-lobe and the β-hairpin motif for Oxy domain engagement. Whether this conformation has functional implications, given that it is derived from the inactive monomer, remains to be determined. In addition, nNOS and eNOS contain the CT and AL elements within the Red that differ from those of iNOS (Extended Data Fig. 
7a,f
). It remains unclear how these additional regulatory elements are accommodated in the Red
DS
 state and whether they confer functional roles.
Subsequent to our study, structures of murine iNOS:CaM were reported that identify both shielded and deshielded conformations (termed input and output, respectively)
65
. The deshielded (output) conformation closely matches the engaged state determined here, with the Red domain spanning the Oxy dimer and the FMN subdomain positioned adjacent to the heme of the opposing protomer. Conversely, the shielded (input) state structure resembles the shielded class we identified in the isolated monomeric fraction of iNOS:CaM, with only the Red:CaM portion determined, while density corresponding to the Oxy domain appears to not be present (Extended Data Fig. 
8d
). These observations suggest that the shielded-state classes in that study may likewise represent monomeric species, as indicated by the unresolved Oxy density.
The β-hairpin interaction observed in our studies, together with CaM’s interaction with the Oxy dimer and the hydrophobic FNR–FMN subdomain junction, positions the FMN subdomain atop Oxy2, establishing 
trans
 electron transfer across protomers. The FMN subdomain position might be further defined by the HTH motif, uniquely present in the Oxy domain of NOS within the P450 family. The reduced activity caused by the E156R substitution of the HTH may indicate that this motif is required to position the FMN subdomain on Oxy2. This is also supported by the discrete density of the HTH of Oxy2, observed only when it faces the FMN subdomain and not in Oxy1 in the engaged state, indicating a direct interaction, consistent with previous predictions and XL-MS results of other NOS proteins
30
,
43
 (Supplementary Fig. 
2
). In addition, XL-MS conducted here identified the K155–Y575 and K158–K607 crosslinks between the Oxy2 HTH motif and the FMN subdomain of human iNOS, corroborating this interpretation (Extended Data Fig. 
6
). Together, these extensive and coordinated contacts allow the FMN subdomain to remain flexible while maintaining structural integrity and may additionally support multiple rounds of electron transfer without full dissociation.
Several residue–residue interaction pairs change to mediate the conformational shift of Red relative to Oxy (Extended Data Fig. 
10e
). First, in the proximal state, a potential salt bridge between the HTH of Oxy2 (K155) and FMN subdomain (D573) is broken as FMN subdomain slides toward Oxy2, forming new salt bridges between nearby residues E156 and K574/R576 (Extended Data Fig. 
10e(I)
). Second, the proximal state is likely stabilized by hydrogen bonding between R452 of Oxy2 and a Ca
2+
-binding loop of CaM (Extended Data Fig. 
10e(II)
), consistent with previous molecular dynamics predictions
29
. Lastly, the loops adjacent to the FMN cofactor also move toward Oxy2, forming a potential hydrogen bond between E546 of FMN subdomain and N208 of Oxy2 and a salt bridge between E551 of FMN subdomain and K445 of Oxy2, which positions the FMN cofactor near the heme (Extended Data Fig. 
10e(III)
). Supporting these interaction interfaces, previous studies show that substituting E546 disrupts interactions between the FMN and Oxy domains
43
,
66
 and substituting K445 reduces NOS activity
33
.
M434, located adjacent to the critical, conserved W372 residue, adopts different occupancies in the iNOS:CaM complex and iNOS:CaM Oxy-only densities (Supplementary Fig. 
3
). Previous studies suggest a methionine adjacent to a tryptophan residue can facilitate electron transfer by modulating the reduction potential or serving as a ‘bridging’ motif over long distances
67
,
68
. Thus, M434 may regulate electron transfer and heme accessibility in accordance with Red engagement. In addition, reduced substrate arginine density near the heme was observed in the engaged iNOS:CaM complex, in contrast to the previous nNOS:CaM complex at similar density threshold (Supplementary Fig. 
4
). The reduced 
l
-arginine density may reflect decreased substrate occupancy and/or conformational heterogeneity associated with catalytic turnover. The iNOS:CaM Oxy-only densities showed similarly reduced 
l
-arginine intensity, suggesting that the Red domain may transition away from the engaged Red–Oxy configuration in association with catalytic turnover. Notably, BH
4
 in Oxy1 of the distal iNOS:CaM state exhibited continuous density with the nearby W463 residue, consistent with previous structural and functional studies describing a conserved 
π
-stacking interaction between BH
4
 and this tryptophan residue
69
,
70
 (Supplementary Fig. 
4b,c
). Surprisingly, the density also extends toward R199 and R381, suggesting that these residues may contribute to a structured BH
4
-binding environment through additional contacts. This local organization may be relevant to the redox role of BH
4
 during NOS catalysis, as BH
4
 serves as a transient one-electron donor during reductive activation of the oxyferrous heme intermediate and is subsequently regenerated
71
,
72
. The conserved interaction with W463 and surrounding residues may, therefore, help to position and stabilize BH
4
 for its catalytic electron-transfer function
73
,
74
.
Our work here reveals shared structural features that underlie electron transfer across cytochrome P450 reductase-dependent members of the P450 family
56
,
61
,
75
,
76
, including the FMN subdomain dissociation from FNR, orientation of the FMN cofactor C7 and C8 methyl groups toward the heme and adjacent residues that mediate electron transfer. However, the mechanisms of these redox partner interactions remain unclear. Although the Red domain of the iNOS:CaM complex in the engaged state aligns well with that of the mammalian NADPH–cytochrome P450 reductase open state (PDB 
3ES9
), the FMN cofactor is displaced by ~16 Å relative to the Oxy dimer, making it incompatible with electron transfer (Supplementary Fig. 
5a
). Moreover, the oxygenase domains of P450s differ substantially from those of NOS, requiring distinct electron transfer pathways
61
,
77
,
78
 (Supplementary Fig. 
5b
). How P450 enzymes recognize P450 reductase and promote FMN subdomain opening remains unknown, whereas CaM mediates both processes in NOS. Collectively, the results presented here reveal the architecture and dynamics of the iNOS:CaM complex, uncovering a previously unrecognized mechanism of interprotomer electron transfer underlying NO generation.

Cryo-EM data processing and structure modeling
A total of 35,896 videos from untilted (22%), 30° tilted (7%), OG (7%), DDM (51%) and amphipol (13%) datasets were collected for the iNOS:CaM complex on a Titan Krios. Each dataset was processed independently in cryoSPARC

Contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation was performed with patch CTF and micrographs with thick or broken ice, severe contamination or poor CTF fits were excluded. Approximately 100 particles were manually picked to generate 2D templates for automated particle picking. Reference-free 2D classification and ab initio reconstruction revealed two distinct states: the engaged iNOS:CaM complex and the Oxy-only species.
These reconstructions, along with three junk references, were used for heterogeneous refinement without prior 2D classification to minimize selection bias. Particles corresponding to the engaged and Oxy-only states were refined separately. For the engaged complex, iterative heterogeneous refinements and nonuniform refinement were followed by 3D classification into ten classes. Well-resolved classes were reclassified into six classes and the best subset (engaged, consensus) yielded a 2.96-Å map from 56,656 particles. To probe Red domain movements, all particles from the second 3D classification were subjected to 3DVA with three clusters, producing 2.97-Å (40,215 particles, proximal) and 3.02-Å (41,786 particles, distal) maps.
For the Oxy-only state, combined particles underwent three rounds of heterogeneous refinement using the initial Oxy-only and engaged-state references with three junk classes. After nonuniform refinement, ~2 million particles were divided into five subsets for ab initio reconstruction. The subset displaying discrete Oxy-only density was combined and refined through additional heterogeneous, followed by nonuniform and local refinement using an Oxy mask, yielding a 2.58-Å map from 143,117 particles.

The broad band observed at the dimeric molecular mass is consistent with intersubunit crosslinked protein, as those without covalently crosslinked subunits would dissociate into monomers under the denaturing conditions of the gel.
In-gel digestion
The excised gel pieces were destained by washing with 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate in 50% acetonitrile. Cysteine residues were reduced by incubating the gel pieces with 10 mM DTT at 56 °C for 1 h. Alkylation was then conducted by treating the gel pieces with 54 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After thorough washing with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 8.5), the gel pieces were dehydrated with acetonitrile. Excess solvent was removed and the samples were dried using a Speedvac. Digestion was initiated by adding MS-grade trypsin–LysC solution (20 ng µl
−1
; Promega) with enough volume to cover the gel pieces and incubating overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were extracted from the gel with 50% acetonitrile containing 5% formic acid, dried and then reconstituted in 0.1–0.5% formic acid (pH 2–4). The peptide solutions were transferred to low-binding vials for LC–MS/MS analysis.

DDA-PASEF data analysis
Analysis of the DDA-PASEF data was performed following the procedure in the literature
93
. The raw data files were first processed using Compass DataAnalysis software (version 6.1; Bruker Daltonics) with a customized method script. The mgf output files were analyzed by MeroX (version 2.0)
91
 to search for peptide pairs crosslinked by DSBU. For the search, lysine, serine, threonine or tyrosine and the N termini were considered as crosslinking sites and only the pairs that contained the MS-cleavable fragments of DSBU modifications (+85.05276381 Da and +111.03202835 Da) at both sites in the MS/MS were considered. The precursor mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm and fragment mass tolerance was set to 15 ppm. The precursor mass range was set to 300–8,000 Da. The cutoff score was first set to a highly tolerable value of 15. The generated zhrm files were combined in MeroX (version 2.0)
91

As FDR for single-protein analysis provides rather limited accuracy because of a small search space, crosslinked peptide hits were further examined and filtered in Skyline
94

K

To build a peptide spectral library in Skyline, a proXL xml file was generated for each run using the ‘merox2ProxlXML.jar’ Java executable file and all resulting proXL xml files were imported into Skyline. After importing all raw files, the library peptides were examined and those with low-intensity noisy MS/MS spectra were removed. An isolation list was then exported from Skyline and imported to the timsControl (version 6.0; Bruker Daltonics) data acquisition software for PRM-PASEF acquisition. All information regarding the above-mentioned customized scripts and Java executable file can be found in the literature

PRM-PASEF data analysis
Targeted MS/MS analysis by PRM was performed to validate the identified crosslinked peptides from the DDA experiment. This step is essential because of the typically low abundance of crosslinked peptides and rather limited reproducibility of XL-MS, particularly with DDA-based XL-MS
43

1

The high-quality PRM data obtained for these crosslinks demonstrate that the PRM acquisition parameters and LC conditions were well optimized for accurate detection and validation of the targeted crosslinked peptides. Only these PRM-validated crosslinks were used in the subsequent structural dynamics analyses.
Lysine, serine, threonine or tyrosine and protein N termini were included as potential crosslinking sites in the DDA search. Crosslink site probabilities calculated by MeroX (version 2.0)

Statistics and reproducibility
No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Biochemical experiments were performed in technical duplicate or triplicate, in accordance with standard practice in the field, and independent replication yielded consistent results. Cryo-EM micrographs were excluded on the basis of predefined quality criteria, including outlier average defocus values, low maximum CTF fit resolution, suboptimal ice quality or excessive astigmatism. Particle images were excluded during 2D and 3D classification when assigned to low-resolution or noninterpretable classes. Biochemical and XL-MS experiments were not randomized, as randomization was not applicable to the experimental design. Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Statistical analyses and replicate numbers are described in the corresponding figure legends (Table

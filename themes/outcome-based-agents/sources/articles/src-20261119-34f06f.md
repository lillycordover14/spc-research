      Financial review

      Unless the context indicates otherwise, all comparatives relate to the six months ended 30 September 2025 (1H2026). Salient operational features for the period under review include:
    

    
      Total operating income increased by 4.0% to R10 912 million (1H2026: R10 497 million). The components of operating income are analysed further below:
    

        
          Net interest income increased 5.4% to R8 365 million (1H2026: R7 937 million) driven by an 8.4% growth in average net core loans period on period and a lower cost of funds. This was partly offset by the negative endowment effects of lower average interest rates and competitive pricing
        

        
          Net core loans grew by 8.2% annualised to R413.3 billion (FY2026: R397.3 billion) supported by 7.9% annualised growth in the private banking lending book, and strong growth across our corporate credit portfolios particularly Leveraged Finance and Fund Finance. The Corporate mid-market lending book grew by 20.8% annualised, reflecting continued investment in this strategic growth segment
        

        
          Net fee and commission income increased by 12.7% to R1 762 million (1H2026: R1 564 million), primarily driven by higher private banking fees, supported by increased client activity and strong client growth, together with higher FX structuring fees in the corporate and investment banking business
        

        
          Investment income decreased, primarily reflecting lower realised and unrealised fair value gains compared with the prior period
        

        
          Trading income from customer flow amounted to R479 million (1H2026: R275 million), supported by stronger client activity in equity derivatives as well as institutional flows in interest rate and commodity trading desks
        

        
          Trading income from balance sheet management activities decreased to R61 million (1H2026: R139 million), reflecting unrealised MTM losses associated with the management of interest rate risk, compared with MTM gains in the prior period. Recognition of these MTM movements is temporary and reverses over the life of the financial instruments.
        

    
      ECL impairment charges decreased to R150 million (1H2026: R237 million), primarily reflecting higher recoveries relative to the prior period and an IFRS 9 model release in the current period, compared with a model charge in the prior period. The credit loss ratio improved to 7bps (1H2026: 12bps).
    

    
      The cost to income ratio was 48.2% (1H2026: 47.7%). Operating costs increased 5.1% to R5 263 million (1H2026: R5 009 million), reflecting our continued investment to support strategic growth, including additional headcount and annual salary increases.
    

    
      Technology investment increased, particularly in the modernisation of transactional banking and the roll-out of new features to expand existing capabilities.
    

    
      As a result, profit before tax grew by 4.7% to R5 499 million (1H2026: R5 251 million) while profit after taxation increased 4.8% to R4 423 million (1H2026: R4 222 million).

Key judgements

      Key judgement areas under IFRS 9 are subject to robust governance processes. As at 30 September 2026, the composition and weightings of the forward-looking macroeconomic scenarios have been revised to reflect the current macro-economic environment. In addition, there is reliance on expert credit judgements to ensure that the overall level of ECL is reasonable particularly in relation to specific impairments raised against Stage 3 exposures. There are currently no management overlays. Stage 3 ECLs continue to be assessed using a combination of scenario analysis, informed by expert judgement, and modelled ECL.
    
Forward-looking macro-economic scenarios

      The measurement of ECL requires the use of multiple economic scenarios to calculate a probability weighted forward-looking estimate. These scenarios are updated at least twice a year, or more frequently if there is a macroeconomic shock or significant shift in expectations. The weighting of these scenarios for IFRS 9 as well as the scenarios themselves are discussed and presented at the relevant BRCCs as well as the relevant capital committees for approval, which form part of the principal governance framework for macro-economic scenarios. They are also approved by the relevant Audit Committees.
    

    
      A number of forecast economic scenarios are considered for capital planning, stress testing (including Investec-specific stress scenarios) and IFRS 9 ECL measurement.
    

    
      For the Bank, five macro-economic scenarios incorporate a base case, two upside cases and two downside cases. The aim of this economic scenario generation process was to provide a view of the current and projected state of the South African economy and the different economic scenarios that could occur in various stressed or improved environments over the next five years for a number of identified variables/ risk drivers.
    

    
      As at 30 September 2026, all five scenarios were updated to incorporate the latest available data. The base case is characterised by modest economic growth that lifts to 3.0% over the five-year period. That is, economic growth is limited initially until structural (including infrastructure such as freight transport) constraints are overcome. Some fiscal consolidation leads to upgrades from the three main credit rating agencies towards BB+ or the equivalent and the Rand stabilises then strengthens. Inflation moves to, then becomes entrenched at 3.0% y/y over the five-year period. A modest transition to renewable energy occurs and some meaningful measures to alleviate the impact of climate change on the economy are implemented. Global financial market risk sentiment is neutral to positive and geopolitical tensions do not exacerbate markedly on a persistent basis. Extremely limited expropriation without compensation occurs with no negative effect on the economy or sentiment.
    

    
      The weighting of the base case increased to 59% at
    

    
      30 September 2026 from 55% at 31 March 2026, as there is an improved trajectory for economic growth and government finances.
    

    
      The lite down case has the same international environment (including global financial market risk sentiment) of the base case, and only the domestic environment differs. South Africa's GDP growth is weak, business confidence becomes more deeply depressed, with weak investment growth, weak rail and port capacity, water and other services loadshedding, civil and political unrest, and a modest recession. Substantial Rand weakness drives higher inflation, with unfavourable weather
    

    
      conditions. Little transition to renewable energy is apparent. There is increased pressure on government finances from disaster relief on unfavourable weather conditions from climate change. Government debt and debt projections fail to stabilise. South Africa drops into the single B credit ratings from the three key rating agencies for local and foreign currency sovereign debt but avoids C grades on fiscal consolidation.
    

    
      Expropriation without compensation is limited and has a modestly negative impact on the economy.
    

    
      As at 31 March 2026, the weighting of the lite down case was 28% and decreased to 24% at 30 September 2026, as government finances and credit ratings improved.
    

    
      Under the current very low probability severe down case, credit ratings fall into C grades, with increased risk of default. A lengthy global and domestic recession and/or financial crisis occurs and geopolitical tensions worsen, with insufficient monetary and other policy support. Substantial Rand weakness occurs on severe global market risk-off sentiment. There is a failure to transition to renewable energy and implement sufficient measures to alleviate the impact of climate change on the economy. Very high domestic inflation and other deteriorated economic indicators affected by unfavourable weather conditions occur. Implementation of expropriation without compensation takes place (particularly land held for speculative purposes), with significant negative impact on the economy, along with loadshedding of both electricity and water services, high incidences of strike action and civil unrest.
    

    
      The scenario weighting of the severe down case remained at 1% at 30 September 2026, unchanged from 31 March 2026.
    

    
      In the up case structural constraints to sustained, robust economic growth are removed in an environment of strong global and domestic growth, with rising business confidence and investment levels. The global financial market environment is risk-on. Low domestic inflation on Rand strength and favourable weather conditions sees moderate inflation. A significant transition to renewable energy away from fossil fuel usage occurs on comprehensive measures to alleviate the impact of climate change on the economy. No nationalisation or expropriation without compensation occurs. Credit rating upgrades occur on strong fiscal consolidation. Geopolitical tensions ease.
    

    
      The scenario weighting remained at 15% at 30 September 2026, unchanged from 31 March 2026.
    

    
      The extreme up case is an acceleration of the up case as good governance and growth-creating reforms overcome structural constraints rapidly. Business confidence is high, property rights immutable, fixed investment growth rates very strong, and substantial foreign direct investment (FDI) inflows occur. In addition, strong fiscal consolidation sees government debt fall back to the low ratios of the early 2000s. Domestic economic growth of 3%-5%, then 5%-7%, is achieved, and investment grade credit ratings are attained on substantial fiscal consolidation. There is very subdued domestic inflation on extreme Rand strength and favourable weather conditions, with a rapid transition away from fossil fuels, to renewable energy. Robust global growth and a commodity boom occur internationally, with low geopolitical disturbances and a low level of risk aversion in global financial markets.
    

    
      This scenario weighting remained 1%.

Liquidity risk

      Liquidity risk is the risk that, despite being solvent, the Bank is unable to meet payment obligations as they fall due, in normal and stressed conditions, or to fund increases in assets, as a result of insufficient capacity or the inability to access liquidity. This includes repaying depositors or maturing wholesale debt. This risk arises from mismatches in the timing of cash flows and is inherent in all banking operations, and can be impacted by a range of institution-specific and market-wide events.
    

    
      Liquidity risk is further broken down into:
    

        
          Funding liquidity risk: 
this relates to the risk that the Bank will be unable to meet current and/or future cash flows or collateral requirements in the normal course of business, without adversely affecting its solvency, financial position or its reputation

          Market liquidity risk: 
this relates to the risk that the Bank may be unable to trade in specific markets or that it may only be able to do so with difficulty due to market disruptions or a lack of market liquidity.

Interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB)

          IRRBB arises from the impact of adverse movements in interest rates on both net interest earnings and economic value of equity. IRRBB is an inherent consequence of conducting banking activities, and arises from the provision of retail and wholesale (non-trading) banking products and services.
        

        
          Sources of IRRBB include:
        

        
          Repricing risk: 
arises from the timing differences in the fixed rate maturity and floating rate repricing of Bank assets, liabilities and derivative positions. This affects the interest rate margin realised between lending income and borrowing costs when applied to our rate sensitive portfolios

          Yield curve risk: 
repricing mismatches also expose the Bank to changes in the slope and shape of the yield curve

          Basis risk: 
arises from imperfect correlation in the adjustments of the rates earned and paid on different instruments with otherwise similar repricing characteristics

          Embedded option risk: 
arises from optional elements embedded in items where the Bank or its customers can alter the level and timing of their cash flows, such as the prepayment of fixed rate loans and withdrawal of non-maturity deposits (NMDs)

          Endowment risk: 
refers to the interest rate risk exposure arising from the net differential between interest rate insensitive assets, interest rate insensitive liabilities and capital.

      The above sources of interest rate risk affect the interest rate margin realised between lending income and borrowing costs when applied to our rate sensitive asset and liability portfolios, which has a direct effect on future net interest earnings and the economic value of equity.

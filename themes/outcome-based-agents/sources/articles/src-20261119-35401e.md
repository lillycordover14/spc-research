15,000-Year-Old Clay Figurines Carry The Fingerprints of Children And Adults, Suggesting a Shared Craft

Humans
20 November 2026
By 
Ivan Farkas
 

 

(Fadil Aziz /Alcibbum Photograph/Getty Images)
 

The tortured (or at least hungry) 
itinerant
 
artist
 is a common trope, but in some parts of the world, we humans as a whole only really kicked up our art-making abilities once we became settled. 

And unlike today's build-your-own-Bluey craft kits and the like, many ancient artistic endeavors seem to have been reserved for adults – with few but nonetheless fascinating examples of 
children's creations
 in 
deep history
. 

Or so it generally seems, but now researchers have presented a twofer: a society that developed a clay-based figurative art form, which they directly shared with their children.

In a study published in the journal 
PLOS One
,  archaeologists and ethnologists have shifted back the "revolution of symbols," exploring the evolution of symbolic depictions made by the Natufian culture, the first settled peoples of the Levant, circa 15,000 years ago, predating their advent of agriculture by millennia. 
Previously analyzed Natufian artifacts that were not the primary focus of this work. (Davin et al., 
PLOS One
, 2026)
"These discoveries change how we understand the beginnings of artistic expression in this region," 
explains
 Laurent Davin, a prehistory archaeologist at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and the study's first author. 

"Thousands of years before agriculture emerged, people were already using clay to create figures, as part of their beliefs and knowledge of the natural world."

It also appears to have been a generational bonding experience: 

"What is particularly fascinating is the evidence that children were involved in making these objects, suggesting that creativity and cultural knowledge were shared across generations," Davin 
adds
. 

The researchers identified 154  fragmented figurative clay artifacts dating from 15,000 to 11,650 years ago, unearthed from three sites in the Southern Levant, including Mount Carmel, the Upper Jordan Valley, and Lower Galilee. 
Natufian sites, including those included in this work, highlighted in red. (Davin et al., 
PLoS One
, 2026)
Their identification involved sorting through tens of thousands of small lumps of heated clay and sediment, many smaller than a sugar cube, totaling around 40 kilograms (nearly 90 pounds), previously excavated between the 1950s and 2020s from terraces and open-air sites. 

Notably, these artifacts include 15 recognizable depictions of humans, gazelles, and birds, as well as 139 less-recognizable fragments. 
A table describing the finds analyzed in this work. (Davin et al., 
PLoS One
, 2026) 
Therefore, this work overturns a couple of long-standing paradigms. First, that the Natufian peoples lacked such an artistic tradition. Secondly, that this tradition emerged alongside an agricultural economy. 

The artifacts contain unintentional inclusions, such as freshwater shells, bone splinters, flint flakes, and charcoal. So the ancient artists weren't scooping the plentiful sediment from the floors of their habitat, but traveling beyond their settlements to collect material for their burgeoning art form. 

"This pattern of acquisition, shared by all three communities, suggests that the Natufians sought out clays with particular plastic qualities, grain size and possibly also colors to suit this new mode of expression," the researchers 
say
. 

Of the 15 figurines, the majority (11) depict birds, including those that were hunted, such as 
ducks
 and other waterfowl, as well as falcon-like flyers. 
(Davin et al., 
PLoS One
, 2026)
Some figurines are simple while others are more anatomically detailed, featuring feathers and seemingly posed in courtship displays, such as an end-of-winter mating practice in which male ducks spray females with water – scenes that play out poolside among humans today. 

Other figures, such as those of humans, seem to have been ornamented with perishable materials, like headdresses made of grass, string, hair, or feathers. 

Signs of wear and tear suggest these figurines had short lives. They were not handled daily but perhaps only on special occasions, such as in ceremonies or during specific seasons (in line with the ducks' courtship displays), and were not passed down through generations like heirlooms. 

The researchers also found fingerprints on 2 figurines, 5 cylinders, and 50 other artifacts, forming the largest such assemblage yet known from the pre-Neolithic, and revealing the caring touch of hands that have long since returned to dust.

The diversity and morphology of fingerprints suggest that, in some communities at least, all members of the society interacted with these clay depictions. 

Importantly, one figurine holds the fingerprints of an adult and a child, perhaps showing a conveyance of generational knowledge, or antediluvian apprenticeship.
The stylized figurine of a human body, found in the Upper Jordan Valley, featuring the fingerprints of an adult and a child. (
Laurent Davin
)
The young Natufian craftspeople seem to vary by socio-cultural factors across sites and times, but the researchers say it seems clear that children were not merely handling these objects; their fingerprints show that they were shaping and coloring them. 

Overall, this work reinforces that artistic enterprises take their own timeline, are inspired by our experiences, and best shared with loved ones. 
"This figurative shift in zoomorphic expression reflects developments through deep observation and accumulation of practical knowledge, possibly stored and transmitted in ritualized performance," the researchers 
conclude
. 

The research has been published in 
PLoS One
.

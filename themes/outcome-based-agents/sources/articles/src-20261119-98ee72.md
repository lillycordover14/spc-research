Not every part belongs or fits on a workbench. Fighter jet wings, vehicle frames, submarine or ship hulls, and aircraft flight controls are just some examples of thousands of parts that need to be built, repaired, overhauled, inspected or otherwise maintained.
A large share of the work facing defense OEMs, depots, and MRO shops involve exactly these kinds of parts: large, heavy, geometrically complex, and produced in quantities that range from single units to a few dozen a year or more. In the past, automating much of that work hasn’t always been possible or economically feasible.
But technology is changing, and the change says as much about how automation is engineered as it does about the robots themselves. Three capabilities, layered together, are turning defense production and sustainment work that used to require days of manual labor into tasks measured in hours:
Linear motion systems extend the work envelope so robots can physically reach a large part wherever it sits.
Robot systems are becoming the processing tools that can scan and understand a part without being programmed for it in advance.
Interfaces simple enough that the operator running the cell doesn't need to be a robotics specialist are available now.
Combining these capabilities offers the ability and flexibility to work on today’s mission as well as the next.
The Reach Problem

Titan Robotics high accuracy positioner with expanded work volume using Güdel TMF-3.

(Provided by Gudel and Titan Robotics)

Industrial robotics grew up solving a narrower problem than the one defense manufacturing and maintenance presents today. A robot arm mounted to a fixed base has a fixed envelope: it can reach what its geometry allows and nothing more. That worked for small parts that came to the robot or parts flowing down an assembly line. It required parts to be indexed and positioned for that treatment and application.
That becomes less feasible when the part is a twelve-foot weldment or an aircraft fuselage section or the hull assembly of a main battle tank that’s too large and heavy or too inconsistent to move into hard fixturing near a stationary robot arm.
Linear motion technology stops asking the part to come to the robot and instead allows the workpiece to be larger and the robot arm to reach the workpiece. Mounting a robot arm on a linear track—running along the floor, up a vertical column, or suspended overhead—turns a small work envelope into a much larger one. With integrated linear axes, the reach of the robot is a function of the track lengths used, not the arm alone. And multiple axes of motion can be stacked so that the robots can cover complex structures of almost any size.
Solutions for expanding robot reach applications such as those offered by Güdel Inc., a Swiss manufacturer of heavy industrial linear motion equipment with manufacturing located in the U.S., and others, enable that sort of flexibility and functionality.
“We are increasingly approached by customers with requests for equipment capable of handling ever larger, ultra-heavy parts. The robots are being capable of handling more and thus payload demands on the equipment needs to be able to adapt,” says Güdel Managing Director Martin Kaezig. “And yet, the tolerance requirements for accuracy and repeatability remain.”
These heavy-duty tracks and gantry systems provide the expanded work envelopes needed that are beyond the traditional stationary robot.
The shift—treating the robot as a tool with a linear motion system that increases the reach of the robot arm—is what makes large-part automation economically viable in a defense context. Not only can the parts be large and heavy, but also of an extremely high-mix, low-volume nature. It also changes the calculus on which jobs are worth automating in the first place. A simple robot arm on a fixed base cell must justify its cost against a small part or require many fixturing setups; a large-scale cell with linear motion external axes can be justified against an entire new category of work: airframes, ground vehicles, weldments, et cetera. Now the same track and arm combination can be used across a mix of parts variations.
Scanning the Part in Place

Titan Robotics laser coating removal with an expanded work volume using Güdel TMF-4.

(Provided by Gudel and Titan Robotics)

Reach solves where the robot and therefore the tool can go. It does not solve what the robot does once it gets there—and this is where the second capability matters most. Traditional robotic programming assumes a known, repeatable part in a known repeatable pose: a programmer writes a unique toolpath in a simulation environment using a CAD model to be used thousands of times on identical parts positioned the same each time. That assumption does not hold in the context of modern aerospace and defense applications where components often carry decades of accumulated wear, prior repairs, and numerous redesigns—or are simply too large to reliably and repeatably be placed in the same relative position. Even newly manufactured defense hardware is often built in such small batches that writing a dedicated robot program for each part would cost more than the labor it replaces.
Technology comes to the rescue. The ability to scan the actual physical part in place and build its own reference model on the spot, rather than relying on an idealized digital twin prepared in advance, offers versatility and adaptability required to meet today’s challenge. Vision sensors generate data of the real surface to plan for force feedback, and motion sensing add a second layer of information about contact and material response for contact processes. Together, they allow the robot to work on the actual part and its position in space and adapt its path in real time.
Though work frame offsets have existed for some time, this relatively new approach is emerging. Tool paths are entirely generated automatically and processed. This means they can account for the limitations of the hardware, including handling singularities that would otherwise result in tool path failures in a traditional work frame offset approach. This is because robot motions are generated by the control system at a higher level where they are created to achieve a goal instead of a prescribed trajectory.
The goals of the process are encoded with a “digital workpiece” so once the robot localizes a defined workpiece, the robot trajectories can be generated relative to it to achieve the desired relative motions for the tooling set. A byproduct this process provides is the creation of data to contribute to the parts’ “digital thread.” Data that is a focus for the defense industry—data that can be used to inform future needs around any serialized part. Being able to trace what a robot measured, adjusted for, and executed upon provides value beyond throughput alone and lines up with where defense production and sustainment programs are already investing.
One-Off Parts?
Scanning solves recognition of where the part is and how robot motions need to change. The third piece involves the economics and logistics of the high-mix, low-volume and large-part production process. Manually programming robot trajectories with or without an offline tool becomes increasingly challenging and time consuming when a part being targeted for automation is large and has complex geometry and when precision motion is required by the process being automated. Because of this scale and complexity challenge, high-mix, low-volume work has historically made automation not viable because the programming cost per part usually exceeds the cost of the labor it replaces.
Enter integrated systems such as those developed by Titan Robotics. These systems require configuration more than programming. A library of pre-built process task templates, sometimes described as “tasks,” organized around finishing operations, material types or geometric features are provided with an intuitive interface. A supervisor or manufacturing engineer can select, configure, and sequence these task templates for the specific operation on a specific part, without an engineer writing new code for every job. Task templates range from part localization, inspection, and traditional manufacturing tasks such as metal grinding and random orbital sanding.
The shortage of skilled engineers and in-house robotics programmers is a serious constraint, one that is ill-afforded in crucial defense applications that could be automated. But a system designed to select and recombine various existing process templates is easily implemented across new part families or finishing requirements without a re-engineering cycle each time. This allows the capital investment to survive changes in mission scope—only the template library needs to be used in new ways or potentially needs to grow. Our modern defense industrial base cannot predict which platform, part mix or threat environment it will be supporting. Systems such as these allow a certain amount of futureproofing of the infrastructure investment.
Interface Operators Already Know

Titan Robotics’ intuitive HELM task-based interface, available for many production processes including coating removal, painting, drilling, defastening, inspections, and more, shown on a SPARC metal finishing system with expanded work volume using a Güdel TMF-3/TMV-3.

(Provided by Gudel and Titan Robotics)

None of the above matters if running the cell still requires a credentialed robotics programmer, or an experienced automation system operator, because that is precisely the skillset the defense manufacturing workforce does not have enough of. Multiple industry workforce studies point to the same shortage: skilled trades and technical operators are retiring faster than they are being replaced, and specialized robotics programming talent is among the hardest and most expensive skill sets to backfill.
For example, often it’s welders who wind up doing most of the metal grinding in a fabrication environment, which further strains the supply of skilled welders. One hundred percent of grinding is unwanted work. Aside from diverting talent, the process involves harmful dust, hazardous materials and hand-arm vibration that is detrimental to the skilled tradespeople and their goals. The need to provide for the safety of our workers cannot be overstated. It seems like metal grinding would be a great target for automation, but traditional automation has not filled that need.
This need has led to companies like Titan Robotics developing advanced automation solutions that encode the complexity of the human operators’ hands into a robotic system which users can learn quickly versus the months to years of training and experience to achieve proficiency building and programming traditional robotic solutions.
The enabling technology in this supposed advanced automation is an underlying controller that interprets simple tasks that a user works with and translates them into the motion and process steps that the hardware executes to achieve the users’ goals. All process verification and validation must occur automatically to enable user friendly feedback—versus a stream of programming syntax and faults requiring an expert to interpret—as would be normal in a traditional automation approach.
Sparc is the Titan Robotics metal grinding and finishing product family that addresses the challenges manufacturers are facing in automating metal grinding. An easy-to-use no-programming approach to metal grinding. “You can think of Sparc as the tool, an integrated system that provides turnkey sanding, grinding and finishing operations through Titan’s HELM user interface,” says CJ Brey, commercial leader at Titan Robotics. “Simple task-based interactions replace traditional teach pendants; task selection and configuration replace path programming.”
The interface a manufacturing engineer uses to tell a system what to do, and the interface an operator uses to make the system execute a process, are both equally important in a high-mix environment. It is critical for an automation system to provide an interaction mechanism where the manufacturing engineer can provide the system basic tasking and instruction in industry relevant terms to enable adoption in today’s manufacturing environments.
Why It Matters
Each of these three capabilities; expanded reach, a tool with in-place scanning, and configurable task-based operation—solves a distinct engineering problem. Combined, they enable a system larger and more flexible than any single traditional automation cell. A system built to reach whatever part shows up, recognize it without being told in advance what it is, and run it through a reconfigurable set of process templates is, by construction, a system that does not need to know in advance what its next job will be.
That is a vastly different approach with vastly different returns than automation in the high-volume commercial manufacturing sector. It reduces capital expenditure while increasing the types of processes, parts and materials critical to the mission. Defense production and sustainment run on unique economics: short runs, unpredictable part mixes, aging fleets alongside emerging platforms, and a workforce that cannot be expected to reprogram a robot cell every time the job changes. Systems now have engineering capabilities designed around reach, adaptive planning and execution, and configurable operation which are infinitely more useful and economical in today’s environment.
The strategic implications reach beyond any single depot or program office. As the Defense Department pushes onshoring, surge capacity and industrial-base resilience, the automation infrastructure best positioned to support that push will be the kind that can be redirected rather than replaced. Hardware and software configured for flexibility and affordability provide a strategic advantage. It also helps leverage the existing workforce much in the way it enables the engineering challenge. A reconfigurable system amortizes its training investment the same way it amortizes its capital cost, because the operators who learned it on one part family do not need to be retrained from scratch when the part family changes.
The true test of "next mission" readiness is not whether a system can do today's job competently, affordably and efficiently. It is whether the same investment—the track, the sensing stack, the task library, the interface an operator already knows how to run—is still earning its keep the day the mission changes.

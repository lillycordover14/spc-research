U.S. District Judge Stacey Neumann, who has ruled against U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement in earlier Maine detention cases, has ordered the federal government to explain why Border Patrol agents arrested an 18-year-old Ecuadorian without a warrant during a traffic stop in Livermore Falls.

The teenager was arrested Monday, Nov. 16, after a Border Patrol agent stopped a van carrying eight people. Agents arrested six of them without warrants.

According to court records reviewed by the Portland Press Herald, the agent stopped the van because it had Massachusetts license plates, a ladder rack, and no commercial markings. The agent said similar vehicles have been used to bring people without legal immigration status to job sites in Maine.

Agents asked the occupants about their citizenship before making the arrests.

Some of those arrested were taken to a Border Patrol station in Rangeley. The teenager was taken to ICE’s facility in Scarborough, where federal authorities served him with an administrative immigration warrant that was prepared after the roadside arrest.

Immigration authorities, not a judge, issue an administrative immigration warrant. That difference is at the center of the legal dispute.

Neumann ordered immigration authorities to keep the teenager in Maine while the court reviews the arrest. Federal officials say he was already being taken to a detention facility in Rhode Island when agents learned of the order. As of Wednesday, Nov. 18, the Press Herald said it could not confirm where he was being held.

His attorney, Talia Rothstein, filed a habeas corpus petition challenging his detention and questioning whether Border Patrol agents had the authority to arrest him without a warrant.

Federal immigration law allows warrantless arrests in some cases, such as when an officer believes a person is unlawfully in the country and likely to escape before a warrant can be obtained. The government says the teenager is now in removal proceedings and that his continued detention is lawful.

The teenager’s immigration history is also at issue. His petition says he entered the United States at age 16 and later received Special Immigrant Juvenile classification after a Massachusetts court found he had been abused and neglected by his father. The federal classification can give qualifying young immigrants a path to lawful permanent residency.

The petition says the teenager’s brother told agents about the classification during the stop, and that an agent said the teenager did not appear to have an active removal case at that time.

Whether that classification limits the government’s authority to detain him remains one of the case’s main questions.

Neumann has ruled against federal immigration authorities in earlier Maine cases involving arrests, detention and the 
transfer of detainees out of state
.

Earlier this year, she ruled in the case of Tong Qi Lu, a Chinese immigrant who had lived in the United States for decades and was detained in Bangor after previously being released under federal immigration supervision.

In January, Neumann ordered immigration authorities not to transfer Lu out of Maine while his challenge was pending. Authorities transferred him to Massachusetts anyway. Neumann then ordered his immediate release, but federal authorities did not release him until the next day.

In a Feb. 9 ruling, Neumann found that Lu’s renewed detention was unlawful because the government had not shown the circumstances needed to justify taking him back into custody. She ordered him released under his previous supervision conditions and barred federal officials from detaining him again unless circumstances changed. The ruling did not halt his removal proceedings.

In June, Neumann ruled against ICE in the case of Eduardo Alves, a Lewiston man arrested by immigration agents at his home. Court filings say Alves was detained June 20 and moved to a Massachusetts jail the next day, even though a court order said he was not to be removed from Maine. Neumann ruled his detention unlawful and ordered his release.

Unlike those cases, the Livermore Falls case centers on the reason for the traffic stop and the warrantless arrests that followed.

The case comes as 
demonstrations continue
 outside ICE’s Scarborough facility, which has become the main site of immigration protests in Maine. Earlier this month, federal authorities increased security there after months of demonstrations.

The court has not yet ruled on whether the teenager’s arrest or continued detention was lawful. For now, the federal government must explain the roadside stop and the legal authority it used to take the 18-year-old into custody.

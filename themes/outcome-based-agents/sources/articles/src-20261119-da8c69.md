Abstract
Reliable optimization of complex numerical and constrained problems requires algorithms that can exploit accumulated search experience without sacrificing population diversity. This paper proposes MAO-APO-RC, an enhanced Artificial Protozoa Optimizer that couples memory-augmented learning with adaptive competitive control. The method combines historical-solution guidance, quasi-oppositional sampling, Lévy-flight exploration, stagnation-triggered rescue, success-history parameter adaptation, and feedback-based operator scoring within a unified multi-candidate search framework. At every iteration, alternative candidates are produced by complementary operators, evaluated under a common selection rule, and reinforced according to their recent contribution, enabling dynamic regulation of exploration and exploitation. The performance was assessed in 30 independent runs on 15 benchmark functions including shifted, rotated, ill-conditioned, multimodal, hybrid and composition landscapes in 30 and 50 dimensions, and four constrained engineering design problems. The comparisons were made to APO, PSO, DE, GWO, WOA, SCA, CMAES-lite, and SHADE-lite. MAO-APO-RC achieved the best overall Friedman mean rank of 2.3667 on the numerical benchmark suite. Six of the eight competing algorithms were statistically significantly different with Wilcoxon–Holm analysis, whereas APO and SHADE-lite were statistically comparable. For the engineering problems, MAO-APO-RC obtained a joint-best mean rank of 1.875 with DE. Ablation analysis revealed the APO + QOD as the best reduced configuration with a mean rank of 1.8500 compared to 1.9167 for MAO-APO-RC whereas the complete framework significantly outperformed the Memory and Energy variants. Despite measurable constant-factor runtime overhead, MAO-APO-RC preserves APO’s asymptotic time and space complexity. These findings demonstrate that MAO-APO-RC provides competitive aggregate performance across the evaluated numerical and engineering problems, while its statistical advantage remains competitor- and problem-dependent.

Introduction
Optimization is a fundamental problem in science, engineering, machine learning, energy systems, manufacturing, signal processing, scheduling, and structural design. Many of the real-world optimization problems are nonlinear, non-convex, multimodal, constrained, high-dimensional and often characterized by complex interactions between variables. Classical deterministic methods may be efficient when mathematical assumptions such as differentiability, convexity or availability of gradient information are satisfied. In practice, such assumptions are often violated in black-box optimization problems. This has led to a lot of research on metaheuristic algorithms, which search for high quality solutions using stochastic, population based and problem-independent mechanisms.
Population-based optimization covers a variety of well-known evolutionary, swarm-based and adaptive techniques. The representative examples include Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)

Despite their success, these algorithms still suffer from several persistent challenges such as premature convergence, poor population diversity control, sensitivity to parameter settings, slow convergence on ill-conditioned functions, and unstable performance over shifted, rotated, hybrid, and composition landscapes.
Recent review studies have verified that metaheuristic optimization remains a lively research direction because real-world engineering and global optimization problems are often characterized by nonlinearity, constraints, multimodality and high-dimensional search spaces. Houssein et al. presented a survey on recent metaheuristic applications and pointed out open issues including scalability, parameter sensitivity, premature convergence, and robustness to classes of problems. These challenges motivate the development of optimizers that combine adaptive search, diversity regulation and statistically validated performance

The No Free Lunch theorem states that there is no optimizer that can be better than all others for all optimization problems
9
. Hence, the development of new or improved optimization frameworks is still a valid and active research direction, as long as the proposed method introduces meaningful search mechanisms and is evaluated through rigorous benchmarking and statistical analysis. Biology-inspired optimization has been attracting attention in recent years. Biological systems often exhibit adaptive survival, cooperation, competition, memory, reproduction and environmental-response behaviors, which can be interpreted into computational search strategies.
Recently, the metaheuristic optimization paradigm has experienced a shift from the development of purely metaphor-based algorithms to the construction of more adaptive, hybrid, and statistically validated frameworks for complex search landscapes. The motivation behind this trend is the constant difficulty in solving high-dimensional, multimodal, shifted, rotated, and constrained problems. The main limitations are still premature convergence, loss of diversity, and weak stagnation recovery. Thus, recent studies have identified exploration–exploitation balancing, hybrid search operators, success-history learning, mathematical search guidance, and benchmark-based validation as key components for reliable optimizer design

Artificial Protozoa Optimizer (APO) is an emerging bio-inspired metaheuristic based on the survival mechanisms of protozoa through foraging, dormancy and reproduction behaviors
17
. APO gave competitive results on benchmark functions and engineering design problems. However, the original APO may still suffer from the limited adaptive control, inadequate memory utilization, and possible imbalance between exploration and exploitation when solving high-dimensional and composition optimization problems as many population-based algorithms. Such limitations motivate a more robust APO-based framework that can regulate the search behavior dynamically and exploit successful historical information.
The selection of APO as the underlying framework was based on three main reasons. First, it is a relatively recent optimizer which has demonstrated competitive performance on numerical and engineering optimization problems, thus representing a relevant platform for further methodological investigation. Second, the native foraging, dormancy, and reproduction behaviors produce heterogeneous search dynamics, but the original framework provides limited explicit coordination among these behaviors and does not employ the historical-solution memory and feedback-based operator-scoring mechanisms introduced in the present framework. Third, recent variants of APO indicate an increasing interest in extending the algorithm. Memory-guided learning, competitive operator control and explicit stagnation recovery are relatively less developed within the APO family. Therefore, APO provides a suitable test-bed to study whether the coordination of complementary search mechanisms driven by feedback can lead to improved robustness, without assuming that APO itself is universally superior to other meta-heuristics.
To solve these problems, in this paper, a Memory-Augmented Adaptive Artificial Protozoa Optimizer with Robust Competitive control (MAO-APO-RC) is proposed. The proposed framework improves APO by employing historical memory guidance, quasi-oppositional search, adaptive competitive operator selection, Lévy-based exploration, stagnation rescue, and success-history based parameter tuning. The memory part leads people to promising regions in the past. Quasi-oppositional search improves the probability of identifying better candidate solutions by taking the opposite regions of the search space into account. The competitive operator mechanism adaptively chooses between multiple search behaviors based on their success during the optimization process. At the same time, stagnation rescue and Levy exploration help to reduce premature convergence and improve the global search ability.
Recent studies still show that metaheuristic algorithms are still very active for complex engineering and global optimization problems. Recent reviews stress their usefulness for nonlinear, multimodal, constrained and high-dimensional engineering problems. They also point out open challenges concerning premature convergence, sensitivity to parameters, scalability and robustness with respect to problem classes. These results motivate the development of optimization frameworks that combine adaptive control, diversity regulation, and statistically validated performance, rather than a single fixed search behavior
8
.
To motivate the extension of APO in a more evidence-based manner, the baseline algorithm was analyzed by considering its convergence and population-diversity behavior on representative benchmark landscapes. Results show that APO is competitive across a range of problems, but its behavior depends on the problem. In some difficult shifted, hybrid and composition landscapes, the search exhibits higher population concentrations and/or longer periods of little improvement. These observations do not imply a universal deficiency of APO but rather point to search conditions under which further adaptive control might be beneficial. The proposed mechanisms were thus introduced to address complementary aspects of these observed and structural limitations:

Historical memory: deals with the limited explicit re-use of previously successful search information in the baseline APO, and provides guidance toward historically promising regions.

Quasi-oppositional search: adds more search-space coverage when population concentration decreases the exploratory capability.

Lévy exploration: makes occasional long range moves to reduce the chance of being trapped in the neighborhood of locally attractive areas.

Stagnation rescue: directly tackles sustained periods of no improvement by reintroducing controlled search activity.

Competitive operator control: Adjusts the selection of different search behaviors based on observed improvement to address their problem-dependent effectiveness.

Success-history adaptation: adapts the control parameters associated with successful search moves using information from the successful search moves, instead of using fixed values.

This mapping reflects the design rationale for MAO-APO-RC and not proof that each mechanism individually solves a specific limitation of APO. The ablation study is mechanism-oriented, meaning that it does not allow for a complete determination of the causal contribution of each component in isolation.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as below:

The key methodological contribution is a feedback-coupled multi-operator control architecture embedded in the APO framework. Rather than a single fixed search rule, heterogeneous exploitative and exploratory operators compete with online success scores, and their selection probabilities are continuously adapted based on the improvements they generate.

This operator-level feedback is synchronized with the historical-solution memory and success-history adaptation, providing two complementary levels of adaptation: online selection of effective search behaviors and reuse of successful search information. Within this adaptive control architecture, quasi-oppositional search and Lévy exploration are constituent scored operators, whereas stagnation rescue is an auxiliary conditional mechanism; none of these constituent mechanisms is claimed as individually novel.

A memory-augmented search strategy is designed to leverage historical best information while maintaining population-level diversity.

The proposed method is carried out on 15 benchmark functions in 30D and 50D, including shifted, rotated, hybrid, composition, multi-modal and ill-conditioned landscape.

The performance of MAO-APO-RC under constrained engineering-oriented conditions is evaluated using four classical engineering design benchmark problems.

The obtained results are verified by non-parametric statistical tests including Friedman ranking and Wilcoxon-Holm post-hoc analysis.

Population-based metaheuristic optimization
Population based metaheuristic algorithms explore and exploit the search space using a set of candidate solutions. PSO was proposed by Kennedy and Eberhart
1
 imitating the social learning behavior in swarms where individuals update their position based on personal and global best experiences. DE, proposed by Storn and Price
2

Several algorithms of swarm-intelligence have also been developed based on the animals’ behavior. GWO simulates the leadership hierarchy and hunting behavior of grey wolves
3

4
. SCA applies sine and cosine mathematical transformations to attract or repel candidate solutions to/from the best solution
5
. These algorithms are simple and flexible but may suffer from performance degradation on high-dimensional, rotated or composition landscapes if the exploration-exploitation balance is not well controlled.
Critical studies have also raised doubts on the scientific importance of simply presenting optimization algorithms via metaphorical descriptions, stating that important contributions should be judged based on their search mechanisms and empirical evidence
18
,
19

Another class of important optimizers are evolutionary strategies and variants of adaptive differential evolution. CMA-ES
6
 adapts the covariance matrix of a multivariate normal search distribution in order to allow an efficient search on difficult continuous optimization problems. SHADE proposed success-history-based parameter adaptation for DE, where successful parameter values are stored and reused to guide future search behavior
7

Recent works also show the effectiveness of adaptive and multi-strategy enhancement of population-based optimizers. Recent studies on metaheuristic and swarm-based optimization confirm that algorithmic improvement is increasingly achieved through modular hybridization, diversity control, adaptive operator selection, and problem-aware search mechanisms rather than relying on a single update rule. Artificial protozoa optimizer
Wang et al.
17
 introduced the Artificial Protozoa Optimizer (APO), a bio-inspired metaheuristic based on survival mechanisms of protozoa. APO models three major behaviors: foraging, dormancy and reproduction. Foraging can be either autotrophic or heterotrophic, two different ways of getting nutrition. In the algorithm, dormancy is used to replace weak or unfavorable individuals, and reproduction is used to generate perturbed offspring which are based on binary fission.
APO showed competitive performance against several state-of-the-art optimizers and was validated on benchmark functions, engineering design problems and image segmentation tasks
17
. However, the original APO is mainly based on its native behavioral operators, with limited adaptive memory and competitive learning mechanisms. For difficult optimization landscapes, especially shifted, rotated, hybrid and composition functions, a fixed behavioral structure may not be sufficient to maintain diversity, avoid stagnation and accelerate convergence consistently. This inspires the design of better APO variants with improved adaptive control and historical learning.
Since the introduction of the APO, several recent works have tried to extend or reinterpret the protozoa-inspired optimization. For instance, Wang et al. proposed MAAPO, a membrane-algorithm-based APO for global optimization, while Bo et al. developed MSAPO, a multi-strategy fusion APO to improve the robustness of the search. These recent developments based on APO suggest that APO is an emerging optimization family, but also suggest that more work is needed on memory-guided learning, adaptive operator competition, stagnation rescue, and statistically robust validation
33
.

Memory-guided and success-history adaptation
Memory mechanisms have been widely used to improve the optimization performance. In PSO, personal and global best positions are memory terms that guide the swarm toward promising regions
1
. In SHADE, the successful control parameters are stored in historical memories and sampled at later generations, thus improving robustness over fixed-parameter DE variants
7
. The results demonstrate that the memory can improve the convergence efficiency by keeping the useful search experience.
Memory in metaheuristic design can be at different levels : solution memory, parameter memory, operator memory and archive memory. Solution memory stores historically good positions, parameter memory stores successful control parameters and operator memory records which search behaviors recently yielded improvements. The MAO-APO-RC implements this principle by utilizing memory-guided movement and success-history adaptation to the APO framework. This enables the algorithm to benefit from previous search experience while maintaining stochastic exploration through competitive operator selection.
Opposition-based and quasi-oppositional search
Tizhoosh
34
 proposed the Opposition-Based Learning (OBL) based on the idea that the consideration of both a candidate solution and its opposite estimate could increase the probability of finding a better solution. OBL has been used in initialization, generation jumping, mutation and local search in many metaheuristic algorithms. Subsequent studies demonstrated that the use of opposition-based strategies can speed up the convergence and enhance the search space coverage when properly incorporated into population-based algorithms.
Quasi-oppositional search extends this to generate candidates between the center of the search space and the opposite point. This could be a more balanced alternative than strict opposition, since aggressive jumps are avoided and exploration of under-investigated regions is still encouraged. MAO-APO-RC: Quasi-oppositional search is one of the competitive operators to improve the diversity and help the population to escape from undesired regions.
Recent works on opposition-based learning have further confirmed that OBL is still a good enhancement approach for metaheuristic algorithms. Lakbichi et al. reviewed and classified OBL techniques in metaheuristics, and Rai et al. reported that OBL can improve efficiency and convergence behavior when properly embedded in optimization algorithms. These results support the use of quasi-oppositional search in MAO-APO-RC, especially as a controlled diversity-enhancement mechanism instead of a random restart strategy
35
.
Exploration–exploitation balance and stagnation control
One of the most important design issues in metaheuristic optimization is the balance between exploration and exploitation. Exploration enables the algorithm to investigate various regions of the search space, while exploitation refines promising candidate solutions. Too much exploration can slow down convergence, and too much exploitation can cause premature convergence. This trade-off is particularly difficult on rotated, hybrid, and composition functions, where dependencies between variables and local optima can misguide the search process.
To address this problem, several mechanisms have been proposed, including adaptive parameters, restart strategies, opposition-based learning, chaotic maps, Lévy flights, local search, and hybridization. Lévy-based exploration is frequently used to produce occasional long-range jumps, which may help to escape from local optima. Strategies of restart or rescue can also reinsert diversity when the search process becomes stagnant. MAO-APO-RC adopts these concepts by employing adaptive competitive control, Lévy exploration and stagnation rescue to control the switch from global search to local search.
Recent research on metaheuristics points out that the introduction of a new biological metaphor is not sufficient to guarantee strong performance: effective search-control mechanisms are key. Adaptive parameters, restart strategies, opposition-based search, local search or multi-strategy fusion are commonly used in enhanced and hybrid optimizers for improving convergence and reducing stagnation. This trend is consistent with the design philosophy of MAO-APO-RC, in which the original biological operators of APO are enhanced by memory guidance, adaptive competitive control, quasi-oppositional search, Lévy exploration, and stagnation rescue
36
.

Engineering design optimization remains a central validation domain for metaheuristic algorithms because it combines nonlinear objectives, mixed design variables, inequality constraints, and strict feasibility requirements. Recent studies in 2025 and 2026 continue to use classical constrained problems such as the tension/compression spring, pressure vessel, welded beam, three-bar truss, speed reducer, cantilever beam, tubular column, and clutch brake designs as practical testbeds for assessing convergence speed, constraint handling, robustness, and solution quality. Recent adaptive operator selection and positioning of MAO-APO-RC
Recent work on adaptive operator selection (AOS) has increasingly considered operator selection as an online decision problem, where the relative usefulness of search operators varies during evolution. In
48
, Jiao et al. integrated Q-learning with a reference-vector-based multi-objective evolutionary algorithm, where the crossover operators are selected adaptively according to the performance of the generated offspring. Ming et al.
49
 proposed a deep reinforcement learning-assisted online operator selection framework for constrained multi-objective optimization, where the population state is represented by convergence, diversity and feasibility, the actions by candidate operators, and the reward by population improvement. Zhang et al.
50
 proposed LS-AOS mechanism, which uses a campaign-incumbency procedure, two-layer operator supervision, and an election campaign indicator combining convergence and diversity to evaluate the quality of operators and dynamically regulate operator pools. A more recent work is DRL-AEOSF by Guo et al.

These studies indicate the growing importance of dynamic evaluation of the operator and the stage-dependent operator selection in contemporary evolutionary optimization.
Competitive operator control in MAO-APO-RC differs from these recent approaches in terms of its information requirements and its coupling with the underlying optimizer. It does not learn a Q-network or deep-reinforcement-learning policy. It does not explicitly encode the population state as a learned state-action representation. Instead, candidate operators are initially given the same score, and their probability of selection is adapted on-line according to the normalized success score, which is reinforced or penalized according to the improvement obtained during the search. The score-based feedback is directly applied to the seven scored operators: autotrophic foraging, heterotrophic foraging, dormancy, reproduction, memory-guided search, quasi-oppositional search, and Lévy exploration. Stagnation rescue and success-history parameter adaptation operate as auxiliary mechanisms outside this categorical operator-scoring process. Thus, MAO-APO-RC’s contribution is not a general-purpose learned operator selector. Rather, it is a feedback-driven competitive operator-control mechanism embedded in an APO-family framework. Compared to state-rich reinforcement-learning approaches, this design does not require neural-policy training, nor compared to LS-AOS, does it require campaign-based operator pools or multi-objective convergence-diversity indicators. The approach proposed is therefore a different design trade-off between selector complexity, online feedback and integration of heterogeneous search mechanisms.

This section presents the proposed Memory-Augmented Adaptive Artificial Protozoa Optimizer with Robust Competitive Control, MAO-APO-RC. The proposed algorithm is an extension of the original Artificial Protozoa Optimizer with the use of memory guided learning, quasi-oppositional search, Levy-based exploration, adaptive competitive operator selection, success-history parameter adaptation and stagnation rescue. These mechanisms aim to improve the balance between exploration and exploitation, avoid premature convergence and improve robustness for complex numerical and engineering optimization problems.
The proposed MAO-APO-RC framework improves the original APO by introducing a multi-stage optimization architecture that incorporates exploration enhancement, competitive candidate selection, and adaptive learning mechanisms, as shown in Fig. 
3
. After the initial population is generated and the fitness of individuals is assessed, the algorithm keeps track of the population diversity and stagnation. A variety of candidate solutions are produced by complementary search operators, including APO-based search, memory-guided learning, quasi-oppositional search, Lévy-flight exploration, and rescue mechanisms for stagnated individuals. These candidates are evaluated by a robust competitive control strategy which selects the best promising solution for everyone. Finally, adaptive learning updates memory archive, operator success statistics and success-history parameters, thus allowing the search behavior to evolve based on previous optimization experiences. The integrated workflow improves the trade-off between exploration and exploitation, accelerates convergence and increases the robustness against premature convergence on complex optimization landscapes.

The components of MAO-APO-RC were introduced to overcome complementary limitations of a fixed APO search process, and not to increase algorithmic complexity as such. The memory-guided search retains successful historical information and enhances the exploitation of promising regions, whereas the quasi-oppositional search adds a diversity-oriented mechanism to explore underexplored regions of the search space. Lévy perturbations provide sporadic long-range exploratory moves and stagnation-rescue mechanism replenishes search activity when improvement stops. The coordination layer is provided by competitive operator control, which favors search behaviors based on their observed success. Success-history adaptation dynamically adjusts the search using information from successful moves. The integrated framework is therefore based on the rationale that different landscape characteristics may require different search behaviors at different stages but the utility of their combination must be established empirically rather than assumed from the fact that multiple mechanisms are present.
Accordingly, the novelty claimed in this study is architectural rather than operator-level: it concerns the feedback-driven coordination of heterogeneous APO-family search behaviors with historical-memory and success-history learning, not the independent invention of each constituent search mechanism.

\(\:f\left(x\right)\)
is the objective function, 
\(\:{\Omega\:}\)
 is the feasible search space, 
\(\:L\)
 is the lower-bound vector, and 
\(\:U\)
 is the upper-bound vector. The solution vector is represented as:
$$\:{x}_{i}=\left[{x}_{ir1},{x}_{i2},\dots\:,{x}_{i,}D\right]$$

                    (4)
                
where x
i
 represents the 
\(\:i\)

Population initialization
Let N be the population size. The initial population is generated randomly within the search boundaries as follows:
$$\:{x}_{ij}^{0}={L}_{j}+{r}_{ij}\times\:\left({U}_{j}-{L}_{j}\right)$$

                    (5)
                
$$\:\text{w}\text{h}\text{e}\text{r}\text{e}:\:\text{i}=\text{1,2},\dots\:,\text{N},\:j=\text{1,2},\dots\:,D,\:\text{a}\text{n}\text{d}\:{r}_{ij}\in\:\left[\text{0,1}\right]$$
After initialization, the fitness value of each protozoan is evaluated, and the best solution is stored as 
\(\:{x}^{\text{best\:}}\)
APO-based search behaviors
The original APO simulates three main protozoan survival behaviors: foraging, dormancy, and reproduction. In MAO-APO-RC, these behaviors are retained as the basic search foundation.
The APO-based candidate solution is denoted as:
$$\:{\text{V}}_{\text{i}}^{\text{a}\text{p}\text{o}}=\text{A}\text{P}\text{O}\_\text{Search\:}\left({\text{x}}_{\text{i}}\right)$$

                    (6)
                
where 
\(\:\text{A}\text{P}\text{O}\_\text{Search\:}\left(.\right)\)
 represents one of the APO search behaviors, including autotrophic foraging, heterotrophic foraging, dormancy, or reproduction.
Autotrophic and heterotrophic foraging mainly guide the population during exploration and exploitation, while dormancy and reproduction help regenerate or perturb solutions to maintain search activity.
Memory-guided learning
To improve exploitation and preserve useful information from previous successful solutions, MAO-APO-RC uses a historical solution memory M. This archive stores historically good solutions during the search process. The memory-guided candidate is generated as:
$$\:{v}_{i}^{mem}={x}_{i}+\alpha\:{r}_{1}\left({m}_{k}-{x}_{i}\right)+\beta\:{r}_{2}\left({x}^{\text{best\:}}-{x}_{i}\right)$$

                    (7)
                
where: 
\(\:{\text{m}}_{\text{k}}\in\:\text{M}\)
, 
\(\:{r}_{1},\:\:{r}_{2}\in\:\left[\text{0,1}\right]\)
, 
\(\:\alpha\:\)
is the memory learning coefficient, and 
\(\:\beta\:\)
 is the global-best attraction coefficient. The term 
\(\:\left({m}_{k}-{x}_{i}\right)\)
 guides the current solution toward a historically successful solution, while the term 
\(\:\left({x}^{\text{best\:}}-{x}_{i}\right)\)
 improves exploitation around the best solution found so far. In the implemented memory-guided operator, the two guidance coefficients are scheduled deterministically according to search progress. Let 
\(\:r=t/T\)
denote normalized search progress. Then 
\(\:\alpha\:\left(r\right)=0.25+0.35\left(1-r\right),\:\beta\:\left(r\right)=0.25+0.45r.\)
 Thus, 
\(\:\alpha\:\)
decreases from approximately 0.60 to 0.25, whereas 
\(\:\beta\:\)
increases from approximately 0.25 to 0.70. The schedule gives relatively greater emphasis to historical-memory information in the earlier search and progressively increases global-best guidance. These values were used unchanged for all experiments and were not optimized separately for individual problems; therefore, no claim of theoretical optimality is made. Let the historical memory at iteration 
\(\:t\)
be.
\(\:{M}^{t}=\left\{{m}_{1}^{t},{m}_{2}^{t},\dots\:,{m}_{N}^{t}\right\},\)
where 
\(\:{m}_{i}^{t}\in\:{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\)
is the best historical solution retained for the 
\(\:i\)
-th population position. The memory is initialized directly from the initial population:
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{m}_{i}^{0}={x}_{i}^{0},\:{f}_{M,i}^{0}=f\left({x}_{i}^{0}\right),\:i=1,\dots\:,N.&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (7a)
                
Therefore, the historical memory has a fixed capacity
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:\mid\:{M}^{t}\mid\:=N&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (7b)
                
throughout the optimization process.
Let 
\(\:{y}_{i}^{t}\)
denote the accepted candidate generated for individual 
\(\:i\)
. The corresponding historical-memory entry is updated according to
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{m}_{i}^{t+1}=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}{y}_{i}^{t},&\:f\left({y}_{i}^{t}\right)<f\left({m}_{i}^{t}\right),\\\:{m}_{i}^{t},&\:\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (7c)
                
with
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{f}_{M,i}^{t+1}=\text{min}\text{\hspace{0.17em}}\left[{f}_{M,i}^{t},f\left({y}_{i}^{t}\right)\right].&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (7d)
                
Thus, a memory entry is overwritten only by a strictly improving solution. The memory 
\(\:M\)
is not an append-only archive and therefore requires no capacity-reduction procedure. No explicit duplicate-elimination operation is applied; consequently, identical or numerically very similar historical solutions may occur in different indexed memory entries, although they do not increase the memory size.
Quasi-oppositional search
To enhance diversity and improve search-space coverage, MAO-APO-RC applies quasi-oppositional search. First, the opposite solution of x
i
 is calculated as:
$$\:{x}_{i}^{\text{o}\text{p}\text{p}}=L+U-{x}_{i}$$

                    (8)
                
The center of the search space is defined as:
$$\:c=(L+U)/2$$

                    (9)
                
Then, the quasi-oppositional candidate is generated as:
$$\:{v}_{i}^{\varphi\:o}=c+{r}_{3}\left({x}_{i}^{\circ\:pp}-c\right)$$

                    (10)
                
where: 
\(\:{r}_{3}\in\:\left[\text{0,1}\right]\)
. This operator searches for the region between the center of the search space and the opposite position of the current solution. Therefore, it provides additional diversity without applying a fully random restart.
Lévy-based exploration
To strengthen global exploration and help the algorithm escape local optima, MAO-APO-RC uses Lévy-flight perturbation. The Lévy-based candidate is generated as:
$$\:{v}_{i}^{\text{levy\:}}={x}_{i}+\gamma\:{L}_{v}\odot\:\left({x}_{i}-{x}^{\text{best\:}}\right)$$

                    (11)
                
where: 
\(\:\gamma\:\)
 is the Lévy step-size coefficient, 
\(\:{L}_{v}\)
 is the Lévy-flight vector, and 
\(\:\odot\:\)
 denotes element-wise multiplication.
Each element of the Lévy-flight vector is calculated as:
$$\:{L}_{wrj}={u}_{j}/{\left|{v}_{j}\right|}^{\wedge\:}(1/\lambda\:)$$

                    (12)
                
where: 
\(\:{u}_{j}\sim\:N\left(0,{\sigma\:}_{u}^{2}\right)\)
, 
\(\:{v}_{j}\sim\:N\left(\text{0,1}\right)\)
, and 
\(\:1<\lambda\:\le\:2\)
. This mechanism introduces occasional long-distance moves, which helps the population escape from local optimum regions.
Stagnation rescue mechanism
The search is said to be stagnant if the current best solution does not change for a predefined number of iterations $T_s$. In this case, MAO-APO-RC initiates a rescue strategy.
The rescue candidate is produced as:
$$\:{v}_{i}^{\text{res\:}}={x}^{\text{best\:}}+\eta\:{r}_{4}(U-L)\odot\:z$$

                    (13)
                
where: 
\(\:\eta\:\)
 is the rescue amplitude, 
\(\:{r}_{3}\in\:\left[\text{0,1}\right]\)
, and 
\(\:\text{z}\sim\:\text{N}(0,\text{I})\)
. This strategy brings back a controlled diversity around the current best solution and decreases the probability of long-term stagnation.
If the global-best objective does not change by a numerically meaningful amount, the stagnation counter is incremented and it is reset when an improvement occurs. In the current implementation, the rescue is activated after 12 consecutive stagnating iterations. The threshold is fixed across all numerical and engineering problems and is not optimized independently for each function. The rescue stage is a population-level mechanism separate from the seven adaptively scored operators. When triggered, it concentrates on the worst 20% of the current population and generates controlled diversification candidates through the implemented rescue procedure. The resulting candidates are boundary clipped and evaluated by the same fitness function as the main search and are accepted only if they improve the corresponding current solutions. Then the stagnation counter is reset to zero.
The choice of 12 iterations is a practical compromise. A much smaller threshold could trigger rescue during short stochastic plateaus and unduly disturb productive exploitation, whereas a much larger threshold could delay recovery from real stagnation. Hence, the value is regarded as a fixed implementation parameter rather than a theoretically optimal threshold. Such sensitivity analysis is left for future work. No systematic sensitivity analysis of this threshold was conducted.
Adaptive competitive operator selection
MAO-APO-RC distinguishes between controller-selected operators and auxiliary search components. The adaptive controller maintains scores for seven operators: autotrophic foraging, heterotrophic foraging, dormancy, reproduction, memory-guided search, quasi-oppositional search, and Lévy exploration. At initialization, these seven operators have equal scores and equal selection probabilities. During optimization, at least one operator is sampled for each individual according to the normalized success scores. A second controller-selected trial is considered for lower-ranked individuals, while an additional quasi-oppositional trial is introduced for the lowest-ranked portion of the population. The candidate set of the i-th solution is defined as:
$$\:{V}_{i}=\left\{{V}_{i}^{spo},{V}_{i}^{mem},{V}_{i}^{\varphi\:o},{V}_{i}^{levy},{V}_{i}^{res}\right\}$$

                    (14)
                
The best candidate is picked as:
$$\:{{v}_{i}}^{*}=\text{a}\text{r}\text{g}minf\left(v\right)\text{,\:for\:all\:}v\in\:{V}_{i}$$

                    (15)
                
Then the solution is updated through greedy selection:
$$\:\text{\:If\:}f\left({v}_{i}^{*}\right)<f\left({x}_{i}^{*}\right)\text{,\:then:\:}$$
$$\:{x}_{i}^{t+1}={v}_{i}^{*}$$

                    (16)
                
Otherwise:
$$\:{x}_{i}^{t+1}={x}_{i}^{t}$$

                    (17)
                
Such competitive selection mechanism allows the most successful operator to guide each solution in its search process.
The framework also has auxiliary candidates, which are not independent members of the seven-operator categorical controller. These are the success-history current-to-
p
-best/1-like candidate, late-stage local intensification, and stagnation-rescue mechanism. The candidate pool accessible to an individual is hence a function of its population rank, search stage, stagnation state, and remaining function-evaluation budget.
The same implemented fitness function is used to evaluate all generated candidates. For unconstrained numerical benchmarks this is the original objective value, for the engineering benchmarks this is the penalized objective described in Sect.

. Greedily accept the best improving candidate. The success statistics of controller-selected operators are updated according to the observed improvement, which allows their normalized selection probabilities to evolve during optimization.

Operator success score update
The success score of each operator measures its recent ability to improve the solutions. If operator o improves a solution at iteration t, then its success indicator is given by:
$$\:{I}_{0}^{t}=1$$

                    (18)
                
If the operator 
\(\:0\)
 does not improve the solution, then:
$$\:{{\text{I}}_{0}}^{\text{t}}=0$$

                    (19)
                
The score for operator o is updated as follows:
$$\:{S}_{0}^{t+1}=(1-\rho\:){S}_{0}^{t}+\rho\:{I}_{0}^{t}$$

                    (20)
                
where: 
\(\:{S}_{0}^{t}\)
 is the success score of operator o at iteration 
\(\:t\)
, 
\(\:\rho\:\)
 the learning rate, and
$$\:0<\rho\:<1$$
To convert the updated success scores into valid operator-selection probabilities, the score of each operator is first bounded to avoid either complete exclusion or excessive dominance:
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{\stackrel{-}{S}}_{o}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}=\text{min}\left({S}_{\text{m}\text{a}\text{x}},\text{max}\left({S}_{\text{m}\text{i}\text{n}},{S}_{o}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}\right)\right),&\:&\:\text{}\end{array}\text{}$$

                    (20a)
                
where 
\(\:{S}_{\text{m}\text{i}\text{n}}=0.05\)
and 
\(\:{S}_{\text{m}\text{a}\text{x}}=10\)
. Let 
\(\:\mathcal{O}=\left\{1,\dots\:,K\right\}\)
denote the set of 
\(\:K\)
candidate operators. The normalized probability assigned to operator 
\(\:o\)
for the next selection is
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{P}_{o}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}=\frac{{\stackrel{-}{S}}_{o}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}}{\sum\:_{j=1}^{K}{\stackrel{-}{S}}_{j}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}},\:o\in\:\mathcal{O},&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (20b)
                
such that
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{P}_{o}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}>0,\:\sum\limits_{o=1}^{K}{P}_{o}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}=1.&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (20c)
                
The operator used in the next competitive search step is then sampled from the categorical distribution
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{o}_{t+1}\sim\:\text{C}\text{a}\text{t}\text{e}\text{g}\text{o}\text{r}\text{i}\text{c}\text{a}\text{l}\left({P}_{1}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1},{P}_{2}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1},\dots\:,{P}_{K}^{\text{\hspace{0.17em}}t+1}\right).&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (20d)
                
At initialization, all operator scores are set to 
\(\:{S}_{o}^{0}=1\)
. Consequently,
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{P}_{o}^{0}=\frac{1}{K}.&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (20e)
                
In the present implementation, 
\(\:K=7\)
, corresponding to autotrophic foraging, heterotrophic foraging, dormancy, reproduction, memory-guided search, opposition-based search, and Lévy exploration. Hence, all operators initially have the same probability 
\(\:{P}_{o}^{0}=1/7\)
. As optimization proceeds, operators associated with successful improvements receive higher scores through Eq. (20), which increases their normalized probability in Eq. (20b), whereas less successful operators receive relatively lower probabilities. The lower score bound ensures that no operator is permanently removed from the candidate pool, thereby retaining a minimum level of exploration.
The probability adaptation in Eqs. (20a)–(20e) controls which operator is selected, whereas the success-history mechanism described in Sect. 
Success-history parameter adaptation
 controls the parameter values associated with successful search moves; the two adaptation processes therefore operate at different levels of the proposed framework.
Success-history parameter adaptation
MAO-APO-RC employs success-history adaptation for the current-to-
\(\:p\)
-best search parameters 
\(\:F\)
and 
\(\:CR\)
. Two historical memories, 
\(\:{M}_{F}\)
and 
\(\:{M}_{CR}\)
, each of size 
\(\:H=6\)
, are initialized to 0.5. At each sampling step, a memory index 
\(\:h\)
is selected uniformly. The scaling factor 
\(\:F\)
is sampled from a Cauchy distribution centered at 
\(\:{M}_{F}\left[h\right]\)
with scale 0.1 and clipped to 
\(\:\left[0.05,1\right]\)
, whereas 
\(\:CR\)
is sampled from a normal distribution centered at 
\(\:{M}_{CR}\left[h\right]\)
with standard deviation 0.1 and clipped to 
\(\:\left[0,1\right]\)
. After successful replacements, the corresponding successful 
\(\:F\)
and 
\(\:CR\)
values are used to update the historical memories, with improvement-weighted adaptation. This success-history mechanism is distinct from the scheduled memory-guidance coefficients 
\(\:\alpha\:\)
and 
\(\:\beta\:\)
, which vary deterministically with search progress.
In addition to the per-individual historical memory 
\(\:M\)
, MAO-APO-RC maintains an auxiliary external archive 
\(\:A\)
for the success-history/current-to-pbest search component. When a candidate 
\(\:{\varvec{y}}_{i}^{t}\)
successfully replaces the current solution 
\(\:{\varvec{x}}_{i}^{t}\)
, the displaced parent is inserted into the archive:
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:{A}^{t+1}={A}^{t}\cup\:\left\{{x}_{i}^{t}\right\}.&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (21d)
                
The archive capacity is limited to the population size 
\(\:N\)

$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:\mid\:A\mid\:\le\:N.&\:&\:\end{array}$$

                    (21e)
                
If insertion causes 
\(\:\mid\:A\mid\:>N\)

\(\:A\)

Constraint handling for engineering design
For all four constrained engineering problems, the inequality constraints are expressed in the form 
\(\:{g}_{j}\left(\varvec{x}\right)\le\:0\)
. The implemented penalized objective is
$$\:\begin{array}{cccc}&\:F\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)+{10}^{12}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\left[\text{max}\left(0,{g}_{j}\left(x\right)\right)\right]}^{2},&\:\:\:\:\:\:&\:\text{}\end{array}$$

                    (23)
                
Therefore, a satisfied constraint has zero penalty, while a positive violation of a constraint is squared and multiplied with the common static coefficient 
\(\:\lambda\:={10}^{12}\)
. All constraints and all four engineering benchmark problems use the same value of 
\(\:\lambda\:\)
 without any problem-specific tuning. No constraint-specific weighting or normalization is applied before the quadratic penalty, so each violation is penalized using the numerical scale of its original constraint expression. This means that constraints with larger numerical violations might have a bigger impact on the overall penalty. The component-wise clipping procedure from Eq. (22) treats variable-bound violations separately. No other nonlinear feasibility-repair mechanism is used.
Using 
\(\:\lambda\:={10}^{12}\)
enforces strong feasibility but is a fixed choice of implementation, not a theoretically optimal or scale-invariant setting. If the coefficient is too small infeasible candidates would be competitive and if it were too large the penalty term would dominate the original objective function near the boundary of the feasibility. Furthermore, the current formulation does not normalize the individual constraint violations so its behavior may depend on the relative numerical scales of the constraint functions. Thus, a systematic comparison with normalized or adaptive or alternative feasibility-handling strategies is left for future work.

The measured runtime overhead can be explained by the additional operations performed within the same function-evaluation budget. Instead of getting more objective-function evaluations than APO, MAO-APO-RC has the available evaluations distributed among a more diverse set of candidate-generation mechanisms. Each individual is given at least one operator trial, chosen adaptively; the lower half of the ranked individuals are given a second competitive trial and the lowest 20% are given an additional quasi-oppositional trial. A success-history current-to-$p$-best candidate is also evaluated whenever the budget allows. Late stage local intensification is limited to a small elite subset, while stagnation rescue is launched after the stagnation threshold and focuses on the worst 20% of the population. Other non-objective-evaluation operations are historical-memory updating, external-archive maintenance, operator-score and probability updates, and success-history adaptation. These operations keep the leading asymptotic complexity order but increase the constant computational cost. The implementation only logged total optimizer runtime, so precise wall-clock fractions for individual mechanisms are not available and thus not estimated retrospectively.
From a practical simplification perspective, the ablation results indicate that APO + QOD is a particularly attractive lower-complexity alternative because it achieved a slightly better mean rank than the complete framework without a statistically significant difference between the two configurations.
Experiment and results
This section evaluates the performance of the proposed MAO-APO-RC algorithm by a comprehensive set of experiments. The evaluation is done on a diverse benchmark suite containing unimodal, multimodal, hybrid and composition functions in 30 and 50 dimensional search spaces. The proposed method is compared with the original APO and the selected comparison algorithms PSO, DE, GWO, WOA, SCA, CMAES-lite, and SHADE-lite.
In the comprehensive performance evaluation, various aspects of performance are examined, such as optimization accuracy, convergence behavior, population diversity dynamics, statistical significance, component-wise contribution through ablation studies, and performance on constrained engineering design problems. Moreover, the statistical tests of Friedman and Wilcoxon-Holm are used to test the significance of the observed differences. The computational complexity and runtime of the proposed modifications are analyzed to evaluate the efficiency.
The experimental results demonstrate that MAO-APO-RC achieves competitive aggregate performance across a broad range of the evaluated optimization scenarios while maintaining a favorable balance between exploration and exploitation.
Experimental setup
To investigate the behavior of the algorithms as the dimensionality increases in the range of evaluation, each benchmark function was tested on 30D and 50D. The experiments are evidence of optimization performance at the tested dimensionalities but not meant to demonstrate general large-scale scalability beyond 50 dimensions. In addition, four classical constrained engineering design problems were taken, namely tension/compression spring, pressure vessel, welded beam and three-bar truss, to test the method on well-known engineering optimization benchmarks.
The performance of MAO-APO-RC was compared with eight optimization algorithms: the original (APO), (PSO), (DE), (GWO), (WOA), (SCA), CMAES-lite and SHADE-lite. The chosen comparison methods are popular general-purpose metaheuristic and evolutionary optimization methods, not algorithms specifically designed for large-scale optimization. Therefore, the comparison presented here is for relative performance within the considered 30D–50D setting and should not be taken as evidence of competitiveness against specialized large-scale optimization methods. For this purpose, a dedicated comparison to state-of-the-art LSO algorithms on established large scale benchmark suites would be required.
All algorithms were run for 30 independent runs to ensure statistical reliability. A common base random seed of 42 was used to ensure reproducibility of the random-number streams. The value 42 was not simply passed on to every algorithm, but deterministic seeds were derived from the base seed for each algorithm, independent run, problem and dimension. Consequently, all methods utilized the same seed generation scheme but had independent stochastic trajectories. This procedure guaranties reproducibility without forcing different algorithms, which use random numbers differently, to use the same random-number sequence. For all algorithms, the population size was set to 50. The maximum number of function evaluations for the benchmark functions was set to (10000 × D), where D is the dimension of the problem. Hence, the maximum number of function evaluations was 300,000 and 500,000 for 30D and 50D problems, respectively. For engineering design problems, the number of function evaluations was limited to 50,000. The results were given as mean, standard deviation, best, worst, median and average runtime values Moreover, Friedman ranking, Wilcoxon signed rank test, and Holm post-hoc correction were applied to assess the statistical significance of the obtained results, as shown in Table

No problem-specific tuning was performed, and the same parameter configuration was used for all benchmark functions, dimensions and engineering design problems. In MAO-APO-RC, some of the control quantities are adaptive/scheduled rather than fixed hyperparameters. In particular, the memory-guidance coefficients depend on the search progress, the success-history parameters are updated from successful moves, and the operator-selection probabilities are computed from the online success scores. All other fixed meta-parameters, like the size of the success-history memory and the stagnation-control settings, were kept constant across all experiments. No systematic sensitivity analysis of these fixed settings was performed in the present experimental design, and no claims are made about parameter insensitivity or optimality.
The adaptive mechanisms reduce reliance on predetermined fixed values for selected search quantities; however, the sensitivity of the complete framework to its remaining fixed meta-parameters is not established in the present study. It should also be stressed that the online adaptation of some control quantities does not imply that the complete framework is insensitive to its remaining fixed meta-parameters. All problems employ a single common configuration, indicating that there is no problem-specific tuning in the present experiments, but this is not a parameter-sensitivity analysis. Thus, adaptive design is to be understood as a reduction of dependence on fixed values for chosen search quantities, rather than as evidence for general insensitivity of hyperparameters.

The observed behavior can be also associated with the functional roles of the MAO-APO-RC mechanisms. Quasi-oppositional search and Lévy perturbations offer candidates in remote or under-explored regions, and the stagnation-recovery mechanism ensures diversity of search when progress ceases. And those mechanisms are mostly for exploration. Conversely, guidance from historical memory, best-directed search, and local intensification build upon previously successful and currently promising areas. The competitive operator-selection mechanism dynamically coordinates these heterogeneous behaviors by increasing the probability of the operators that generate successful improvements. Therefore MAO-APO-RC does not depend on a fixed switch between exploration and exploitation, but the relative contribution of the search behaviors dynamically changes according to the search progress and the success of the operators.
Thus, the evidence of convergence and diversity together suggest an adaptive, problem-dependent switching from exploration to exploitation. The results support a useful balance over the evaluated benchmark suite, but do not imply that a universal optimal exploration-exploitation ratio is reached for every problem.

Discussion
The overall benchmark results should therefore be interpreted in a problem-dependent manner. MAO-APO-RC achieves the best aggregate Friedman mean rank and statistically significant differences relative to six of the eight comparison algorithms, but its differences from APO and SHADE-lite are not statistically significant after Holm correction. Moreover, the proposed method is not the best performer on every individual problem, with F15 at 50D providing a particularly clear counterexample. Consequently, the results support competitive aggregate performance rather than universal statistical or numerical superiority.
The experimental data do not prove that the full MAO-APO-RC architecture is always better than simpler alternatives. MAO-APO-RC achieves the best aggregate Friedman mean rank on the numerical benchmark suite, but the pairwise differences to APO and SHADE-lite are not significant after Holm correction. Similarly, APO + QOD obtains the best ablation mean rank of 1.8500 compared to 1.9167 for MAO-APO-RC, with no statistically significant difference between the two configurations. The results indicate that the extra mechanisms of the complete framework are not always beneficial but depend on the problem.
This difference is also significant from a practical perspective since the complete framework suffers a constant-factor computation overhead. In the benchmark experiments, the observed runtime ratio (compared to APO) is approximately 1.481×, in the engineering experiments it is 1.594×, and in the ablation experiments it is 1.490×. The present results therefore do not warrant the increased complexity on the basis of uniformly improved solution quality. Instead, MAO-APO-RC should be considered as a more general search architecture for adaptive search that pays the price of additional computation for heterogeneous search capabilities, while simpler solutions such as APO + QOD may be more desirable when computational efficiency and implementation ease are of concern.
The robustness of MAO-APO-RC is evaluated from several complementary perspectives. First, all the numerical experiments are repeated in 30 independent runs, and the statistics reported are the mean, standard deviation, best, worst and median objective values. Therefore, robustness is evaluated not only by the best solution obtained but also by the variability of stochastic outcomes. Secondly, the benchmark suite consists of heterogeneous shifted, ill-conditioned, multimodal, hybrid and composition landscapes, while the engineering evaluation offers nonlinear constrained search spaces. The convergence and population-diversity analysis also show that MAO-APO-RC tends to gradually transition from exploration to exploitation rather than abruptly collapsing the population. However, the statistical and ablation results also show problem-dependent behavior. Specifically, MAO-APO-RC is not statistically better than all the comparators and is not the best configuration for each single problem. Thus, the observed robustness should be understood as stable and competitive behavior over the considered problem classes rather than as universal supremacy.
Dimensional robustness was examined by evaluating all numerical benchmark functions at 30D and 50D under a maximum function-evaluation budget of 
\(\:\text{10,000}D\)
, corresponding to 300,000 evaluations at 30D and 500,000 evaluations at 50D. MAO-APO-RC remained competitive on several shifted, ill-conditioned, and hybrid functions as dimensionality increased from 30D to 50D. These results provide evidence of dimensional robustness only within the evaluated 30D–50D range. Because dimensions above 50 were not evaluated, no claim of general large-scale scalability is made.
The adaptive operator-control mechanism updates the operator scores and selection probabilities online, but in the present experiments no complete iteration-wise telemetry of operator selection frequencies, probability trajectories, or operator-specific improvement contributions were maintained. A further limitation concerns the comparator set. The present experiments compare MAO-APO-RC with general-purpose optimizers, including APO, PSO, DE, GWO, WOA, SCA, CMAES-lite, and SHADE-lite, but do not include algorithms specifically developed for large-scale optimization. Consequently, the results cannot establish competitiveness with specialized LSO approaches. Combined with the absence of a dedicated CEC large-scale benchmark suite and dimensions substantially above 50, this limits the scalability conclusions to dimensional robustness within the tested range. Future work should therefore evaluate MAO-APO-RC against established LSO-specific algorithms under common evaluation budgets on standardized large-scale benchmark problems.
Future diagnostic studies should therefore retain operator-selection frequencies, operator-specific improvements, memory-archive evolution, and success-history trajectories to determine more precisely why particular landscapes, such as F15, favor simpler configurations.
Conclusion and future works
MAO-APO-RC achieved the best aggregate Friedman mean rank of 2.3667 on the numerical benchmark suite. However, pairwise Wilcoxon–Holm testing showed that its difference from the original APO was not statistically significant (
\(\:{p}_{\text{a}\text{d}\text{j}}=0.221872\)
). Therefore, the improved aggregate ranking should be interpreted as evidence of competitive overall performance rather than statistically established superiority over APO. The Wilcoxon–Holm analysis nevertheless showed statistically significant differences between MAO-APO-RC and six of the eight comparison algorithms, while the difference relative to SHADE-lite was also not statistically significant after multiple-comparison correction. The ablation study also showed that APO+QOD achieved the best reduced-variant mean rank of 1.8500 and was closely followed by MAO-APO-RC with 1.9167; the difference between them was not statistically significant 
\(\:\left({p}_{\text{a}\text{d}\text{j}}=0.5901\right).\)
Conversely, MAO-APO-RC was significantly different from APO+Memory 
\(\:\left({p}_{\text{a}\text{d}\text{j}}=0.00465\right)\)
and APO+Energy 
\(\:\left({p}_{\text{a}\text{d}\text{j}}=0.000270\right)\)
. On the four classical constrained engineering benchmarks, MAO-APO-RC and DE obtained a joint-best mean rank of 1.875. However, no single pairwise superiority could be detected after Holm correction. The integrated adaptive mechanisms led to runtime ratios of approximately 1.481×, 1.594×, and 1.490× against APO in the benchmarking, engineering and ablation experiments, respectively. Overall, these results support the competitiveness of MAO-APO-RC in the problem set considered, while showing also problem-dependent trade-offs and additional computational cost. Future work should evaluate MAO-APO-RC at dimensions substantially above 50 using standardized large-scale benchmark suites and specialized LSO comparators, perform systematic sensitivity analysis of the remaining fixed meta-parameters, retain operator-level and archive-level telemetry for diagnostic analysis, and extend validation to a wider range of engineering applications. The present 30D–50D experiments therefore support dimensional robustness within the evaluated range but do not establish general large-scale scalability.

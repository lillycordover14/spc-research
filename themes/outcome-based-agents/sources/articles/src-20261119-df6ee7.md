Regulators starting to keep pace with innovative medicine development&nbsp;&nbsp;Clinical Trials Arena

[page text]
At London Life Sciences Week, experts overall agreed that regulators are managing to keep pace with innovation. Credit: Khan Vector Co / Shutterstock.com

At a time when innovative medicines with new mechanisms of action (MOA) are growing at pace thanks to artificial intelligence (AI) helping in discovery, regulators have been working to keep up.

While this 
has been holding companies back in previous years
, during a panel at the London Biotechnology Show, held at the ExCeL London on 18 and 19 November, during London Life Sciences Week, experts agreed that regulators finally seem to have caught up.

Anusha Foy, vice president of regulatory affairs at Allucent, said: “Regulators have to keep up with the pace of development, so they have to constantly ensure they have the expertise. At Allucent, we are conducting a lot of cell and gene therapy (CGT) studies globally, as well as RNA editing and gene editing trials at an early stage, and companies need scientific advice; they need regulators that understand the new modalities, and there’s always the risk-proportionate approach.”

Foy added that the number of avenues available for dialogue with regulators is “encouraging” for companies in this space and believes that they are keeping up with innovation.

Speaking on the same panel, Nicola Kimes, PhD, co-founder and CEO of biotech Siolta Therapeutics, said that the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has been open to their novel therapeutics during the investigational new drug (IND) and clinical trial application (CTA) phases.

“Early communication is so key when you’re talking about a novel modality,” Kimes explained. “Often there is a perception that the regulatory side is a bottleneck to novel modalities, and my experience with the FDA is the exact opposite. It can be challenging and frustrating because you don’t have set guidance that has all of the steps laid out, but the reason there is no guidance like this is that they are trying to be adaptable and open to innovation. They understand that there are nuances in how we need to develop new modalities, and they are looking for programme-specific interactions to help guide that.”

Ruplekha Choudhurie, research director at the Everest Group, holds a slightly different opinion around complex therapeutics regulation. In an interview with 
Clinical Trials Arena
, she said: “Hearing from our clients, we find regulatory complexity is actually increasing, especially when we’re talking about complex modalities. There are no harmonised standards across geographies. For certain modalities it’s not too complex, but for CGTs, RNAs and ADCs, I think the regulatory ecosystem is still evolving, and pharma is still struggling to tackle the lack of standardisation.”

In an interview with 
Clinical Trials Arena
 at the Jeffries Healthcare Conference, Erik Scalfaro, CEO and cofounder of Rivia, an AI-based clinical trial platform that unifies and analyses data, agrees that regulators are successfully keeping up with the fast-paced development in AI and trial development.

Scalfaro said: “One example is the ICH GCP E6 R3 guidelines, which provided clear signals of where the reality of biotech and pharma trials is today. It’s a shift from reactive to proactive monitoring, a risk-based approach to monitoring and other technology considerations.”

Another notable development for Scalfaro is the FDA’s Real-Time Clinical Trials (RTCT) guidance, as well as guidance from the UK’s Medicines and Health Regulatory Agency (MHRA) about advancing trials with technology.

“Regulators are looking at this through a lens of opportunity and optimism,” Scalfaro added. “It doesn’t mean that they’re going to adopt things willy-nilly from one day to the next, but it is extremely encouraging that they are open to it and see it as an opportunity to shift how we oversee trials, increase patient safety, and increase data reliability. Without that, we would be playing defence all the time.”

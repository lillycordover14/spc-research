A new analysis of the nation’s public transit systems finds that people with disabilities continue to face widespread access barriers, but services in some cities are better than others.

Among the 99 fixed-route public transit systems receiving the most federal funding, none are easy to use for people with disabilities, according to researchers at the University of Michigan who created a 
first-of-its-kind dashboard
 evaluating accessibility across the transportation services.

Researchers examined accessibility data for buses, trains and other forms of fixed-route public transit in 85 cities, evaluating affordability, the availability and frequency of services, how well transit systems communicate about accessibility features and how comfortable people with disabilities are with providers.

“For a rider with a disability, one barrier can make the entire trip impossible,” said Erica Twardzik, an assistant professor at the University of Michigan School of Kinesiology and director of the Center for Inclusive Mobility Environments who led the development of the dashboard. “That is why we designed the dashboard to look across multiple parts of the rider’s experience, not just one feature like ramps or elevators.”

Even though it’s been more than three decades since the Americans with Disabilities Act imposed accessibility requirements on transit systems, problems remain pervasive, the dashboard shows.

“None of the transit systems we evaluated rated high on all measures of accessibility,” Twardzik said. “This affects ridership and access for the disability community.”

The best-performing cities included Washington, D.C.; Phoenix; and San Diego, which were all deemed “sufficient.”

The cities that performed the worst were Hampton, Va., Allentown, Pa. and Anchorage, Alaska, the researchers said.

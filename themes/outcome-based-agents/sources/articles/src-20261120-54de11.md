Artificial intelligence systems are now widely used in research settings, primarily to analyze data, make predictions and discover promising materials, drugs or solutions. Most of the agents used for research are disembodied, which means that they are computer-based and require humans or separate automated systems to physically perform experiments.

More recently, some computer scientists have been exploring the possibility of developing embodied AI agents that can both plan and execute scientific experiments. These agents typically combine AI agents with robotic systems that can interact with their surroundings.

Researchers at University College London (UCL), the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) and other institutions recently developed a new agent for planning and conducting science experiments, called embodied science agent (ESA). Presented in a 
paper
 posted to the 
arXiv
 preprint server, ESA was able to autonomously plan and run wastewater treatment experiments involving dye removal.

Most previously developed embodied agents for scientific discovery rely on AI agents to plan experiments and robotic systems to execute them. However, the authors argue that many of them remain limited in how closely they connect the planning of experiments with physical feedback and learned skills enabling the control of scientific instruments.

"By treating models as disembodied planners that output symbolic workflows for separate robotic systems, current architectures inherently block the development of embodied experimental intuition," wrote Yihang Chen, Zhongwei Yu and their colleagues in their paper. "We argue that overcoming this paradox requires embedding learnability along three interdependent axes: cognitive learnability (learning what to investigate), experimental learnability (learning where to search) and embodied learnability (learning how to act)."

An embodied agent that runs experiments

The new agent developed by the researchers has three primary components: a digital agent, an embodied agent and an interface connecting them. The AI agent uses available scientific data and records of earlier experiments to decide what to investigate next.

To select promising experimental conditions, the agent relies on a mathematical approach called Bayesian optimization. The information derived by the AI agent is used to plan the actions of the embodied robotic system adaptively, accounting for differences in the position of instruments or other variations in the experimental environment. Finally, the embodied unified interface connects the disembodied and embodied agents, coordinating the plans produced by the first with the actions performed by the second.

"We introduce the ESA, which unifies digital intelligence with physical embodiment through three synergistic components: a Digital Agent that provides cognitive and experimental learnability via memory-based reasoning and Bayesian optimization, an Embodied Agent that provides embodied learnability via generalizable instrument control, and an Embodied Unified Interface for seamless coordination," wrote the authors.

Chen, Yu and their colleagues tested their system by asking it to autonomously plan and execute wastewater treatment experiments. The agent selected experimental conditions from more than 200 million possible combinations, using each experiment's results to guide subsequent tests.

ESA carried out 15 consecutive rounds of wastewater treatment experimentation. In a separate test, the team assessed whether the embodied system could reliably handle an electrode rack placed in different positions.

"Through 15 continuous rounds, ESA raised the final solution transmittance from 82% to 99.7%, corresponding to a 98.5% reduction in residual dye concentration and near-complete decolorization, while its learned visuomotor policies generalized across varying instrument placements, a promising step toward next-generation SDLs for autonomous scientific discovery," wrote Chen, Yu and their colleagues.

Initial results and future possibilities

The agent developed by the authors was successfully used to plan and carry out wastewater treatment experiments, using earlier results to guide subsequent tests. The researchers found that across the 15 experimental rounds, the agent identified treatment conditions that produced clearer solutions.

The agent also appeared to adapt to changes in its surroundings, successfully performing experiments even when instruments were moved to different positions. Notably, the researchers' tests measured decolorization, which does not by itself establish the complete breakdown of dye or the removal of other pollutants.

Nonetheless, the results of this study demonstrate the possibility of developing more adaptive agents for scientific research, which can both plan and run experiments in laboratory settings. In the future, the team's system could be improved and tested on a wider range of experiments rooted in different scientific disciplines.

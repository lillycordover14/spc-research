Reconsidering the Deficit: Tax Court Withdraws § 743(b) Basis Analysis in Otay Project LP, Leaving Disallowance to Rest on Economic Substance&nbsp;&nbsp;Current Federal Tax Developments

[page text]
Otay Project LP, Oriole Management LLC, Tax Matters Partner v. Commissioner
, T.C. Memo. 2026-120 (
Otay II
)
,
 November 19, 2026

In late February 2026, the United States Tax Court issued a headline-grabbing memorandum decision in 
Otay Project LP, Oriole Management LLC, Tax Matters Partner v. Commissioner
, T.C. Memo. 2026-21 (
Otay I
). The decision sustained the Internal Revenue Service’s disallowance of $713,759,615 of a claimed $743,977,826 basis deduction (the “Basis Deduction”) reported on Otay Project, LP’s (OPLP) final partnership return (Form 1065) for its short taxable year ending October 3, 2012.

The transaction arose from a sprawling real estate development in San Diego County known as Otay Ranch, operated by developer brothers Albert and James Baldwin. Confronting bitter disputes that led to binding arbitration requiring the division of their mutually owned assets, the brothers—guided by major accounting and law firms—embarked upon a complex series of restructuring transactions. OPLP, which deferred hundreds of millions of dollars of land sales income under the completed contract method (CCM) of accounting pursuant to I.R.C. § 460 and Treas. Reg. § 1.460-4(d), transferred promissory notes from affiliated homebuilders to newly formed finance companies (AB Finco and JB Finco), distributed those Finco interests to its 99.9% limited partner, Otay Project, LLC (OPLLC), and left OPLP saddled with substantial, long-term construction obligations. Subsequent multi-tiered estate planning transfers in 2007 triggered a technical termination of OPLP under former I.R.C. § 708(b)(1)(B). Because OPLP had an election under I.R.C. § 754 in effect, the taxpayer claimed a massive positive basis adjustment under I.R.C. § 743(b) of more than $867 million with respect to OPLLC, which was eventually claimed as an offsetting deduction upon OPLP’s final liquidation in 2012.

In 
Otay I
, Judge Weiler rested the court’s decision on two independent grounds:

Mechanical Calculation Error under Subchapter K (Part II.B):
 The court held that OPLP incorrectly determined its basis adjustment under I.R.C. § 743(b) and Treas. Reg. § 1.743-1(d) by failing to account for OPLLC’s reported negative tax capital account of approximately $912 million, concluding that Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(ii)(
b
)(
3
) imposed an unconditional deficit restoration obligation (DRO) on OPLLC that eliminated the basis step-up.  

Economic Substance Doctrine (Part II.C & II.D):
 Alternatively, the court held that the entire restructuring—separating income-producing notes from ongoing construction liabilities—was a tax-driven sham lacking objective economic substance and a bona fide nontax business purpose under the Ninth Circuit’s framework in 
Sochin v. Commissioner
, 843 F.2d 351 (9th Cir. 1988), and 
Reddam v. Commissioner
, 755 F.3d 1051 (9th Cir. 2014).

At the same time, the court fully relieved the partnership of a 40% gross valuation misstatement penalty (I.R.C. § 6662(h)), a 20% substantial valuation misstatement penalty (I.R.C. § 6662(b)(3)), and a 20% negligence penalty (I.R.C. § 6662(b)(1)), finding reasonable cause and good faith under I.R.C. § 6664(c)(1) due to the taxpayer’s reliance on extensive legal and tax opinions from Ernst & Young, LLP, Loeb & Loeb, LLP, and McKee Nelson, LLP.

While the taxpayer was shielded from penalties, the court’s substantive partnership tax analysis in Part II.B ignited immediate concern and debate among Subchapter K practitioners. Specifically, the court appeared to conflate a partner’s 
tax-basis capital account
 with its 
Section 704(b) book capital account
, erroneously concluding that distributions of untaxed cash/notes from deferred completed contracts triggered a mandatory deficit restoration obligation under Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2).

Recognizing this fundamental flaw, petitioner filed a Motion for Reconsideration under Tax Court Rule 161. On November 19, 2026, the Tax Court responded by issuing a Supplemental Memorandum Opinion: 
Otay Project LP, Oriole Management LLC, Tax Matters Partner v. Commissioner
, T.C. Memo. 2026-120 (
Otay II
).

The Motion for Reconsideration and Legal Standards Under Rule 161

Tax Court Rule 161 governs motions for reconsideration of findings or opinion. Under established Tax Court jurisprudence, reconsideration is an extraordinary remedy:

“As applicable here, reconsideration under Rule 161 serves the limited purpose of correcting substantial errors of law or fact. 
Estate of Quick v. Commissioner
, 110 T.C. 440, 441 (1998), 
supplementing
 110 T.C. 172 (1998); 
accord
 Fed. R. Civ. P. 60(b). Reconsideration is not the appropriate forum for rehashing previously rejected legal arguments or tendering new legal theories to reach the result desired by the moving party. 
Estate of Quick
, 110 T.C. at 441–42. Whether to grant a motion for reconsideration rests within the discretion of the Court and generally requires a showing of unusual circumstances or substantial error. 
CWT Farms, Inc. v. Commissioner
, 79 T.C. 1054, 1057 (1982), 
supplementing
 79 T.C. 86 (1982), 
aff’d
, 755 F.2d 790 (11th Cir. 1985).” (
Otay II
, at *3).

In its Motion for Reconsideration, petitioner mounted a laser-focused surgical strike. Petitioner did 
not
 seek reconsideration of the court’s alternative holding that the transactions lacked economic substance (
Otay II
, at *2, *3 n.3). Instead, petitioner directed its challenge exclusively at Part II.B of 
Otay I
, asserting that the court’s basis computation analysis contained clear and substantial errors of law and accounting:

“Specifically, petitioner contends that the figures used in 
Otay I
 do not reflect OPLLC’s section 704(b) book capital account balance, and the Court’s reliance on these figures led it to incorrectly reject OPLP’s section 743(b) Basis Deduction calculation. Moreover, petitioner argues that the Court incorrectly determined that OPLLC had a negative capital account and that OPLLC had an unconditional obligation to restore its negative capital account. 
Otay I
, T.C. Memo. 2026-21, at *30–31.” (
Otay II
, at *3).

The Commissioner opposed the motion procedurally, arguing that because petitioner challenged only one of two independent grounds supporting the disallowance, granting reconsideration would not change the ultimate outcome. Crucially, however, the Commissioner was forced on the merits to make a major substantive concession regarding the partnership rules:

“However, respondent does agree that the Finco distributions, 
see Otay I
, T.C. Memo. 2026-21, at *28, did not result in OPLLC’s having a deficit restoration obligation.” (
Otay II
, at *3).

What Went Wrong in Otay I: Conflating Tax Capital with § 704(b) Book Capital

To appreciate why Judge Weiler took the rare step of granting reconsideration and withdrawing an entire section of his original opinion, practitioners must revisit the court’s rationale in 
Otay I
.

Under I.R.C. § 743(b), when a partnership interest is transferred by sale or exchange (including a deemed exchange resulting from a technical termination under former I.R.C. § 708(b)(1)(B)) and a Section 754 election is in effect, the partnership increases or decreases the adjusted basis of partnership property by the difference between the transferee’s outside basis in its partnership interest and the transferee’s proportionate share of the adjusted basis of partnership property.

Under Treas. Reg. § 1.743-1(d)(1), a transferee’s share of the inside adjusted basis of partnership property equals: 

Transferee’s Share of Inside Basis = Previously Taxed Capital + Share of Partnership Liabilities

Under Treas. Reg. § 1.743-1(d)(2), previously taxed capital is determined through a hypothetical transaction immediately after the transfer: 

Previously Taxed Capital = Cash on Hypothetical Liquidation + Allocable Tax Loss - Allocable Tax Gain

In OPLP’s calculation, immediately after the deemed 2007 technical termination, a hypothetical liquidation at fair market value would have generated $106,608,746 in cash to OPLLC, but would have recognized massive unrealized tax gain—predominantly deferred CCM income—allocating $973,590,432 of net gain to OPLLC. This produced a “previously taxed capital” (and inside basis share) of 
negative $866,981,686
 ($106,608,746 cash minus $973,590,432 gain). Because OPLLC’s outside basis was zero, subtracting negative $866,981,686 produced a positive § 743(b) basis adjustment of $866,981,686: $0 - (-866,981,686) = +866,981,686.

In 
Otay I
, the Tax Court reacted with disbelief to this mechanical result, writing:

“In this case the foregoing calculation resulting in the section 743(b) basis adjustment to OPLLC is illogical. The balance sheet of OPLP, which was used to make these calculations, reflects assets of only $28 million, liabilities of $71 million, and a total negative capital of $848 million. More specifically, OPLLC—the 99.9% partner in OPLP—reflects negative capital of $911,595,423 ($912 million)... OPLLC’s negative capital flies in the face of reality and proper tax accounting.” (
Otay I
, at *28, *30).

Judge Weiler continued:

“Second, this negative capital is fundamentally impossible from a cash perspective, meaning it is ordinarily impossible for any partner to withdraw nearly a billion dollars in capital in excess of the amount of capital that the partner previously contributed to the partnership. It is apparent this discrepancy can only be attributed to the $921 million in deferred profits listed on the CCM Schedules. In other words, for cash (or book) accounting purposes OPLP has distributed deferred gains to its partners, principally to OPLLC. However, for tax reporting purposes no gain has been recognized since OPLP retains its Construction Obligations under CCM.” (
Otay I
, at *30).

The fatal misstep in 
Otay I
 occurred when the court attempted to resolve this perceived anomaly using the “substantial economic effect” safe harbor rules of Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2). The court reasoned that under Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(ii)(
b
)(
3
), if a partner has a deficit balance in its capital account, the partner must be unconditionally obligated to restore that deficit upon liquidation:

“Lastly, and importantly, to achieve a negative capital account of nearly $912 million while maintaining substantial economic effect (as required under the partnership agreement), OPLLC must (1) have an equal unconditional obligation to restore the amount of its deficit capital balance to the partnership and (2) bear an economic burden equal to its previously received economic benefit relating to the partnership for tax purposes. 
See
 Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(ii)(
a
), (
b
)(
3
)... Considering the foregoing, since OPLP has treated the prior distributions of excess capital to OPLLC of $912 million as having substantial economic effect, OPLLC holds an unconditional obligation to restore its negative capital account. Accordingly, the Basis Deduction calculation must account for OPLLC’s negative capital, for the prior partnership distributions to maintain economic effect.” (
Otay I
, at *30–31).

“A correct section 743(b) adjustment must account for OPLLC’s negative capital of $912 million and its unconditional obligation to restore this negative balance through a contribution of additional cash upon liquidation or recognition as a liability. 
See
 I.R.C. § 752; Treas. Reg. §§ 1.743-1(d)(1)(i), 1.704-1(b)(2)(ii)(
b
)(
3
).” (
Otay I
, at *30–31).

Why That Analysis Was Fatally Flawed

Tax professionals immediately recognized the doctrinal error:

The Fundamental Distinction Between Book and Tax Capital:
 The rules of Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(iv) govern 
Section 704(b) book capital accounts
, which reflect the fair market value of partnership property upon contribution or revaluation. They do 
not
 govern tax-basis capital accounts. When Oriole was admitted as a general partner in December 2006, OPLP revalued its assets to fair market value under Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(iv)(
f
), booking up the partners’ capital accounts to reflect hundreds of millions of dollars of unrealized appreciation. Consequently, OPLLC’s 
book
 capital account was distinctly 
positive
.  

Deficit Restoration Obligations Apply Only to Book Deficits:
 Under Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(ii)(
b
)(
3
), an unconditional deficit restoration obligation is required only if a partner has a deficit in its 
book
 capital account maintained in accordance with § 704(b). Negative tax-basis capital accounts are common in partnerships with leveraged distributions, depreciated assets, or deferred income (such as CCM under § 460 or installment sales under § 453). Negative tax capital does 
not
 create a legal liability under state law or a deemed deficit restoration obligation under the tax regulations.  

No DRO Under the Partnership Agreement or State Law:
 Under California limited partnership law and the OPLP agreement, OPLLC, as a limited partner, had no obligation to repay properly made distributions or restore a negative tax capital account. Treating OPLLC as having a fictional $912 million cash restoration liability distorted both I.R.C. § 752 and I.R.C. § 743(b).

The Court’s Correction in Otay II: Complete Withdrawal of Part II.B

In 
Otay II
, Judge Weiler candidly acknowledged the error. After noting the IRS’s concession that the Finco distributions did not create a deficit restoration obligation, the court ruled:

“However, as respondent acknowledges, we have confirmed on reconsideration that OPLLC had a positive 
book
 capital balance and hence it did not have an unconditional obligation to restore its capital account. 
See
 Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(ii)(
b
)(
3
). To correct our prior conclusions as to OPLLC’s unconditional obligation to restore its negative capital account and other related conclusions, we will grant in part petitioner’s Motion for Reconsideration, withdraw Part II.B. of 
Otay I
, T.C. Memo. 2026-21, at *25–31, and refrain from deciding whether (or not) OPLP erred in calculating its section 743(b) basis adjustment and the resulting Basis Deduction, since deciding the issue is not necessary.” (
Otay II
, at *3).

How This Impacts the Prior Basis Computation Analysis

By formally withdrawing Part II.B (*25–31) in its entirety, the Tax Court achieved the following legal and analytical consequences:

Erasure of Flawed Precedent:
 The court’s statements in 
Otay I
 that a § 743(b) adjustment “must account for OPLLC’s negative capital of $912 million and its unconditional obligation to restore this negative balance” are wiped from the record. They can no longer be cited or relied upon as Tax Court authority regarding the mechanics of previously taxed capital or deficit restoration obligations.  

Avoiding the Negative Inside Basis Question:
 The court completely abstains from deciding whether OPLLC’s mechanical calculation under Treas. Reg. § 1.743-1(d) was valid, whether a partner’s share of inside basis can be negative, or whether deferred CCM profits can properly generate a § 743(b) basis step-up on a technical termination. The court explicitly chooses to “refrain from deciding whether (or not) OPLP erred in calculating its section 743(b) basis adjustment.”  

Sole Reliance on Economic Substance:
 The disallowance of the $713,759,615 deduction now rests 
exclusively
 on the common law economic substance doctrine (and the companion subchapter K anti-abuse rule in Treas. Reg. § 1.701-2).

As Judge Weiler emphasized in 
Otay II
:

“However, this change does not alter our analyses and conclusions as to the application of the economic substance doctrine. As we held in 
Otay I
, we continue to conclude that the transactions discussed in 
Otay I
 are shams and lack economic substance. 
Otay I
, T.C. Memo. 2026-21, at *41, *46. Accordingly, and for the reasons detailed in 
Otay I
, T.C. Memo. 2026-21, at *31–41, we continue to sustain respondent’s disallowance of $713,759,615 of a more than $743 million Basis Deduction.” (
Otay II
, at *3–4).

The Surviving Holdings: Economic Substance and Penalty Relief

Because Part II.B was withdrawn while Parts II.C, II.D, and III were reaffirmed, the legal landscape of 
Otay Project LP
 as it stands today is defined by two key determinations:

1. Economic Substance: The Unnatural Divorce of Cash from Obligations

Applying the Ninth Circuit standard (
Reddam
, 
Sochin
), the court scrutinized the objective economic reality and subjective intent behind the restructuring:

Circular Flows and Lack of Risk Shift:
 In 
Otay I
, the court highlighted that cash flows used to retire the Homebuilder Notes and replace them with Intercompany Notes were circular, and the formation of the Finco entities did not meaningfully alter pre-tax profit potential or credit risk.  

Separation of Cash from Contractual Burdens:
 The fatal flaw in the transaction was the deliberate segregation of receivables from liabilities:

“the restructuring purportedly separated OPLP’s CCM cash from its CCM contracts, and the result (according to petitioner) is that the income disappears. This separation is unnatural, from a business standpoint, and the Finco entities were merely vehicles to facilitate indefinite tax deferral, as ultimately Al and Jim, indirectly, continued to receive all payments and have all construction obligations.” (
Otay I
, at *38).

Sham Transaction:
 The court held that while the brothers had a legitimate goal to separate their businesses under the arbitration award, the creation of the Fincos and the transfer of notes while leaving construction liabilities inside OPLP was an engineered tax avoidance device:

“Accordingly, we conclude that the transactions at issue were a tax-driven sham since these transactions primarily resulted in mere tax benefits... when considering the nontax reasons for the transactions at issue, any third-party investor would not benefit from the restructuring and distributions from OPLP.” (
Otay I
, at *41).

2. Total Penalty Relief Under I.R.C. § 6664(c)(1)

Despite characterizing the multi-hundred-million-dollar transaction as a “tax-driven sham” and finding a gross valuation misstatement under I.R.C. § 6662(h), the court completely rejected the IRS’s assertion of penalties:

Competent, Independent Advisors:
 The taxpayers retained prominent, independent advisors (Ernst & Young, Loeb & Loeb, and McKee Nelson) who were not promoters and were unburdened by conflicts of interest (
Oakhill Woods, LLC v. Commissioner
, T.C. Memo. 2020-24; 
Neonatology Assocs., P.A. v. Commissioner
, 115 T.C. 43 (2000)).  

Full Disclosure:
 The Baldwins provided their advisors with full access to their operations, accounting schedules, arbitration orders, and business goals.  

Substantial Authority Opinions:
 Each advisor issued detailed written opinions concluding at a “substantial authority” level that the positions were proper:

“Having reviewed the opinions from EY, Loeb, and McKee Nelson, we are satisfied that each contains substantial authority for each and every conclusion reached therein... The substantial detail found in each opinion runs counter to the notion of negligence; and the fact that Al and Jim obtained three separate opinions only further negates any claims of negligence on their part.” (
Otay I
, at *45, *46).

Strategic and Tactical Lessons for CPAs and EAs

The evolution of 
Otay Project LP
 from February to November 2026 provides invaluable lessons for tax practitioners navigating partnership structuring, IRS examinations, and Tax Court litigation:

1. Master the Division Between § 704(b) Book Capital and Tax Capital

The Tax Court’s initial mistake in 
Otay I
 highlights how easily practitioners and judges can confuse tax basis balances with economic capital.

Practice Rule:
 A negative tax-basis capital account does 
not
 indicate insolvency, an improper distribution, or a deficit restoration obligation.  

Whenever a partnership engages in revaluations (book-ups) under Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(iv)(
f
), book capital accounts reflect current fair market value, while tax accounts continue tracking historical tax attributes.  

Deficit restoration obligations under Treas. Reg. § 1.704-1(b)(2)(ii)(
b
)(
3
) are strictly creatures of the § 704(b) book capital account and state law governing partnership agreements. Tax practitioners must keep these ledger systems strictly segregated in their analysis and documentation.

2. The Tactical Power of Rule 161 Reconsideration in Preserving Appellate Posture

Non-lawyer tax professionals might wonder: 
If the taxpayer still lost the $713 million deduction under economic substance, why bother moving for reconsideration on the § 743(b) basis computation?

Appellate Strategy:
 Under I.R.C. § 7482, an appeal lies in the Ninth Circuit. In 
Otay I
, the taxpayer faced two independent hurdles: reversing the economic substance holding 
and
 overcoming the Tax Court’s determination that its § 743(b) calculation was illegal.  

On appeal, the Ninth Circuit reviews legal interpretations 
de novo
, but factual findings on economic substance under clear error. Had Part II.B remained in the opinion, the Ninth Circuit could have affirmed the disallowance on the statutory § 743(b) ground alone.  

By securing the complete withdrawal of Part II.B, petitioner cleared the underbrush. If the Ninth Circuit reverses or modifies the Tax Court’s economic substance ruling, the IRS will have no standing Tax Court holding invalidating the mechanical basis computation under Treas. Reg. § 1.743-1(d).

3. Mechanical Compliance Cannot Overcome Economic Substance

Subchapter K offers extraordinary flexibility, and taxpayers often attempt to navigate its intricate statutory formulas with mathematical precision. However, 
Otay Project LP
 serves as a stark warning:

The Economic Reality Doctrine:
 You cannot rely solely on the literal words of Treas. Reg. § 1.743-1(d) or former § 708(b)(1)(B) to wipe out hundreds of millions of dollars of deferred income if the underlying structure strips out the income assets (the notes) while leaving the matching business liabilities (the construction obligations) in a shell.  

As the court quoted from 
Gregory v. Helvering
, 293 U.S. 465, 469 (1935), the ultimate inquiry remains: 
“whether what was done, apart from the tax motive, was the thing which the statute intended.”
 Structures designed to manufacture basis deductions through circular entity webs without altering real economic risk will be struck down under common law sham and economic substance doctrines.

4. The Gold Standard for Penalty Protection Under § 6664(c)

In high-stakes tax planning, avoiding penalties is frequently the difference between a manageable dispute and financial catastrophe. 
Otay Project LP
 is a textbook case on how to establish a successful reasonable cause defense:

Independence:
 The advisors were reputable, established firms, not turnkey promoter syndicates selling off-the-shelf tax shelters.  

Timeliness:
 Opinions were sought and obtained contemporaneously with the restructuring and return filings, not after an audit commenced.  

Full Transparency:
 The taxpayer hid nothing from the advisors.  

Depth of Analysis:
 Even though the court ultimately disagreed with the tax positions and labeled the structure a sham, the court acknowledged that the advice was predicated on complex, unsettled provisions of Subchapter K and supported by substantial authority.

Conclusion

The Tax Court’s November 19, 2026 supplemental opinion in 
Otay Project LP
 (
T.C. Memo. 2026-120
) represents a critical course correction in partnership tax jurisprudence. By acknowledging that OPLLC maintained a positive § 704(b) book capital account and withdrawing Part II.B of its original opinion, the court excised a deeply flawed interpretation of deficit restoration obligations and § 743(b) adjustments.

While the bottom-line disallowance of the $714 million Basis Deduction remains intact under the economic substance doctrine, the decision reinforces the vital distinction between book and tax accounting in Subchapter K, illustrates sophisticated appellate tax litigation in action, and underscores the enduring protective power of independent professional advice.

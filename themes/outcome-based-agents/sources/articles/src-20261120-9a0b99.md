Jack Johnson 
has an amazing life. To be raised in a world of surfing, having such poetic songwriting capability, a humble soul, and to be able to craft music that sells out arenas. I genuinely believe he used the 
New Zealand 
tour stop to have a dip in our waters. 

There was something rather special about this lineup for 
SURFFILMUSIC 
tour. Three artists, each with their own distinct sound, brought together by a shared love of songwriting, acoustic music and the sort of musicianship that doesn’t need much dressing up. 
John Butler
, 
Ben Harper
 and 
Jack Johnson
 made for a seriously good bill. No need for enormous theatrics or unnecessary spectacle. Just good songs, good musicians and a room full of people who seemed very happy to be there.

John Butler

Opening the evening was 
John Butler
, whose music has always carried a certain earthy warmth. His guitar playing is instantly recognisable, with that intricate fingerpicking style and roots-driven sound that has made him such a fixture of Australian music.

There is something wonderfully unpretentious about watching Butler perform. He doesn’t need a wall of musicians behind him or a complicated stage production to hold an audience. Give him a guitar, a microphone and a little room to breathe, and he’s away.

His set brought that familiar mixture of folk, blues and roots music, with songs that have become firm favourites among fans. 
Better Than
, 
Betterman
 and 
Wade In The Water
 were particularly welcome. There’s a reason these songs have stuck around. They’re beautifully written, emotionally direct and have just enough grit to stop them becoming too polished.

There’s a real physicality to Butler’s playing, as though every note is being pulled from the strings rather than simply played. His performance was a lovely way to ease into the evening. Rather than trying to whip the crowd into a frenzy, he let the music do the talking.

It was a reminder that sometimes the best way to start a concert is to simply let a good guitarist play.

Ben Harper

Then came 
Ben Harper
, another artist whose music seems to exist somewhere between blues, folk, soul and rock. He’s one of those musicians who can make a guitar sound like an entire conversation, and his voice has that beautifully weathered quality that gives even the simplest lyric a little extra weight.

Where Butler brings an earthy, rootsy energy, Harper has a slightly more soulful presence. His playing feels steeped in blues tradition, but there’s always something distinctly his own about it.

She’s Only Happy In The Sun
, 
By My Side
 and 
Please Bleed
 brought a lovely introduction to his set, before he moved into the more familiar territory of 
Excuse Me Mister
. The latter allowed Harper’s guitar work and vocal delivery to stretch their legs.

One of the more interesting moments came with his acoustic, lap-style slide guitar take on 
Bruce Springsteen’s
 
Dancing In The Dark
. Rather than simply recreating the original, Harper gave it a bluesier character, stripping it back and letting the melody breathe.

His rendition of 
Hallelujah 
becomes my new favourite cover of the song (sorry 
Jeff Buckley
). He sings and plays it with extreme precision in his vocals. Every high note you knew it was considered so thoughtfully leading up to the show. 

By the time Harper finished, the crowd had been treated to two very different performances, yet both seemed to fit together perfectly. And there was still one more artist to come.

Jack Johnson

There’s a particular kind of magic to 
Jack Johnson
. He isn’t an artist who needs to convince you he’s a rock star. In fact, he seems rather happy doing the opposite.

With his relaxed manner, easy smile and familiar acoustic sound, Johnson has built a career around making music that feels like home. His songs don’t demand your attention so much as invite you into their little world. They’re warm, uncomplicated and wonderfully human.

And perhaps that’s why they mean so much to so many people.

Johnson took the stage with his longtime band, including 
Adam Topol
 on drums, 
Zach Gill
 on keys and an assortment of other instruments, and 
Merlo Podlewski
 on bass. The sound was fuller than the laid-back acoustic image might suggest, but it never lost that easygoing charm.

He was sporting a 
Raglan Surf Resort
 shirt, which seemed entirely appropriate for the occasion. At one point, he explained his slightly sunburnt appearance to the crowd, giving everyone a glimpse into the sort of surf-filled life that has always been part of his identity. It’s hard to imagine a more fitting look for a man whose music practically smells of salt water and sunscreen.

The songs came with stories, too. In fact, nearly every track seemed to have a little tale attached to it. Johnson spoke about writing songs while out on boats, sharing the sort of details that make you appreciate just how much of his life finds its way into his music. He has a lovely way of telling stories. You start listening to an anecdote, and before you know it, you’re completely immersed in it.

On And On
 was one such moment. While Johnson played, 
Zach Gill
 provided one of the evening’s more amusing sights, playing the piano with one hand while casually sipping tea from a ceramic mug with the other. It’s not every day you see a musician multitasking quite so comfortably. Somehow, it was perfectly in keeping with the atmosphere.

Inaudible Melodies
 followed, introduced with a story about martial arts legend 
Bruce Lee
. Johnson explained how Lee’s movements were so incredibly fast that the film director struggled to capture them on camera. It was a wonderfully unexpected detour, but that’s what made the stories so enjoyable. Johnson wasn’t just introducing songs. He was giving the audience little windows into the thoughts, experiences and odd observations that helped shape them. Who knew 
Bruce Lee 
was the inspiration to the lyric: s
low down everyone You’re moving too fast Frames can’t catch you when You’re moving like that.

Johnson also brought up 
September Sessions
, his surf film from years ago. For anyone unfamiliar with the genre, think of an 
A24’s 
production 
mid-90s 
surf film, only made a couple of decades later: a collection of surfers, waves and beautifully captured moments from the water. Johnson explained how the film works alongside a live band, giving the audience a sense of the creative world that has always surrounded his music.

Banana Pancakes
 was another highlight. Teardrops played across the screen, adding a visual layer to the performance, while Gill once again found a way to make himself the centre of attention. Out came the piano and what looked like a squeezebox, before he wandered across the speakers while playing. It was wonderfully ridiculous, and all the better for it.

Tape Deck
 was another standout, with its infectious groove and familiar Johnson charm. There’s a lovely balance in his music between laid-back simplicity and clever musical detail. The songs sound effortless, but the band behind him knows exactly what it’s doing.

At around the one-hour mark, things became even more interesting when 
John Butler
 returned to the stage. The pair launched into 
Trippin’ On You
, before moving into 
Breakdown
, with Johnson picking up a ukulele alongside Butler. It was a natural pairing, and their musical chemistry made the collaboration feel less like a planned guest appearance and more like two mates enjoying a song together.

Then came 
Flake
, with 
Ben Harper
 joining in. Harper’s slide guitar brought a bluesy edge to the track, before the two moved into 
The Wailers’
 
High Tide Or Low Tide
. It was a beautiful combination of voices and instruments, and one of those moments where the three artists’ different styles seemed to meet in exactly the right place.

But Johnson wasn’t finished inviting people into the evening.

At one point, he asked the audience what they’d like to hear next. Requests started coming in, including one from someone who apparently wanted to get a tattoo. You never quite know what you’re going to get when an artist opens the floor to requests, but Johnson made the whole thing feel like a casual conversation rather than a formal setlist.

Then came one of the night’s most unexpected moments.

A man from the crowd, a Dunedin local named 
Rylan
, made his way onto the stage to perform a song with Johnson. Initially, it looked as though the whole thing might have been arranged beforehand. Johnson seemed familiar with him and his covers, which made it easy to assume this was a carefully planned surprise.

But no. It was a genuine coincidence.

Rylen hopped over the barricade from the crowd and the pair performed 
Hold On To The Light
, and the moment had a lovely spontaneity to it. There was something particularly refreshing about seeing a fan go from watching his favourite artist in the crowd to sharing the stage with him. No elaborate production, no manufactured viral moment. Just a bloke, two guitars and a song.

Another request followed, this time for 
Washing Dishes
, before Harper and Butler returned to the stage. With all three musicians together again, they played 
Constellations
, bringing another wonderful collaborative moment to the evening.

And then, fittingly, came 
Better Together
.

There was no need for a grand finale involving fireworks or enormous production. The music was enough.

Walking away from Spark Arena, I was left thinking about just how much music can hold. A song can be a memory, a place, a person or an entire period of your life. Sometimes you don’t realise how much it means until you hear it again, years later, sung by the person who wrote it.

Jack Johnson
 has spent more than two decades making music that feels timeless without ever trying too hard to be. His songs are simple, but simple doesn’t mean shallow. There’s a real gift in making music that people can carry with them through different stages of life.

You might have first heard him through 
Curious George
. You might have discovered him on a summer road trip, or found his records sitting in an old CD collection. Perhaps his music has followed you through relationships, family life and all the little changes that come with getting older.

Whatever the connection, Friday night offered a chance to revisit it.

And with 
Ben Harper
 and 
John Butler
 alongside him, the evening became something even more special. Three remarkable songwriters, sharing a stage, trading musical ideas and reminding everyone why live music matters.

A night of familiar songs, old memories and new moments worth keeping. Like a shoebox of photographs, full of sepia-toned memories and little reminders of who we were, the music brought the past into the present without ever feeling stuck there.

Some things really are better together.

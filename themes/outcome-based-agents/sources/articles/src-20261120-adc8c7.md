A new Sierra whitepaper on consumer choice, the digital front door, and the compounding value of context.
Personal agents have been in consumers’ hands for only a few months, and adoption is increasing fast. They are top of mind for the hundreds of large enterprises we talk to each week at Sierra.
We founded Sierra with the belief that AI agents would become the digital front door for almost every business. Personal agents add a new dimension: some consumers will come directly, and others via their agents. That can mean new customers and transactions that would otherwise never have happened. It also raises hard questions about disintermediation, business models, security, and authorization.

A familiar choice
Meta reported
 that more than 10 million people in the US and Canada set up Muse in its first six weeks. New distribution has always brought both opportunity and the risk of disintermediation, from search engines to app stores. Personal agents can go further: choose a product, complete a purchase, or switch a provider on the customer’s behalf. If customers send agents, do companies become interchangeable? 
Some companies will block them
. Others will engage, knowing that if they decline to support something customers want, a competitor usually will.
The economics of “getting around to it”
Much of commerce today reflects the gap between what people intend to do and what they get around to. As 
Sarah Guo
 puts it, “Somewhere, a CRM still records your exhaustion as loyal recurring revenue.” Personal agents will likely reduce that inertia. They can turn existing demand into completed purchases and appointments, and they can also put pressure on revenue that depends on customers not reviewing their existing arrangements. And because agents research far more thoroughly than people do, they could favor products that are better but less well known, and weaken the advantage of companies that rely on familiarity.
Rent the intelligence, own the context
In a world where every company has access to the same intelligence, durable advantage comes from context. The personal agent knows the person’s preferences, schedule, and what they are trying to achieve. The company knows the account: what the customer owns, what they are entitled to, and what the company can offer or do. And the company is the expert in its own domain, which no general-purpose personal agent can match. Each interaction makes the next one better, so companies can compete on value rather than on the economics of getting around to it. The best outcomes will likely happen when both sides meet, and that requires a capable agent on the company’s side.
It also requires control. Companies need a way to decide which personal agents can engage and what they can do, with clear identity, authorization, and approval. 
Poppy, the Personal Agent Protocol
, is designed to meet these needs.
The practical step
Nobody knows how personal agents will develop. The practical step is the same whichever way things go: build an agent as your digital front door, make what sets you apart easy for agents to find, use every interaction to deepen your understanding of your customers, and decide when and how personal agents can engage with your business.

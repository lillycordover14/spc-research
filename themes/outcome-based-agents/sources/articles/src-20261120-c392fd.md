At the dawn of the 
1970s
, Laurel Canyon served as one of the most influential neighbourhoods in music. 
This small, tree-lined pocket of the Hollywood Hills played 
host to a community of true greats,
 who really epitomised the barefoot thinking of this idealised time; there was nothing else to do but write songs, smoke weed and make love to pass the time. In this hubbub of culture were Cass Elliot of The Mamas & the Papas, Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young, Joni Mitchell, Jackson Browne, Carole King, James Taylor and many, many more icons of the golden age. 
Far enough to feel isolated, yet close enough to the heartbeat of modern culture, Laurel Canyon became the place where folk, rock, and pop merged into a defining sound, and crucial musical partnerships were formed. In fact, if it wasn’t for the open-door policy of the residents, we would have never had the music of Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young delivered to us.
They were a central part of this vibrant community, along with Mitchell, who, at the time, was in a 
relationship with Graham Nash.
 Together, the pair formed something of a central axis for the group, epitomising the peaceful relationships their community hoped to foster, along with a partnership that encouraged some of the greatest songwriting in history.
Ultimately, it was all about bottling up that feeling and putting it into song, giving the wider world a window into the serene outlook of the Laurel Canyon lifestyle. And the neighbourhood’s crowning pair did just that, on Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young’s ‘Our House’, Nash’s ode to the peaceful domesticity he and his lover had built. 
“It’s about a beautiful moment between a man and a woman,” he explained, confirming that the simple celebration of their house was indeed the source of inspiration. But he continued, adding context about how the safety of their liberal bubble meant even more, given the swirling social turmoil that existed outside the sunny hills of California: “It’s about peace. Even though the times were turbulent, particularly with the Vietnam War and Civil Rights, two and a half minutes of peace was what we all needed.”
Laurel Canyon represented the rosiest of retrospective views. That part of the late ‘60s and early ‘70s that we have since eulogised as the golden age of culture for how it fostered the sort of free spirit the era was famed for. And ‘Our House’ is the sound of that, along with every other album born from the commune.

But Nash was right to remind listeners that the music still served as something of an idealistic bubble. The joy of Laurel Canyon wasn’t a homogenised attitude, especially when socio-economic injustice was at its most rife, 
the war in Vietnam its most bloody
 and the disparity between civil liberties the widest. 
As the ‘70s rolled on, that reality became ever clearer, and the sunny disposition of Laurel Canyon was soon overcast by a dark cloud of social injustice that, in turn, waved goodbye to blissful folk-rock and instead welcomed in the grit of punk rock.

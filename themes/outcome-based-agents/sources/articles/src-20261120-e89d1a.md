Abstract
Intelligent cleaning of chemical reaction vessels is an inevitable trend in equipment operation and maintenance, and its implementation highly depends on high-precision perception of the residual amount and spatial distribution of pharmaceutical deposits on vessel inner walls. However, conventional inspection methods suffer from three major limitations: high labor intensity, low efficiency, and weak morphology reconstruction capability. Therefore, these methods are insufficient for supporting refined, demand-driven, and quantitative cleaning strategies. Although point cloud deep learning has been widely applied to 3D semantic segmentation, specialized networks for reactor inner-wall pharmaceutical deposit detection remain limited. Existing general-purpose models are poorly adapted to the small scale, weak boundaries, and irregular adhesion patterns of deposits, resulting in insufficient local detail representation and incomplete boundary delineation. To address these problems, this study proposes LAR-Net, a lightweight adaptive receptive field network for point cloud segmentation of pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls. Built upon the lightweight hybrid backbone LitePT, LAR-Net dynamically adjusts the receptive field according to local geometric complexity, models local inter-voxel relationships through Adaptive Relation Convolution (AR-Conv), and recalibrates channel features within local patches to enhance the feature representation of small-scale deposits. A reactor inner-wall pharmaceutical deposit point cloud dataset was constructed for model evaluation. On the test set, LAR-Net achieved an mIoU of 0.8326, an mAcc of 0.8827, a deposit-class IoU of 0.6781, and a deposit-class accuracy of 0.7713, outperforming LitePT by 1.11, 1.49, 2.18, and 2.99 percentage points, respectively. Furthermore, LAR-Net achieves a favorable balance among segmentation accuracy, boundary preservation, geometric consistency, and computational efficiency, demonstrating improved capability for fine-grained pharmaceutical deposit segmentation and providing support for automated deposit detection and intelligent cleaning of chemical reactors.

Introduction
Basic chemicals and functional materials provided by the chemical industry are widely used in new energy, pharmaceuticals, electronic information, aerospace, and other fields. Among them, reactors are one of the core pieces of equipment in chemical production processes, and their operating conditions are directly related to reaction efficiency, product quality, and production safety. During long-term operation, pharmaceutical deposits can accumulate to varying degrees on reactor inner walls. The formation of such deposits not only reduces heat-transfer efficiency and disrupts the uniformity of the reaction process, but may also cause local blockage, intensified corrosion, and degradation of equipment performance. In severe cases, it may even lead to shutdown maintenance and considerable economic losses. Therefore, the detection and segmentation of pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls have become an essential aspect of intelligent equipment maintenance and industrial process monitoring.
However, the detection of pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls is not limited to identifying the existence of defects. It should also determine the distribution location, thickness variation, and adhesion morphology of deposits through semantic segmentation. Such information can be used to formulate differentiated and precise cleaning strategies according to the structural characteristics of different regions, thereby promoting the development of intelligent cleaning. Without accurate deposit segmentation and spatial localization, cleaning operations still rely heavily on manual experience or global cleaning strategies, which may lead to insufficient cleaning in heavily deposited regions or excessive cleaning in lightly contaminated areas. In existing industrial practice, reactor inner-wall inspection mainly relies on manual observation, endoscopy, or conventional image acquisition. These methods are labor-intensive, inefficient, and difficult to use for stable quantitative analysis of irregular pharmaceutical deposits
1
. To address these problems, researchers have attempted to develop automatic detection methods for analyzing closed-circuit television images and videos. For example, in pharmaceutical deposits-related inspection tasks, Myrans et al.
2
 used random forests to classify abnormal frames in videos, whereas Yang and Su
3
 employed back-propagation neural networks, radial basis function networks, and support vector machines for defect classification. With the rapid development of deep learning in image recognition
4
,
5
,
6
, convolutional neural networks, residual networks, and YOLO-based detectors have also been widely introduced into industrial inspection tasks.
Despite the progress of two-dimensional visual inspection, image-based methods still suffer from inherent limitations. Because they mainly rely on two-dimensional appearance information, their performance is vulnerable to illumination variation, viewpoint distortion, occlusion, surface reflection, and texture degradation. These problems become more pronounced in endoscopic or confined-space inspection scenarios, where captured images often exhibit low signal-to-noise ratios, geometric distortion, and scale inconsistency. More importantly, two-dimensional images cannot reliably provide complete three-dimensional information about deposit thickness, spatial location, surface morphology, and adhesion range. As a result, image-based methods may fail to accurately recover the three-dimensional morphology, spatial distribution, and boundary structure of pharmaceutical deposits, thereby limiting their effectiveness in residual deposit quantification and fine-grained semantic segmentation for intelligent cleaning.
With the development of three-dimensional sensors such as 3D scanners, LiDAR, and depth cameras, point cloud-based 3D detection technology has been widely applied in fields such as 3D reconstruction, scene understanding, semantic segmentation, and industrial defect detection
7
. Compared with two-dimensional images, three-dimensional point clouds can directly acquire the spatial structure, surface morphology, and depth information of targets. They offer advantages such as non-contact measurement, complete geometric information, and reduced sensitivity to illumination, making them more suitable for industrial inspection tasks involving complex curved surfaces, confined spaces, and low-light environments. For reactor inner-wall deposit inspection, point clouds can provide explicit geometric and spatial cues for identifying deposit locations, estimating residual coverage, analyzing adhesion morphology, and supporting subsequent cleaning decisions. In industrial applications, related studies have applied point cloud analysis methods to the semantic segmentation and modeling of industrial components such as pipelines, beams, and columns
8
. Other studies have achieved state representation of structural pipelines and large-scale equipment through high-precision 3D reconstruction
9
. These studies indicate that 3D point cloud technology has considerable application potential in industrial component recognition, structural modeling, and defect perception.
However, most existing studies focus on regular industrial components or overall structural modeling tasks, with emphasis on geometric reconstruction accuracy, structural parameter extraction, and large-scale scene understanding. As shown in Fig. 
1
, pharmaceutical deposits attached to reactor inner walls are characterized by local adhesion, irregular morphology, complex boundaries, and pronounced scale variations. Existing methods still lack targeted research on fine-grained segmentation for such targets. Therefore, for reactor inner-wall pharmaceutical deposit segmentation, the core challenge lies in simultaneously understanding the global reactor-wall structure and preserving local deposit details in complex industrial curved-surface point clouds. Accurate segmentation of such deposits can further provide important information for residual deposit assessment, cleaning-region selection, and differentiated precision cleaning, thereby supporting the intelligent maintenance of chemical equipment.

To improve the adaptability of point cloud networks to spatial structural variations, OA-CNNs

However, OA-CNNs are mainly designed for general 3D semantic segmentation tasks, such as buildings, vehicles, and roads. Its primary focus is on addressing object-scale variations and scene structural differences, rather than the specific characteristics of industrial deposit segmentation, including small-scale targets, weak boundaries, irregular shapes, and severe foreground-background imbalance. Furthermore, OA-CNNs rely on relatively complex multi-scale feature aggregation and sparse convolution operations, and their higher model complexity and computational cost limit lightweight deployment in industrial inspection scenarios. LitePT
14
 adopts a hybrid modeling strategy combining convolution and attention mechanisms. Specifically, convolution is utilized in shallow layers to extract local geometric features, while attention mechanisms are employed in deeper layers to enhance global semantic relationships, achieving a good balance among local structural representation, global context modeling, and computational efficiency. Therefore, LitePT is selected as the backbone network in this study. However, LitePT still has certain limitations for this task. For reactor inner-wall scenes, where smooth wall regions and irregular deposit regions coexist within the same point cloud, the above design remains insufficient. Specifically, large-scale reactor-wall regions require broader contextual information to maintain segmentation consistency, whereas small deposits with weak boundaries and fragmented structures require fine-grained local geometric modeling capability. In addition, the wall background occupies a dominant proportion while deposit regions are sparse, resulting in severe class imbalance between the two categories. The global feature aggregation strategy may weaken the discriminative responses of small deposit regions, leading to blurred boundaries, local missed detections, and incomplete segmentation results. Therefore, a task-oriented lightweight framework is required to simultaneously achieve adaptive spatial perception, local relation feature modeling, and deposit-oriented feature enhancement.
To address these limitations, this study proposes LAR-Net, a lightweight adaptive receptive field network based on LitePT for point cloud segmentation of pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls. The proposed network aims to enhance the representation capability of small-scale, weak-boundary, and irregular deposits through adaptive local geometric modeling while maintaining computational efficiency. Experimental results demonstrate that LAR-Net achieves improved segmentation performance in terms of mIoU and deposit-class IoU, together with enhanced boundary preservation and geometric consistency. Furthermore, LAR-Net achieves a favorable balance between accuracy and computational cost, with only 14.79M parameters and 39.52 ms inference latency, demonstrating its potential for industrial inspection and intelligent reactor cleaning.
The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:
To address the coexistence of large-scale smooth wall structures and locally complex deposit regions on reactor inner walls, this study designs a deposit-aware spatial adaptive receptive field module. The proposed module dynamically adjusts the feature aggregation range according to local geometric complexity, thereby maintaining the overall structural consistency of the reactor while enhancing the representation capability of local deposit details.
The Adaptive Relation Convolution (AR-Conv) is further improved by introducing centroid-based local relation modeling. Specifically, it constructs relation representations by learning the feature differences between non-empty voxels and their local geometric centers. This design emphasizes geometric variations within local regions, and the resulting center-offset geometric relationships are more suitable for pharmaceutical deposit segmentation tasks involving thin-layer attachment, weak boundaries, and irregular shapes. It can also effectively characterize the differences in local relationships between deposit regions and large-scale reactor-wall backgrounds.
Considering the sparse distribution, small-scale characteristics, and local fragmentation of deposit regions, this study proposes a Patch-wise Channel Attention module. The module adaptively recalibrates channel features within local patches, enhancing discriminative features related to deposits while suppressing interference from large-scale background wall regions.
Based on the above designs, this study constructs a lightweight point cloud segmentation network, LAR-Net, for the segmentation of residual pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls. While maintaining low model complexity, LAR-Net achieves a balance between global structural consistency and local deposit detail representation capability. The proposed method is validated on a self-constructed pharmaceutical deposit point cloud dataset of reactor inner walls, demonstrating its effectiveness and practical application potential in automated deposit detection and intelligent cleaning of chemical reactors. Related work
As an important data representation for describing the spatial relationships and geometric morphology of real-world objects, three-dimensional point clouds have been widely used in various industrial fields in recent years. With the development of deep learning, point-cloud-based recognition and analysis methods have shown clear advantages in processing large-scale data, improving detection accuracy, and overcoming the limitations of conventional visual inspection. However, point clouds are inherently unordered, unstructured, and sparsely distributed. Designing efficient and robust point cloud processing methods therefore remains an important research problem in 3D vision
15
. This section reviews the development of deep learning methods for 3D point clouds, with a particular focus on semantic segmentation, and further discusses the limitations of existing approaches.
Existing deep learning methods for 3D point clouds can be broadly divided into three categories: projection-based methods, voxel-based methods, and point-based methods. Projection-based and voxel-based methods share a common idea: they regularize unstructured 3D data before feature learning. Specifically, projection-based methods project point clouds onto two-dimensional planes from multiple views
16
,
17
, whereas voxel-based methods embed point clouds into regular voxel grids
18
,
19
. These representations allow the use of 2D or 3D convolutional operators. However, such regularization inevitably causes information loss. In addition, because convolutional kernels are constrained by fixed grid resolutions and kernel sizes, these methods often have limited scalability when processing complex or large-scale point clouds
20
.
Point-based methods directly process raw point clouds and have gradually become the dominant technical route in 3D point cloud analysis. PointNet, proposed by Qi et al. in 2017, is regarded as one of the pioneering works in point cloud deep learning. By introducing symmetric functions, PointNet enabled direct modeling of unordered point sets for the first time, effectively avoiding the information loss caused by regularized point cloud representations. This work laid the foundation for subsequent point cloud analysis methods. Building upon PointNet, PointNet++ further proposed a general hierarchical encoder–decoder backbone. It progressively downsamples point clouds, learns multi-scale hierarchical features within local neighborhoods, and restores point-wise semantic information during decoding through feature propagation and upsampling. In this way, each point can obtain richer semantic features for segmentation. Because PointNet++ established a relatively complete framework for local feature extraction and point-wise semantic recovery, many subsequent point cloud analysis methods have extended and improved its backbone structure
21
. The main research focus has shifted toward more efficient and accurate modeling of point cloud features.Some studies represent point clouds as graph structures
22
,
23
, propagating and aggregating features over local neighborhood graphs to strengthen spatial relationship modeling among points. Other studies have designed advanced continuous convolution operators tailored to 3D point clouds
24
. For example, PointConv
25
 formulates convolution kernels as nonlinear functions of local 3D coordinates and combines weight functions with density functions to effectively compute convolution weights.
In recent years, the success of attention mechanisms in computer vision has inspired the application of Transformer models to point cloud analysis. Compared with traditional local neighborhood aggregation methods, Transformer-based methods can more effectively model long-range dependencies between points, thereby enhancing the representation of global contextual information. Representative methods include Point Transformer, Fast Point Transformer
26
, Point cloud transformer, and PointASNL
27
. However, the stronger global modeling capability of Transformer-based methods usually comes with higher computational complexity and memory consumption. In large-scale point cloud scenarios, the computational cost of self-attention increases significantly with the number of points, limiting its application in high-resolution scenes and real-time industrial tasks. Moreover, although Transformers are effective for global dependency modeling, efficient extraction of local geometric details still requires integration with convolutional operators or local neighborhood aggregation mechanisms.
To balance local geometric modeling and global semantic relationship representation, researchers have further proposed hybrid network architectures that combine convolution and attention mechanisms. Among them, LitePT adopts convolution in shallow layers to extract local geometric features and introduces attention mechanisms in deeper layers to model global semantic relationships. This design achieves a favorable balance between accuracy and efficiency, making LitePT one of the representative lightweight networks for point cloud semantic segmentation. Such methods indicate that the collaborative design of convolution and attention can, to a certain extent, meet the dual requirements of local feature representation and global context modeling.
Method
Overall architecture of LAR-Net
The overall architecture of the proposed LAR-Net is illustrated in Fig. 
3
. LAR-Net was developed based on the lightweight hybrid backbone LitePT and followed a U-Net-like encoder–decoder structure. The network retained the efficient combination of convolution and attention in LitePT, while introducing task-specific adaptive modules to improve the segmentation of pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls. This design was motivated by the main characteristics of the target scene: large smooth reactor-wall regions require stable global representation, whereas pharmaceutical deposits usually appear as small, irregular, weak-boundary, and locally fragmented structures that require fine-grained geometric modeling.
Figure 3
Full size image
Overall framework of LAR-Net.
In the encoder, shallow stages mainly used sparse convolution to extract local geometric features. However, standard sparse convolution has a fixed receptive field and limited adaptability to different local structures. Therefore, the spatial adaptive receptive field module was embedded into the convolutional stages to dynamically adjust the receptive field size according to local geometric complexity. This allowed the network to aggregate broader contextual information in flat reactor-wall regions while preserving detailed responses around deposit boundaries, protrusions, and thin-layer adhesion areas.
To further strengthen local structure modeling, adaptive relational convolution was introduced into the convolutional blocks. Unlike conventional sparse convolution with fixed kernel weights, AR-Conv generated dynamic convolution weights based on voxel–centroid feature differences within local voxel grids. This mechanism enabled lightweight local inter-voxel relationship modeling and improved the representation of irregular adhesion regions and boundary transition areas without directly introducing expensive self-attention into shallow layers.
In the deeper stages, the attention mechanism inherited from LitePT was retained to capture long-range semantic dependencies and global contextual information. This complemented the shallow convolutional modules by providing a broader semantic understanding of the overall reactor-wall scene. In addition, the patch-wise channel attention module was used to recalibrate channel features within local patches. Compared with global channel attention, this local patch-based design reduced the dilution of small deposit features by the dominant reactor-wall background and enhanced the discriminative representation of small-scale deposit regions.
In the decoder, multi-scale encoder features were transferred through skip connections and progressively fused with upsampled features to recover point-wise spatial resolution. The network finally produced semantic predictions for the reactor-wall background regions and the pharmaceutical deposit regions.
In general, LAR-Net integrated spatial receptive field adaptation, dynamic local relationship modeling, and patch-wise channel feature enhancement into a lightweight LitePT-based framework. This architecture preserved the efficiency of LitePT while improving the network’s ability to represent both global reactor-wall structures and local pharmaceutical deposit details. Therefore, it provides a more suitable segmentation framework for complex industrial curved-surface point clouds with small, sparse, and irregular deposit targets.
Spatial adaptive receptive field
In three-dimensional reactor scenes, reactor-wall regions are usually flat and occupy a large spatial extent. These regions require a larger receptive field to obtain consistent and stable predictions. In contrast, pharmaceutical deposit regions often exhibit complex spatial morphology and fragmented boundaries, requiring a smaller receptive field to preserve local geometric details. Although Transformers can dynamically aggregate contextual information from different spatial ranges through attention mechanisms, the sparse convolution used in the shallow layers of LitePT lacks such adaptive perception capability. Therefore, inspired by the concept of the OA-CNNs design, this study introduced a spatial adaptive receptive field mechanism into the convolutional stage, allowing the network to automatically select a more suitable perceptual scale according to the features of the voxel. Unlike OA-CNNs, the spatial adaptive receptive field convolution module proposed in this study does not determine the receptive field size according to the scale of target objects, but dynamically adjusts the receptive range based on the geometric complexity of local regions. OA-CNNs mainly focus on addressing object scale variations in general 3D scenes, where receptive field adaptation considers the spatial scale differences among different objects. For example, objects from different categories may occupy different spatial extents and therefore require contextual information from different receptive ranges for feature modeling. Consequently, the receptive field size is generally related to the scale of the target object.However, in the pharmaceutical deposit segmentation task on reactor inner walls, the receptive field requirement is not simply determined by the size of the target region, but is more strongly influenced by local geometric variations. For instance, large-scale reactor-wall regions usually exhibit continuous and smooth geometric characteristics, requiring a larger receptive field to maintain overall structural consistency. In contrast, pharmaceutical deposit regions contain complex structures such as thin-layer attachments, local protrusions, and fragmented boundaries, which require more precise local perception capabilities. Therefore, this study dynamically adjusts the receptive field according to local geometric complexity, enabling the model to adapt to different geometric characteristics within the same scene.
Voxel grid partitioning
To achieve adaptive perception for point clouds of three-dimensional reactor scenes, this study first addressed the receptive field bottleneck of standard 3

\(\times \)
 3 submanifold sparse convolution. Directly increasing the convolution kernel size would substantially increase the computational cost and parameter burden, making it difficult to simultaneously maintain a lightweight design and a large receptive field. Therefore, a sparse voxel grid partitioning mechanism was introduced to efficiently enlarge the receptive field without increasing the number of convolution kernel parameters.
Formally, let 
\(\mathcal {V}=(\mathcal {P},\mathcal {F})\)
 denote the sparse voxelized representation of a 3D scene, which consists of a set of voxels 
\(v_i=(\varvec{p}_i,\varvec{f}_i)\)
. Here, 
\(\varvec{p}_i\in \mathbb {R}^3\)
 represents the integer voxel coordinate, and 
\(\varvec{f}_i\in \mathbb {R}^d\)
 denotes the corresponding 
d
-dimensional feature vector. The global voxel set 
\(\mathcal {V}\)
 is partitioned into 
N

$$ \left[ \mathcal {V}_1,\mathcal {V}_2,\dots ,\mathcal {V}_N\right] , $$
where each local voxel block is defined as
$$ \mathcal {V}_i=\{v_j \mid \varvec{p}_j\in \Omega (i)\}. $$
here, 
\(\mathcal {V}_i\)
 denotes the 
i
-th voxel block, and 
\(\Omega (i)\)
 represents the spatial index range corresponding to each block. Since the spatial coverage of a voxel block is significantly larger than that of conventional 3D convolution kernels, the proposed voxel partitioning mechanism efficiently expands the network receptive field.
Based on this voxel grid mechanism, the proposed network further incorporated the hierarchical downsampling structure of the encoder and designed a hierarchically adaptive pyramid voxel grid. Specifically, multi-scale grid sizes were assigned to different spatial resolutions in the encoder convolutional stages. As the feature resolution decreased, the grid size was progressively reduced, forming a nested design that combined a macro-scale feature pyramid with a micro-scale voxel grid pyramid. This design ensured that lightweight large-receptive-field feature extraction could be achieved through voxel grids at different spatial scales. Adaptive receptive field construction
Although sufficiently large voxel grids can provide a broader contextual view, they fail to dynamically select the optimal receptive field matching the geometric structure of each individual voxel. Therefore, inspired by the adaptive aggregator module in OA-CNNS, this work realizes autonomous weighted fusion for multi-scale voxel-grid features. Specifically, as illustrated in Fig. 
5
, the network first employs a learnable linear layer 
\(\delta _{\text {adp}}\)
 to predict the preference weights of each voxel across 
K
 grid scales:
$$\begin{aligned} w_i = \text {SoftMax}\big (\delta _{\text {adp}} \big (\varvec{f}_i\big )\big ) \end{aligned}$$

                    (1)
                
where 
\(\delta _{\text {adp}}: \mathbb {R}^d \mapsto \mathbb {R}^K\)
 maps the voxel feature 
\(\varvec{f}_i\)
 to a 
K
-dimensional scale weight vector, and the SoftMax operation guarantees that the sum of all weights equals 1.
Using the predicted weights, the network aggregated the multi-scale grid features and concatenated them with the projected original feature to obtain the final enhanced feature:
$$\begin{aligned} \varvec{f}_i' = \delta _{\text {out}} \left( \delta _{\text {proj}}(\varvec{f}_i) \oplus \sum _{k=1}^{K} w_{i,k} \cdot o_{\phi '(i,k),k} \right) \end{aligned}$$

                    (2)
                
where 
\(\delta _{\text {proj}}\)
 and 
\(\delta _{\text {out}}\)
 denote linear projection layers with normalization and activation functions, 
\(\oplus \)
 represents vector concatenation, and 
\(o_{\phi '(i,k),k}\)
 denotes the feature corresponding to the voxel block that contained voxel 
i
 at the 
k
-th grid scale.
Through this mechanism, the model adaptively assigned larger receptive-field weights to flat reactor inner-wall regions and higher small-scale weights to deposit boundaries or complex adhesion structures. This enabled voxel-level adaptive receptive field regulation. Compared with sparse convolution using a single fixed receptive field, the proposed module better balanced global scene consistency and local detail preservation. Moreover, compared with fixed hierarchical multi-scale designs, the hierarchical pyramid grid adopted in this study was better aligned with the hierarchical encoding process of LitePT, thereby improving the efficiency and adaptability of multi-scale feature representation.
Adaptive relational convolution
The spatial adaptive receptive field module addresses the insufficient contextual modeling caused by fixed receptive fields. However, standard sparse convolution still struggles to explicitly characterize correlations among local voxels. In point cloud segmentation of pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls, deposit regions typically exhibit fragmented boundaries, irregular adhesion patterns, and sharp local geometric variations. Relying only on fixed convolution kernels for local feature extraction may therefore lead to insufficient representation of local structures.
In contrast, Transformer-based methods dynamically model the dependencies between features at different positions through attention mechanisms, thereby achieving better segmentation performance. However, directly introducing self-attention mechanisms into the shallow voxel convolution stages of LitePT would significantly increase computational cost and memory consumption, which is inconsistent with the lightweight design objective. Therefore, this study proposes an Adaptive Relation Convolution (Adaptive Relation Convolution, AR-Conv) module in the convolutional stages of LitePT. Inspired by OA-CNNS, AR-Conv enhances local voxel relationship modeling in a lightweight manner. Unlike OA-CNNS, which mainly captures spatial correlations among neighboring voxels, AR-Conv further introduces centroid-based local relation modeling by learning the feature differences between non-empty voxels and local geometric centers to construct relation representations. This center-offset geometric representation can effectively capture geometric variations within local regions, making it more suitable for pharmaceutical deposit segmentation tasks and improving the model’s fine-grained representation capability for deposit boundaries, local protrusions, and geometric transition regions.
The computational basis of AR-Conv was depthwise convolution. This design retains the high efficiency of depthwise convolution while incorporating the spatial adaptability of local patch modeling. It therefore matches the dual requirements of lightweight computation and high segmentation performance in industrial point cloud scenarios.
Relationship modeling based on local voxel grids
Let the 
i
-th local voxel grid be defined as:
$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {V}_i=\left\{ v_{\phi (i,j)}\right\} _{j=1}^{N_i}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (3)
                
where 
\(N_i\)
 denotes the number of non-empty voxels in the grid, and 
\(\textbf{f}_{\phi (i,j)} \in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 denotes the feature vector of the corresponding voxel. To balance computational efficiency and local structure representation, this study uses depthwise convolution as the basic computational backbone of AR-Conv. That is, local weighted aggregation is performed independently for each feature channel, as shown in Fig. 
5
. This design preserves the advantages of depthwise convolution, including a small number of parameters and low computational overhead, while providing a lightweight formulation for subsequent dynamic relationship modeling.
For the 
i
-th local voxel grid, the output of the 
c
-th channel is formulated as:
$$\begin{aligned} o_{i,c}=\sum _{j=1}^{N_i} w'_{i,c,j} f_{\phi (i,j),c}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (4)
                
where 
\(w'_{i,c,j}\)
 denotes the dynamic convolution weight of the 
j
-th non-empty voxel in the 
i
-th local voxel grid for the 
c
-th channel. Unlike conventional sparse convolution, which uses fixed convolution kernels, AR-Conv adaptively generates convolution weights from the relationships among local voxels. Therefore, the feature aggregation pattern can be dynamically adjusted according to the local geometric distribution.

Dynamic kernel generation based on centroid proxy voxel
To perform local relational reasoning with low computational overhead, this study adopts a many-to-one-to-many relationship modeling strategy. Instead of establishing full pairwise relationships among local voxels as in self-attention, a centroid proxy voxel is first constructed for each local voxel grid. The correlations between each non-empty voxel and the centroid proxy voxel are then used to generate dynamic convolution kernel weights. As shown in Fig. 
6
, this design captures important local geometric relationships while substantially reducing computational and memory costs.
Figure 6
Full size image
Illustration of the adaptive relational convolution module, AR-Conv. The module dynamically generates voxel-grid convolution kernel weights for non-empty voxels according to their relationships with the centroid proxy voxel.
Specifically, for the 
i
-th local voxel grid, the centroid proxy feature 
\(\textbf{f}_{i}^{\textrm{ctr}}\in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 is defined as:
$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{f}_{i}^{\textrm{ctr}} = \operatorname {AvgPool} \left( \left\{ \delta _{\textrm{proj}}\left( \textbf{f}_{j}\right) \mid j \in \Omega (i) \right\} \right) , \end{aligned}$$

                    (5)
                
where 
\(\Omega (i)\)
 denotes the voxel index set corresponding to the 
i
-th local voxel grid, 
\(\delta _{\textrm{proj}}:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 denotes a linear projection layer with normalization and activation, and 
\(\operatorname {AvgPool}(\cdot )\)
 denotes average pooling over the features within the local voxel grid. This operation produces a compact geometric and semantic center representation for the local region.
The dynamic convolution weights are then generated from the difference between each non-empty voxel feature and the centroid proxy feature:
$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{W}_{i,:,j} = \delta _{\textrm{weight}} \left( \textbf{f}_{\phi (i,j)}-\textbf{f}_{i}^{\textrm{ctr}} \right) , \end{aligned}$$

                    (6)
                
where 
\(\delta _{\textrm{weight}}:\mathbb {R}^{d}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 denotes a linear mapping layer, and 
\(\textbf{W}_{i,:,j}\in \mathbb {R}^{d}\)
 denotes the initial dynamic weight vector of the 
j
-th non-empty voxel in the 
i
-th local voxel grid across all channels. To improve training stability and make the weights within the same local voxel grid more comparable, Softmax normalization is applied along the channel dimension, yielding the final aggregation weights 
\(w'_{i,c,j}\)
. Through this process, AR-Conv adaptively generates dynamic convolution kernels only for non-empty voxels according to their correlations with the local geometric center. Therefore, efficient local relationship modeling is achieved without introducing an explicit self-attention module.
After obtaining the local relationship-enhanced features from AR-Conv, this study fuses them with the original voxel features through a residual connection. This operation preserves the original geometric information and feature distribution stability while enhancing local structural representation. Specifically, given the AR-Conv output 
\(\textbf{o}_i\)
 and the input feature 
\(\textbf{f}_i\)
, the enhanced local representation is formulated as:
$$\begin{aligned} \tilde{\textbf{f}}_i = \textbf{f}_i + \delta _{\textrm{res}} \left( \textbf{o}_i \right) , \end{aligned}$$

                    (7)
                
where 
\(\delta _{\textrm{res}}(\cdot )\)
 denotes a linear mapping or feature transformation layer used to align the channel dimension.
Patch-wise channel attention
Standard channel attention mechanisms, such as SENet
28
 and ECA
29
, usually operate on global features and generate channel weights by compressing global spatial information. However, in 3D point clouds, global compression may discard important local geometric cues. In the present application, reactor-wall points occupy the majority of the point cloud. Global average pooling would mix the features of the reactor wall and pharmaceutical deposits, making the generated channel weights less effective in selectively enhancing local deposit regions. Point cloud data also exhibit strong locality: spatially adjacent points often belong to the same category or share similar semantic properties. In this study, the point cloud is serialized according to voxel-coordinate ordering or a space-filling curve, so that adjacent points in the sequence have high spatial proximity. To emphasize the local information of pharmaceutical deposits, which occupy only a small portion of the entire inner wall, neighboring points are grouped into the same patch and share the same attention weights. This design introduces a local inductive bias, enabling the model to learn locally consistent feature representations more effectively. The detailed procedure is shown in Fig. 
7
.

Structure of the patch-wise channel attention module.
Moreover, although point-wise attention provides the strongest representational capacity, it requires independent channel-weight generation for each point and therefore incurs a relatively high computational cost of approximately 
O
(
NC
). Global channel attention is more efficient, but its global feature compression tends to discard local geometric details. Patch-wise channel attention provides a trade-off between these two strategies. Its attention-weight generation has a complexity of 
O
(
PC
), where 
\(P \approx N/K\)
 denotes the number of patches, and can therefore be written as 
O
(
NC
/
K
). Meanwhile, by sharing attention weights within each patch, the module introduces local consistency while preserving local geometric features with reduced computational overhead.
Given the input point cloud feature matrix 
\(\textbf{X}\in \mathbb {R}^{N\times C}\)
, where 
N
 denotes the number of points and 
C
 denotes the number of feature channels, the serialized point features are first divided into fixed-size patches. Specifically, the first 
\(P-1\)
 patches contain 
K
 points each, while the last patch retains the remaining 
\(K'\)
 points if the number of residual points is smaller than 
K
. Thus, the total number of patches is 
\(P=\lceil N/K \rceil \)
.
For the 
p
-th patch, where 
\(p\in \{1,2,\ldots ,P\}\)
, let 
\(K_p\)
 denote the number of points in this patch, and let its feature matrix be 
\(\textbf{X}_p\in \mathbb {R}^{K_p\times C}\)
. Average pooling is applied to aggregate the features of all points within the patch and generate a patch-level descriptor for channel statistics:
$$\begin{aligned} \bar{\textbf{x}}_p = \frac{1}{K_p} \sum _{i=1}^{K_p} \textbf{X}_p[i,:] \in \mathbb {R}^{C}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (8)
                
A lightweight one-dimensional convolution is then applied along the channel dimension to model inter-channel interactions. The channel attention weights are generated through a Sigmoid activation function:
$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{w}_p = \sigma \left( \textrm{Conv1D} \left( \bar{\textbf{x}}_p \right) \right) \in \mathbb {R}^{C}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (9)
                
where 
\(\sigma (\cdot )\)
 denotes the Sigmoid activation function. The generated patch-level channel attention vector 
\(\textbf{w}_p\)
 is then broadcast to all 
\(K_p\)
 points within the same patch for channel-wise feature reweighting:
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\textbf{X}}_p = \textbf{X}_p \odot \left( \textbf{1}_{K_p}\textbf{w}_p^{T} \right) \in \mathbb {R}^{K_p\times C}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (10)
                
where 
\(\odot \)
 denotes element-wise Hadamard multiplication, and 
\(\textbf{1}_{K_p}\in \mathbb {R}^{K_p\times 1}\)
 denotes a 
\(K_p\)
-dimensional all-one column vector used for broadcasting. Finally, all enhanced patch features are concatenated in their original order to obtain the output feature matrix:
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\textbf{X}} = \textrm{Concat} \left( \hat{\textbf{X}}_1, \hat{\textbf{X}}_2, \ldots , \hat{\textbf{X}}_P \right) \in \mathbb {R}^{N\times C}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (11)
                
Compared with methods that generate channel weights independently for each point, patch-wise channel attention substantially reduces computational cost while preserving a local-consistency inductive bias. Compared with global channel attention, it better retains local geometric features of pharmaceutical deposit regions. Therefore, this module selectively enhances feature responses in local abnormal regions and improves the model’s ability to recognize small-scale complex structures.
Experiments and analysis
Dataset construction and evaluation metrics
To evaluate the performance of the proposed network for pharmaceutical deposit point cloud segmentation on reactor inner walls, this study constructs a dedicated point cloud dataset for chemical reactor deposit detection. The simulated reactor used for data acquisition has the same material and diameter as practical industrial reactors, aiming to reproduce the scanning conditions in real industrial environments as closely as possible. Considering that collecting deposit samples from real industrial production environments is constrained by production cycles, equipment safety, and the difficulty of repeated sample acquisition, artificial preparation was adopted to construct deposit samples. Specifically, ethyl acetate, dibutyl phthalate, and target pharmaceutical raw materials were uniformly mixed at a certain ratio to prepare deposit samples attached to the inner wall surface of the reactor. This material combination can generate adhesion characteristics and surface geometric features similar to industrial deposits, providing three-dimensional structural characteristics close to real application scenarios for point cloud acquisition. By adjusting the material composition and preparation process parameters, deposit regions with different attachment states and morphological characteristics were constructed. Deposits generated under different preparation conditions exhibit different surface morphologies and spatial distribution characteristics. Among them, the continuous attachment state mainly forms relatively complete thin-layer structures; the local accumulation state presents regional thickness increases and local protrusion structures; while the fragmented state exhibits discontinuous distributions, broken boundaries, and non-uniform thickness variations. Through the above preparation strategy, the dataset contains various typical deposit morphologies, including thin-layer attachments, local protrusions, irregular lump-like accumulations, strip-shaped deposits, and fragmented boundary regions. The deposit thickness is mainly distributed within 5–20 mm, which reflects the scale variations, morphological diversity, and boundary complexity of deposits in practical applications.
The dataset construction process mainly includes scanning system setup, deposit sample preparation, point cloud acquisition, multi-view registration, point cloud preprocessing, manual annotation, and dataset splitting. A three-dimensional scanning system consisting of a rotary platform and a LiDAR sensor was established to perform 360
\(\circ \)
 circular scanning of the reactor inner wall. Meanwhile, scanning angle and pose information were synchronously recorded, and complete inner-wall point clouds were obtained through multi-view point cloud registration. To cover different deposit attachment patterns, various pharmaceutical deposit regions were constructed on the reactor wall, including thin-layer attachments, local protrusions, irregular block-shaped accumulations, strip-shaped deposits, and fragmented boundary regions. To improve dataset diversity, the attachment locations, coverage areas, and spatial distributions of deposits were adjusted during data acquisition, enabling the dataset to include deposit regions with different sizes, shapes, and spatial distribution patterns. Meanwhile, by changing the position of the scanning device and acquisition viewpoints, point clouds from different observation distances and angles were collected. This allows local deposit regions to exhibit richer geometric variations and improves the dataset coverage of spatial and viewpoint changes.
Although artificially prepared deposits may still exhibit certain domain gaps compared with deposits in real industrial environments, such as differences in chemical composition, microscopic surface morphology, and actual adhesion strength, artificial data cannot completely replace real industrial scenarios. However, for three-dimensional point cloud segmentation tasks, the key factors affecting model performance mainly involve the geometric characteristics of deposits, including thin-layer structures, irregular shapes, weak boundaries, and discontinuous distributions. In this study, different deposit states, spatial locations, acquisition viewpoints, and sensor positions were introduced to improve the coverage of these key geometric characteristics, thereby reducing the potential bias caused by artificially simulated data. The collected deposit point clouds were generally more localized and irregular than the large-area reactor-wall background. Some deposits appeared as concentrated block-like or sheet-like regions with relatively continuous boundaries, whereas others appeared as elongated strips, discrete spots, or spatially separated regions with fragmented edges and incomplete morphologies. Since deposits were adjacent to the reactor wall and occupied only a small proportion of the whole point cloud, they were easily affected by wall background points, scanning stripes, and point-density variations. These characteristics make deposit segmentation challenging in terms of small-object recognition, complex boundary extraction, and class imbalance.
After single-frame point cloud acquisition, multi-frame point clouds were transformed and registered using the scanning pose information and the spatial relationship between the LiDAR coordinate system and the rotation axis to obtain a complete reactor inner-wall point cloud. The registered point cloud was then preprocessed by outlier removal, local noise filtering, voxel grid downsampling, and smoothing, which improved data quality while preserving key geometric features such as deposit boundaries, protrusions, and thin-layer adhesion. After preprocessing, the point cloud was manually annotated into two semantic classes: reactor-wall background and pharmaceutical deposits. In addition to the three-dimensional coordinates, the raw LiDAR point cloud also contains reflectance intensity information. However, the original intensity values are affected by scanning distance, surface reflectivity, and acquisition conditions. Therefore, intensity normalization is required before using it as network input.
Specifically, the original intensity value 
\(I\)
 is linearly normalized to the range of [0,1]:
$$\begin{aligned} I_{\textrm{norm}} = \frac{I-I_{\min }}{I_{\max }-I_{\min }}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (12)
                
where 
\(I_{\text {min}}\)
 and 
\(I_{\text {max}}\)
 represent the minimum and maximum intensity values within each point cloud scene, respectively.
Subsequently, a pseudo-color mapping strategy was adopted to transform the normalized intensity values into RGB color features. Thus, each point was represented by a six-dimensional feature vector comprising spatial coordinates 
\((x,y,z)\)
 and color features 
\((R,G,B)\)
, together with its corresponding semantic label.
The complete dataset consisted of 600 scene samples, each containing approximately one million points. To ensure dataset independence and objective model evaluation, data acquisition was conducted in three independent stages, with deposit configurations prepared and data collected separately at each stage. Specifically, 360 scenes were first collected for the training set, followed by the independent acquisition of 120 scenes for the validation set and a further 120 scenes for the test set, resulting in a 6:2:2 split. The deposit configurations and acquisition batches were independent across the three subsets, ensuring that scans derived from the same physical deposit configuration or acquisition batch did not appear across the training, validation, and test sets. This partitioning strategy reduced the risk of data leakage caused by highly correlated samples. To comprehensively evaluate the segmentation performance of the proposed model, this study employs Accuracy (Acc), mean Intersection over Union (mIoU), deposit-class Intersection over Union (IoU), F-score@10mm, Chamfer Distance (CD), and 95th percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95) as evaluation metrics. The detailed calculation formulations are provided below:
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Acc} = \frac{\textrm{TP}}{\textrm{TP}+\textrm{FP}}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (13)
                
where 
\(\textrm{TP}\)
 and 
\(\textrm{FP}\)
 denote true positives and false positives, respectively.
The Intersection over Union (IoU) is used to evaluate the overlap accuracy of segmentation results by calculating the ratio between the intersection and union of the predicted segmentation region and the ground-truth segmentation region. Specifically, the IoU values of the pharmaceutical deposit category (target class) and reactor wall category (background class) are calculated separately, and their average value is adopted as the overall mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) of the model. The formulations are given as follows:
$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm {IoU_x} = \frac{|P_x\cap G_x|}{|P_x\cup G_x|} \end{aligned}$$

                    (14)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{mIoU} = \frac{1}{2} \sum _{x=0}^{1}IoU_x \end{aligned}$$

                    (15)
                
where 
\(P_x\)
 and 
\(G_x\)
 denote the predicted and ground-truth point sets of category 
\(x\)
, respectively. Here, 
\(x=0\)
 represents the reactor wall class and 
\(x=1\)
 represents the pharmaceutical deposit class.
Due to the fact that pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls usually exhibit thin-layer attachment, blurred boundaries, and irregular geometric structures, point-level segmentation metrics alone cannot sufficiently evaluate the capability of the model to preserve local geometric structures. Therefore, boundary and geometric distance metrics are further introduced for evaluation.
For the predicted boundary 
\(B_p\)
 and the ground-truth boundary 
\(B_g\)
, the Boundary F-score@10mm is adopted to measure boundary consistency.When the Euclidean distance from a boundary point to its nearest point in the opposite boundary set is smaller than 10 mm, the point is considered successfully matched. The boundary precision, recall, and F-score are defined as follows:
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Precision}= \frac{|B_p^{match}|}{|B_p|} \end{aligned}$$

                    (16)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Recall}= \frac{|B_g^{match}|}{|B_g|} \end{aligned}$$

                    (17)
                
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{F}{-}\textrm{score}@10\textrm{mm} = \frac{2\times \textrm{Precision}\times \textrm{Recall}}{\textrm{Precision}+\textrm{Recall}} \end{aligned}$$

                    (18)
                
where 
\(B_p\)
 and 
\(B_g\)
 denote the predicted boundary and ground-truth boundary, respectively. 
\(B_p^{match}\)
 and 
\(B_g^{match}\)
 represent the matched boundary points in the predicted and ground-truth boundaries. A higher F-score indicates better consistency between the predicted deposit boundary and the ground-truth boundary.
To further evaluate the geometric consistency and boundary deviation between the predicted deposit regions and the ground-truth regions, Chamfer Distance (CD) and 95th percentile Hausdorff Distance (HD95) are adopted.
Chamfer Distance measures the average nearest-neighbor distance between the predicted deposit point set 
\(S_p\)
 and the ground-truth deposit point set 
\(S_g:\)
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{CD}= \frac{1}{|S_p|} \sum _{p\in S_p} \min _{q\in S_g} ||p-q|| + \frac{1}{|S_g|} \sum _{q\in S_g} \min _{p\in S_p} ||p-q|| \end{aligned}$$

                    (19)
                
where 
\(S_p\)
 and 
\(S_g\)
 denote the predicted and ground-truth deposit point sets, respectively. A smaller CD indicates a smaller average geometric deviation between the predicted region and the ground-truth region.
HD95 is used to quantify the spatial deviation between the predicted and ground-truth deposit regions. By calculating the 95th percentile of the Hausdorff distance distribution, the influence of outliers is reduced while the accuracy of the segmentation contour is reflected. It is defined as:
$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{HD95}=P_{95}(D(S_p,S_g)) \end{aligned}$$

                    (20)
                
In addition, the training loss was reported in the ablation experiments to analyze the influence of different modules on the model optimization process. These metrics evaluate model performance from two aspects: overall segmentation quality and deposit-target recognition capability.

Sensitivity analysis of LAR-net with respect to point cloud spatial resolution and point density
Due to the thin-layer, fragmented, and weak-boundary characteristics of pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls, the spatial resolution of the input point cloud may directly affect the preservation of fine-grained geometric structures. To quantitatively evaluate the performance degradation of LAR-Net under different point cloud resolutions, this study adjusts the voxel size during the point cloud voxelization process to generate input point clouds with different spatial resolutions and analyzes their impact on segmentation performance.
To quantitatively characterize the influence of different voxel sizes on the spatial resolution of input point clouds, the point retention ratio is introduced as a metric to measure the variation in point number after voxelization. During the point cloud voxelization process, continuous three-dimensional space is divided into regular grids, and a discrete point cloud representation is generated according to voxel indices. The voxel size determines the spatial sampling granularity. A smaller voxel size can preserve more local geometric details but generates more input points and increases computational cost. Conversely, a larger voxel size reduces the number of points and computational complexity but may result in the loss of local geometric structures, especially for thin-layer deposits and fragmented boundary regions. The point retention ratio is defined as follows:
$$\begin{aligned} R_p=\frac{N_v}{N_o} \end{aligned}$$

                    (21)
                
where 
\(N_v\)
 represents the number of non-empty voxels after voxelization with the current voxel size, and 
\(N_o\)
 represents the number of points in the original point cloud. A higher point retention ratio indicates that more spatial sampling information is preserved during voxelization, which is beneficial for maintaining thin-layer structures and boundary details. A lower retention ratio indicates stronger spatial compression, which results in the loss of local geometric information.
With the network architecture and training strategy unchanged, comparative experiments were conducted by only adjusting the voxel size during the point cloud voxelization stage. Considering the computational resource limitations of the experimental platform, four voxel sizes of 0.01 m, 0.02 m, 0.03 m, and 0.05 m were selected for evaluation. Although smaller voxel sizes can improve spatial sampling resolution, they significantly increase the number of active voxels and input point clouds, resulting in substantially higher GPU memory consumption during training. Therefore, when the voxel size was further reduced below 0.01 m, the required GPU memory exceeded the available capacity of the experimental platform, and smaller voxel sizes were not considered in this study.
As shown in Table 
7
, voxel size has a significant impact on both segmentation performance and computational efficiency of LAR-Net. With the increase of voxel size from 0.01 m to 0.05 m, the point retention ratio decreases from 82% to 18%, accompanied by a substantial reduction in computational consumption. Specifically, the training peak GPU memory decreases from 31.05 GB to 8.73 GB, and the inference latency is reduced from 53.27 ms to 26.89 ms, demonstrating that larger voxel sizes can effectively reduce computational requirements. However, excessive spatial compression results in the loss of fine-grained geometric information. When the voxel size is set to 0.01 m, LAR-Net achieves the best segmentation performance, with a pharmaceutical deposits IoU of 0.6879 and a Boundary F-score@10mm of 0.827, indicating that higher spatial resolution is beneficial for preserving thin-layer deposit boundaries and local geometric variations. As the voxel size increases to 0.03 m and 0.05 m, the pharmaceutical deposits IoU decreases to 0.6512 and 0.5962, while the Boundary F-score@10mm decreases to 0.765 and 0.712, respectively. Meanwhile, the Chamfer Distance and HD95 gradually increase with larger voxel sizes, rising from 1.27 mm and 9.05 mm at 0.01 m to 2.41 mm and 15.52 mm at 0.05 m. This indicates that coarse voxelization introduces larger geometric deviations between predicted deposit boundaries and ground-truth surfaces.
These results demonstrate that, for thin-layer pharmaceutical deposits with thicknesses of approximately 5–20 mm, excessively large voxel sizes may remove critical boundary and local structural information, thereby degrading the model’s ability to identify fine-grained deposit regions. Therefore, selecting an appropriate spatial resolution is essential for achieving an effective balance between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency in industrial point cloud segmentation tasks.

Conclusion and future work
Existing deep-learning-based point cloud semantic segmentation methods still face limitations in accurately segmenting pharmaceutical deposits on reactor inner walls. To address the challenges posed by the small scale, irregular morphology, complex boundaries, and dominant reactor-wall background of pharmaceutical deposits, this study proposes LAR-Net, a lightweight adaptive relational network for point cloud segmentation of reactor inner-wall pharmaceutical deposits. Based on the LitePT backbone, LAR-Net introduces three dedicated modules: a spatial adaptive receptive field module, an adaptive relational convolution module, and a patch-wise channel attention module. These modules improve model performance from three complementary aspects: dynamic receptive field adjustment, modeling of local geometric relationships between deposits and reactor inner walls, and enhancement of locally salient deposit features. This design enables the network to maintain global consistency over large smooth reactor-wall regions while strengthening its ability to recognize deposit boundaries, protrusions, discrete small targets, and other fine-grained local structures (Fig. 
18
).

The segmentation results show that, compared with other existing point cloud segmentation methods, the proposed method can more completely preserve the spatial distribution and local morphology of pharmaceutical deposits. In particular, LAR-Net demonstrates stronger recognition capability for small-scale deposits, sparse deposit points, and fragmented boundary regions. In contrast, several existing models can recover the main reactor structure but still suffer from missed detections, local discontinuities, and incomplete boundaries in deposit regions. Based on the deposit segmentation results obtained by LAR-Net, quantitative analysis of deposit regions can be further performed for equipment maintenance applications. First, the predicted deposit point clouds can be reconstructed into surface representations, and the deposit coverage area can be estimated by accumulating the reconstructed surface area. This information can directly reflect the distribution and coverage degree of deposits in different regions, providing a quantitative basis for determining cleaning areas and locating critical maintenance regions. Furthermore, the severity of deposits in different regions can be evaluated by considering their spatial locations, coverage areas, and thickness information. For example, regions with large coverage areas, high deposit thickness, or concentrated accumulation generally require higher maintenance priority, whereas small-scale and low-thickness deposits can be scheduled for subsequent maintenance. By integrating deposit area, spatial distribution, and deposit severity information, differentiated cleaning region planning can be achieved for different reactor conditions, providing data support for precision cleaning strategy development and cleaning cycle optimization. Therefore, LAR-Net not only enables fine-grained three-dimensional point cloud segmentation of pharmaceutical deposits but also provides potential support for subsequent intelligent cleaning and equipment maintenance decision-making.
Nevertheless, several limitations remain to be addressed. Although LAR-Net achieves a favorable balance between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency, further optimization is still required for processing extremely large-scale point clouds or deployment on resource-constrained edge devices. In addition, the current study mainly focuses on pharmaceutical deposit segmentation, and further validation is required for multi-class industrial defect segmentation involving multiple defect types on the same surface. Future work will investigate more efficient deployment strategies, such as model compression, operator optimization, and adaptive point cloud sampling, while extending the dataset to diverse industrial defects to further evaluate the generalization capability of LAR-Net.

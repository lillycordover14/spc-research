Same virus family, more than a sixfold difference in the likelihood of retinal detachment

                      November 20, 2026
                  

Long-term clinical data reveal differences in retinal detachment risk, timing, and vision outcomes

News

Research

Basic Medicine

Clinical Medicine

     What the research is about
    

         At the back of the eye is the retina, a vital layer of tissue that senses light. When a viral infection causes inflammation in the retina, vision can decline sharply and, in severe cases, blindness can result. There is also a risk of retinal detachment, in which the retina pulls away from its normal position.

Two major examples of these conditions are acute retinal necrosis (ARN) and cytomegalovirus retinitis (CMVR), both caused by viruses in the herpesvirus family. Both can threaten vision, but they differ in the immune status of the people they affect and in how the disease progresses.

New Africa/Shutterstock.com

ARN can cause intense inflammation and progress rapidly. CMVR, by contrast, tends to occur in people whose immune systems are weakened and often progresses more slowly. This makes it important to determine which disease a patient has as early as possible, understand the patient’s immune status, and assess the risk of the condition becoming more severe.

A research team led by Associate Professor Koju Kamoi at Institute of Science Tokyo (Science Tokyo) analyzed clinical data from 120 patients, involving a total of 144 eyes, who were diagnosed with ARN or CMVR between 2013 and 2025. The patients were followed for an average of 5.5 years. The researchers compared their immune status, disease characteristics, time to diagnosis, whether retinal detachment occurred and when, treatments received, and subsequent vision.

     Why this matters
    

The study revealed major differences between the two diseases in both the time to diagnosis and the likelihood of retinal detachment.

First, the time from the onset of symptoms to diagnosis differed considerably. The median time — the middle value when the data are arranged from smallest to largest — was 6 days for ARN and 19 days for CMVR. Retinal detachment was found in 68.1% of eyes with ARN, compared with 10.8% of eyes with CMVR, a difference of more than sixfold.

There was also a striking difference in when retinal detachment occurred. Among eyes with retinal detachment, the median time from diagnosis to its detection was 6 days for ARN and 65.5 days for CMVR. This comparison also included patients who already had retinal detachment when they were first diagnosed.

The two diseases also showed different clinical features. ARN was more likely to affect only one eye and to cause changes in the peripheral retina. CMVR, meanwhile, was more common among patients with malignant lymphoma and those taking medications that suppress the immune system.

Even after taking differences such as vision at the first visit into account, ARN was associated with a higher likelihood of retinal detachment than CMVR. Poorer vision at the first visit was also associated with a higher risk of retinal detachment.

The timing of retinal detachment and whether patients received treatments such as surgery were also associated with their later vision.

Together, these findings suggest that predicting how the disease may progress requires looking not only at which type of retinitis a patient has, but also at the patient’s immune status and the condition of the eye at the first examination.

     What’s next
    

The study shows that even retinal diseases caused by viruses from the same family can differ greatly in how often retinal detachment occurs, when it is detected, and how vision is affected afterward.

In ARN in particular, retinal detachment may already be present at diagnosis or may develop soon afterward. This means that the risk of retinal detachment needs to be considered from an early stage.

In the future, combining information such as vision at the first visit, the condition of the eye, and the patient’s immune status may help doctors identify the risk of severe disease earlier. This could support more timely decisions about the most appropriate treatment for each patient and ultimately help protect vision.

     Comment from the researcher
    

         People can sometimes become infected with viruses without realizing it. In fact, many people have already been infected with members of the herpesvirus family studied here. Once a person is infected, these viruses can remain dormant in the body. Most of the time they cause no problems, but depending on the type of virus and a person’s immune status, they can sometimes cause severe retinal inflammation that may even lead to blindness.

By analyzing long-term clinical data, we found major differences in both the likelihood of retinal detachment and the time until it was detected.

We hope these findings will contribute to earlier diagnosis and appropriate treatment, helping to protect the vision of as many patients as possible.

(Koju Kamoi, Associate Professor, Department of Ophthalmology and Visual Science, Graduate School of Medical and Dental Sciences, Institute of Science Tokyo)

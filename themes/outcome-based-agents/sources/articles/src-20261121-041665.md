The newly created 
Northern Illinois Transit Authority
 faces its first major test Dec. 10, when its board votes on a proposed 2027–2031 
Five-Year Capital Program
. Right now, that capital plan is on a collision course with Illinois law.
CTA
 and 
Pace
 tell the public they are committed to achieving 100% zero-emission bus fleets by 2040. Yet both agencies are asking NITA to approve tens of millions of dollars to buy buses that burn fossil fuels and will stay on our streets well past 2040.
Even worse, those purchases appear to be illegal.

Commentary bug

Commentary

Since 2021, the Chicago chapter of the 
Climate Reality Project
 has urged the Chicago Transit Authority, Pace and the former Regional Transportation Authority to accelerate bus electrification. CTA’s diesel buses alone account for roughly 
3%
 of all on-road vehicle greenhouse-gas emissions in Chicago. Frustrated by years of inaction, we drafted legislation that became Illinois law in 2023 to ban fossil-fuel bus contracts starting in mid-2026.
When lawmakers created NITA this year, they preserved and strengthened that mandate. Illinois law now 
states
 plainly, “The Authority may not enter into a new contract to purchase a bus that is not a zero-emission bus for the purpose of a Service Board’s transit bus fleet.”
The math behind the law is simple. Transit buses operate for about 14 years. Any fossil-fuel bus ordered today will still be spewing exhaust in 2040.
Yet CTA is proposing to buy 
150 diesel-electric hybrid buses
 starting in 2027, compared to just 40 battery-electric buses. Calling a diesel hybrid part of a zero-emission fleet does not make it so. Diesel hybrids still burn fossil fuel and cut greenhouse-gas emissions by only 10% to 20% compared to conventional diesel. Meanwhile, CTA programs another $297 million for bus purchases without specifying what types or how many buses it plans to buy.
Pace’s capital plan contains a similar contradiction. Alongside $150 million for 99 battery-electric buses, Pace programs roughly $68 million for additional fossil-fuel buses — including compressed natural gas and hybrid models — without disclosing exact vehicle counts.
This isn’t just an environmental and legal failure; it is a costly financial mistake. Using industry fuel and maintenance benchmarks, we estimate that transitioning CTA and Pace to a fully electric fleet would save the region more than $110 million every year in operating costs.
CTA will likely blame its slow transition on limited federal funding. That excuse doesn’t hold up. In 2022, when the federal government 
distributed $1.6 billion
 for zero-emission buses and garages, transit agencies in New York, Boston and Los Angeles each won more than $100 million. CTA requested only $29 million for 10 electric buses and a minor garage upgrade because it hadn’t done the engineering work needed to compete for major infrastructure grants.
That lack of preparation is still crippling CTA’s garage electrification. CTA’s 2022 Charging Forward plan promised electric buses operating out of its 103rd Street Garage by 2024 and its 77th Street Garage by 2027. Today, CTA is only just requesting $133 million to retrofit 103rd Street — three years behind schedule — while omitting dedicated electrification funding for 77th Street, 74th Street and a planned West Side or South Side garage. Pace has been more proactive in Waukegan, Elgin and Wheeling, yet still hasn’t provided a transparent fleet schedule.
Taxpayers and riders created NITA to bring real oversight and accountability to regional transit. Allowing CTA and Pace to redefine “zero emissions” to include fossil-fuel buses — while hiding fleet numbers behind vague budget lines — would make a mockery of that reform.
Before voting Dec. 10, the NITA Board must send this capital plan back and demand three things: Remove every fossil-fuel bus purchase to comply with state law; publish exact, transparent procurement schedules for every bus dollar; and establish a funded, credible timeline to electrify CTA and Pace garages.
A zero-emission commitment must mean zero emissions from a bus tailpipe — not slightly lower emissions, not hybrid emissions and not promises that are contradicted by the buses being programmed in this five-year capital plan.
The NITA board should send these plans back for revision for a capital plan that complies with Illinois law and make a credible path to a truly zero-emission fleet by 2040. The planet and the people of Northeastern Illinois demand nothing less.
Bruce W. Mainzer is a former mass transit executive in Chicago and New York City and has also worked as a consultant in ground transport and other industries. He retired in 2019 and served as co-chair of the Chicago chapter of the Climate Reality Project between 2021 and 2025.

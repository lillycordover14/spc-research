David Crosby
 always tended to have higher standards for what any rock and roll band was supposed to be whenever he made a record. 
He didn’t have time for pedestrian pop music, and since some of his favourite artists were people who pushed the boundaries of the genre, like 
Joni Mitchell and Steely Dan
, going back to three-chord folk songs wasn’t really what he was that interested in all the time. He liked music that took a while to figure out a lot of the time, but it’s not like he wasn’t willing to lend his services to people that he thought were going to be massive later down the line.
That’s half the reason why he convinced Graham Nash to leave The Hollies in the first place. He knew 
that ‘Marrakesh Express’ 
was bound to be something massive, and even though the rest of Nash’s band didn’t get it, the song took on a whole new life when Crosby, Stills and Nash formed and laid those gorgeous harmonies over top of it. Because if there’s one thing that Crosby was a genius at, it was harmony writing. 
No one seemed to have the same ear for harmonies as he did, and he was bound to show everyone that there was so much more ground for people to cover if they started opening up the playing field. He was someone who could take the simplest melody and turn it into something a lot more complex, and while he did have a lot of classics under his belt during that time, there was always going to be that moment when things started crashing down when he spiralled into drug addiction.
He was going through some of the worst battles of his life with his addiction, but by the time that he started 
working with Phil Collins
, there was some light at the end of the tunnel. He was ready to be healthy again, and while that did lead to him featuring on one of the biggest singles of Collins’s career in ‘Another Day in Paradise’, what was the harm in helping out some of the new kids on the block as well?
After all, the adult alternative movement was like a natural extension of the singer-songwriter genre at the time, and working with Hootie and the Blowfish seemed to be a nice way for Crosby to help out the little guy. The band weren’t exactly well-known by any stretch at this time, but by the time that Crosby featured on their record, he felt that he was wasting his time trying to help out a band that was clearly going nowhere.
They all had the charisma of being a half-decent bar band whenever they sang, and Crosby felt that a song like ‘Hold My Hand’ was never going to take off, with frontman Darius Rucker 
saying
, “It was funny because after he had sung, he said to our producer, ‘Next time, could you have me sing with a band that is going to matter? That’s going to be important?’” Just a few years later, though, Crosby was going to have to eat those words.

While the band didn’t have an ounce of star power like any of the other 1990s rock gods did, 
Cracked Rearview 
was the kind of record that refused to die no matter what was in its way. The world seemed to relate to that everyman aesthetic that they had, and while they could make some truly cornball songs like ‘Only Wanna Be With You’, the fact that the album sold upwards of 20million units is thanks to what they did when they started working on a song like ‘Hold My Hand’.
There was no chance that Crosby saw the same depth in the song as everyone else, but in Rucker’s own words, all that he needed was a little love and some tenderness to get it over the line. Because as much as those songs could be cringy as hell, sometimes clichés like that can actually work if you find yourself in the right place at the right time.

By 
Tonda MacCharles
Ottawa Bureau Chief

OTTAWA — It was before midnight Wednesday when 
Mark Carney
 sat down in front of a computer screen in a Shenzhen hotel to take centre stage, not in China but at a high-profile New York Times event livestreamed half a world away — the last appearance of the prime minister’s jam-packed day.
He’d dined with APEC
 leaders, including the weirdly cosy threesome of 
China
, Russia and the U.S., 
sat down with President Donald Trump
, parried media questions and spoken to hundreds of Asian business leaders, pitching them to invest in Canada.
At least he looked fresher than his cadre of exhausted senior aides and public servants, many of whom were wiped after Carney’s four-country, two-continent, eight-day sprint to international summits in Ireland, the Philippines, Cambodia and China.

The trip offered a window into Carney’s quest to diversify Canada’s alliances — a work in progress. Rather than wait for 
Canada-U.S. relations
 to normalize, he is racing around the world at a relentless pace.
Carney and his team celebrate milestones on Canadian Forces planes — his first trip as prime minister last year marked his 60th birthday. On this hectic trip, they served cake and sang “Happy Birthday” to his travel co-ordinator. While some lay sleeping under blankets, Canada’s top officials huddled to brief the prime minister, standing or sitting in twisted positions in the airplane aisle. Then they changed from casualwear to business suits in the washroom before landing and rushed into motorcades that dashed through cities flashing red lights, often heading straight to meetings with investors or world leaders.

It could all have a chaotic feel, even at the fanciest, most choreographed moments. In Phnom Penh for the Francophonie summit, a large bat swooped and echolocated a frantic path through the ornate prime ministerial palace as the Cambodian military saluted the Canadian prime minister’s arrival for a bilateral meeting added to an international trip that had him on the ground for less than 24 hours.
The rationale for this pace is “because there’s an imaginary clock on the wall, that’s how many days we have left to get the s—t done that we want to get done,” said a senior official who did not look worse for the wear. “Sleep when we’re dead.”
The trip ended with Canadian military pilots rushing to get the prime minister and his team back to Ottawa. Their plane arrived on the tarmac an hour early Thursday night.
Carney is on a global drive to find trading allies and friends other than the U.S., in search of options that reduce dependency on any one partner. He boasts of having signed seven free trade and investment deals and launched talks to reach seven more.

On this journey, the biggest takeaway for the Canadian team was how many more of those allies and friends are proactively seeking Canada out, having assessed — 
as Carney urged in Davos
 — their own strategic dependencies and vulnerabilities, and concluded that Canada is part of the solution.
In a volatile global environment, the data doesn’t yet show a huge turnaround, but Export Development Canada economists are forecasting that Canadian exports will grow 11 per cent in 2026 and surpass the $1-trillion mark for the first time. 
But Carney’s courting of stronger ties with China prompted New York Times interviewer Andrew Ross Sorkin to probe skeptically on Wednesday just how far Carney is prepared to go in distancing Canada from the U.S. Does he really trust President Xi Jinping? What if China invades Taiwan, a democracy — would Carney have regrets? What if American voters realize it was all a terrible mistake and elect a president in 2028 who wants the Canada-U.S. relationship to go back to “the way it was?” What then?
The answer was one of Carney’s most publicly revealing about the extent of the damage to the relationship.
“I can’t just put it back in the bottle,” he said. 
In effect, the rupture is permanent, no matter which U.S. president comes next, because Canadians have been too badly burned.
Carney described a Canada that is strongly united behind his drive to diversify away from the United States and an America that has turned its back on allies across the world. Asked 
whether midterm election losses for Trump’s Republicans
 signalled any prospect for change in trade talks, he said the short answer was no.

The U.S. should not assume good intentions would ever be enough to win back that kind of trust, especially given the president’s stance toward Greenland, comments about Canada as “the 51st state,” and the throttling of Starlink satellite communications that undermined Ukraine defences against Russian missile attacks. You can’t “unsee” that, Carney said.
Although Canada will always be an American ally and co-operate on Arctic defence, within NATO and on national security, Carney made clear that the Trump-led breakup is a reality — a “challenging” and “difficult” one that carries risks for Canada but is inescapable.
On the question of whether he trusts China, Carney said, “We trust alignment.”
Canada is acting without waiting for Trump to sign a trade deal to protect the North American auto sector, which is way behind China’s, Carney said. The deal should have been signed six months ago, he added, rejecting Trump’s claim that the prime minister is ready to give him “anything” and that the U.S. holds “all the cards” in trade negotiations.
“Our ‘cards’ is that everyone else in the world wants to deal with us,” Carney said.
“If the U.S. doesn’t want to do the deal, we’re busy.”
That’s an understatement.
Little more than a week ago, 
Carney travelled to Dublin
 to meet with the European Political Community, a wider grouping than the European Union, where Irish Prime Minister Micheál Martin hailed Carney’s advocacy of multilateral solutions to global problems.

ARTICLE CONTINUES BELOW

“There’s a strong pushback in the world and there’s a strong reassertion from many countries about the principles and the values of multilateralism,” said Martin. “Free and fair trade is one aspect of that, but also in terms of peace and security.”
Carney spent the week spreading that Davos message, and the prime minister believes it has reaped results.
In Manila, he signed off on negotiations to finalize 
free trade deals
 with the Philippines and the Southeast Asian trading bloc known as ASEAN. He also predicted he’d soon nail a free trade deal with India.
In Phnom Penh, Cambodia, Carney dropped in on a chaotic Francophonie summit, arriving after the “family photo” that Gov.-Gen. Louise Arbour stood for.
Carney delivered a powerful 
defence
 of French language and culture — a window onto his thinking about why it was a red line for him during Canada-U.S. trade talks that he broke off in August — and called for francophone nations to align in order to protect it in a digital age.
Then he was off to China, where he stayed the longest in one place all week: two days.

ARTICLE CONTINUES BELOW

Carney is keeping an insane pace for an insane world. The fatigue shows occasionally, when he stumbles as he shifts back and forth in English and French.
Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand, her eyes red and her voice scratchy during a 7:30 a.m. news conference Thursday, told Canadian reporters that “personally” everyone was experiencing the pace “differently,” but that it showed the “urgency” of Carney’s mission.
However, as the prime minister tries to operationalize his Davos vision, he doesn’t yet seem to have figured out where the centre of gravity for his new global order lies — or where Canada might find a replacement for the U.S. as its closest ally.
In Europe, he has said it will be built out from there. In Shenzhen, he said China can “help shape the global order that is open to sustainable, peaceful and human-centred growth.” 
Maybe it’s everywhere all at once.
In Manila, Carney said more countries are coming to see the benefit of broader partnerships, including trade deals and other kinds of agreements, to respond to “the dangers of excessive concentration.”
That’s leading countries to look to Canada, he said. On this trip, Carney chalked up formal bilateral meetings with Cyprus, Greece, Benin, Senegal, Brazil, Japan, Korea, Thailand and Mexico, among others. An official said they also “banged off” dozens of informal ones, and the results can be seen in rising Canadian exports to countries other than the U.S.

        ARTICLE CONTINUES BELOW
        

ARTICLE CONTINUES BELOW

So the vision is “manifesting,” Carney told reporters in China. “It’s part of our strategy … to develop this dense web of connections, to make us resilient, to make us more independent … so we’re not being told what to do by some hegemon or a hyper-scaler.
“And it is, to a varying degree, part of the strategies of many, many countries, and the two are coming together, and that’s one of the reasons why you’re seeing so much progress.”
However, he corrected a reporter near the end of the whirlwind tour, “It’s not my vision — it’s Canadians’ vision.”

Governing Violence: Criminal Networks, Adaptive Systems, and the Aftermath of El Mencho

Guadalupe Correa-Cabrera

November 21, 2026

 

The killing of Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes (“El Mencho”), leader of the Jalisco New Generation Cartel (CJNG), was celebrated as a victory against organized crime. Yet the violence that followed in the aftermath of his death exposed the limitations of equating the removal of a leader with the defeat of a criminal organization. Informed by social network analysis and complexity theory, this article examines how the CJNG combines hierarchical leadership with adaptable regional relationships, and how violence communicates power through political and media networks. It argues that enforcement tactics should investigate structurally important intermediaries rather than assume rank determines operational importance, while acknowledging that such targeting can also provoke substitution and fragmentation. Effective policy must address institutional corruption, violent narratives, and measurable consequences.

Introduction

On February 22, 2026
, Mexican authorities announced the death of 
Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes
, known as El Mencho, leader of the 
Jalisco New Generation Cartel
 (CJNG). Political authorities and media outlets quickly framed the operation as a 
strategic inflection point
 in the fight against organized crime, reaffirming a long-standing enforcement paradigm that views criminal organizations as hierarchies that can be dismantled through leadership decapitation. This interpretation, however, is difficult to reconcile with the developments that followed. Within hours, 
coordinated acts of violence
—including arson attacks, road blockades, and armed confrontations—unfolded across multiple regions of Mexico. These events did not suggest organizational disintegration; rather, they revealed continuity, coordination, and a capacity for rapid adaptation.

The contrast between the expected effects of leadership removal and the CJNG’s immediate capacity to mobilize violence raises an analytical problem. Criminal organizations need not be either rigid hierarchies or entirely decentralized networks. The CJNG combines centralized leadership with regional groups and local partners whose operational relationships may survive the loss of prominent figures. Social network analysis directs attention to the intermediaries connecting these actors; complexity theory helps explain how their relationships can change under pressure. The violence after El Mencho’s death does not prove the organization retained full capacity but shows why leadership removal is an inadequate measure of success. This article examines the CJNG’s relationships, violent signaling, and how enforcement reshapes illicit activity. It develops recommendations on intermediaries, institutional integrity, and media amplification, as well as measuring indicators of outcomes.

From Vertical Hierarchy to Complex Adaptive Systems

Although public discussion often depicts El Mencho as the CJNG’s singular center of authority, available evidence points to a more distributed organizational configuration.  The CJNG operates as a network of semi-autonomous cells embedded within local political and economic environments while maintaining connections through shared branding, logistical access, operational repertoires, and access to transnational logistical infrastructures without requiring direct central supervision of every activity. The organization still maintains 
hierarchical leadership
 while expanding through regional commanders, affiliated groups, and locally embedded partners. A leader may still retain considerable authority even when some operational functions are distributed.

This arrangement can be examined through 
social network analysis
 and 
complexity theory
. The former identifies relationships and brokerage positions that connect otherwise separate actors, while the latter examines how systems adjust to disruption through substitution, changing alliances, and redistribution of functions. In such systems, resilience is not derived from centralized command but from the capacity to redistribute functions across nodes and to adapt through iterative processes of reconfiguration. Authority is therefore diffused, and organizational continuity does not depend on the persistence of any single actor.

Reports of 
El Mencho’s kidney problems
 raised questions about his involvement in daily operations, but they do not establish that he relinquished particular responsibilities. The CJNG’s activities during his reported illness are consistent with some functions being exercised by regional actors; they cannot establish when, why, or how authority was delegated. Treating that distinction carefully strengthens rather than weakens the argument about organizational resilience. El Mencho’s death may therefore have disrupted the leadership without severing the relationships supporting regional operations. This possibility challenges strategies that treat an organization’s most prominent figure as necessarily its most indispensable component. Structural importance depends on what an actor connects or enables, not simply their rank. A financial intermediary or logistical coordinator may sometimes be more difficult to replace than a commander, although the reverse may also be true.

Violence as Signaling Within Networked Systems

The violence following El Mencho’s death was widely interpreted as 
retaliation
. That explanation is plausible, but it does not exhaust the significance of 
roadblocks, arson, and attacks
 whose images circulated far beyond their immediate locations. Such actions can obstruct state operations while communicating a claim of territorial reach. Their political effects depend partly on how authorities, journalists, and online audiences interpret and circulate those images.

Visible disruptions can create the impression that criminal actors can challenge state authority across a much wider area than the incidents themselves affect. This may weaken confidence in the state’s capacity to maintain order and its claim to a 
monopoly over the legitimate use of force
. Residents may change their movements, governments may face demands for a stronger response, and criminal groups may seek to exploit the reputation produced by highly publicized attacks. These reactions can reinforce one another without central coordination.

Violence thus operates as coercion and as communication
. Criminal groups may use spectacular acts to signal strength or discourage rivals; political leaders may invoke them to justify exceptional security measures; and security institutions and contractors may benefit from expanded mandates or spending. These incentives explain why insecurity can become politically salient and economically valuable. They do not establish that these actors deliberately coordinate the production of violence.

The temporal context of El Mencho’s death provides additional context. Occurring within a politically salient period marked by electoral cycles and major international events (such as the 
FIFA World Cup
), the amplification of violence intersected with moments in which security narratives acquire heightened political and economic significance. Under such conditions, the political and informational effects of violence extend beyond the criminal actors responsible for it.

The “War on Drugs” as a System of Adaptive Governance

The CJNG’s evolution
 also raises questions about the effects of the War on Drugs. Enforcement can remove leaders, interrupt routes, and impose real costs on criminal actors. Yet continuing demand and profits give remaining actors incentives to find alternatives. Under some conditions, repeated disruption can encourage delegation, diversification, and new alliances, producing organizations better able to absorb subsequent interventions. This is a contingent mechanism, not evidence that every operation strengthens criminal networks.

From a 
complexity perspective
, drug policy is part of the environment to which criminal groups respond. A closed corridor may redirect trafficking, while the arrest of a financial intermediary may produce a substitute relationship. These adjustments can alter local balances of power and the geography of violence. In that limited but important sense, enforcement helps shape the organization of illicit markets even when its stated objective is to dismantle them. Describing this as governance does not imply officials consciously intend those outcomes.

Militarization is a central component of this model. The 
designation of criminal organizations as terrorist entities
 and the possibility of cross-border enforcement broadens the scope of permissible intervention. At the same time, limited transparency can complicate independent assessment of official claims. The resulting system does not eradicate violence but perpetuates its production and redistribution in new forms.

The CJNG provides a concrete illustration of these organizational dynamics. Its expansion has involved 
regional commanders, affiliated criminal groups
, and relationships with local actors. Its activities extend beyond drug trafficking to extortion and other illicit markets, which depend on different financial and logistical arrangements. This diversity can create opportunities for adaptation, but it also produces points of vulnerability. The organization is neither wholly centralized nor uniformly decentralized.

The 
franchise analogy
 captures one aspect of the CJNG’s expansion: local groups may benefit from affiliation while retaining some discretion over operations. It does not imply that all affiliates are independent or that central leadership is unimportant. Rather, it directs attention to the varying degrees of control, dependence, and coordination across territories. These differences are precisely what an effective enforcement strategy needs to establish empirically.

Policy Implications: Toward Systems-oriented Approaches

If contemporary criminal organizations are best conceptualized as complex adaptive systems, 
enforcement should be recalibrated
 to focus on network structure rather than presumed organizational rank. Leadership decapitation targets visible figures, but the aftermath of El Mencho’s death suggests that the nodes most important to organizational continuity may instead be those that connect otherwise separate parts of the network. Network analysis can help identify actors and infrastructures with high betweenness or brokerage capacity—nodes that link regional cells, coordinate logistical corridors, connect illicit markets, or mediate financial and communication flows. Targeting these structurally consequential points, while continuously reassessing the network as it adapts, would align enforcement more closely with the resilience mechanisms identified in this article.

Targeting brokers differs from leadership decapitation because it selects actors according to the connections they sustain rather than their formal rank. But it is not immune to the same problems: intermediaries can be replaced, routes diverted, and violence displaced. Mexico’s 
Fiscalía General de la República (FGR)
, working with the 
Unidad de Inteligencia Financiera (UIF)
 and vetted customs investigators, could use lawfully obtained financial records, shipment data, and corroborated communications to identify actors linking otherwise separate cells. A corrupt customs intermediary connecting several groups to a port logistics chain is one illustrative possibility, not an allegation against any identified official. Because public servants may themselves be part of these relationships, investigations require restricted access, independent judicial oversight, and corroboration across institutions. Lawful cooperation with U.S. agencies can supplement, but not substitute for, credible Mexican investigations.

The signaling mechanism requires a different response. Mexican authorities should issue prompt, geographically specific accounts of verified incidents, distinguishing actual attacks from recycled footage and unsubstantiated claims. Journalists and digital platforms can add context without repeatedly circulating imagery detached from its location or date. This is not a case for censorship or minimizing harm. Accurate information can reduce the ability of criminal actors—and of sensational political narratives—to turn localized violence into an impression of generalized state collapse.

Evaluation must extend beyond the number of arrests. Investigators should track whether disrupted brokers are replaced within 90 or 180 days, whether financial and logistical connections recover, and whether verified violence increases or declines in affected and neighboring areas. These measures cannot prove causation alone but offer a better test than a prominent arrest. Assessing both operational recovery and the circulation of misleading violent narratives would test the two mechanisms identified in this article.

These recommendations also require a different measure of operational success. Removing a broker or severing a logistical connection may weaken a network, but it may also redirect flows, generate substitute relationships, or accelerate fragmentation. Policy evaluation should therefore examine whether interventions reduce the network’s capacity to reconnect critical nodes, replace lost functions, and sustain illicit activity over time, while also assessing whether official and media responses inadvertently magnify the signaling value of spectacular violence. This shifts the objective from episodic disruption toward measurable reductions in both network resilience and the broader informational effects generated by violent signaling.

Conclusion

El Mencho’s death was a major enforcement event, but the violence that followed neither proves the CJNG’s long-term resilience nor establishes its collapse. The episode instead exposes the limits of treating a leader’s removal as a proxy for organizational defeat. The more important question is whether the financial, logistical, and political relationships supporting criminal activity can be disrupted for a sustained period.

An effective response would identify consequential intermediaries while acknowledging the possibility of substitution, protect investigations from institutional compromise, and communicate verified information without amplifying criminal claims. Success should be measured through durable changes in illicit operations and violence rather than the visibility of a single operation. This approach preserves the central insight of complexity theory: enforcement acts upon a changing system whose reactions must be investigated, not assumed.

 is a Professor at the Schar School of Policy and Government at George Mason University and co-director of the Corruption, Networks, and Transnational Crime Research Center (CONTRA).

by

HAILEY SMALLEY

Daily Inter Lake

|

November 22, 2026 12:00 AM

Tucked in the back corner of her family home, Christina Williams watched the hypnotic churn of one of her many lasers. The beam itself was invisible, but the artist kept track of the pulsing heat through the scorch marks left in its wake.  
Inch by inch, a pattern emerged on the brim of the suede hat situated inside the laser engraving machine. Whispers of smoke unraveled like spools of thread as pathways collided and diverged, weaving the outline of a willow bough arched above lily pads. The laser shuttled onward, etching water ripples and then a squatting frog into the faux leather hat brim.   
"I absolutely love her," Williams said, gesturing to the laser engraving machine. "She is a workhorse."  

She needed a workhorse to keep up with her own runaway creativity. Personal projects and entrepreneurial ventures alike littered her home workshop. Cork coasters boasting the outline of a rainbow trout were stacked on one side of an L-shaped craft table, next to a rack of half-painted hats. In the nearby closet, crates of T-shirts and tumblers awaited embellishment. A woodcut ornament of a hoot owl dangled from a shelf laden with rolls of ribbons and trays of charms. 
The dizzying array of crafts forms the bedrock of Williams' homespun laser-engraving business. Under the pseudonym Montana Mountain Mama, the company has grown from a personal creative outlet to a full-fledged market of hand-crafted products, including jewelry, key chains, drinkware, craft kits and ornaments. Williams introduced the first company offshoot this month: an LLC called Wild Ember Hats, which specializes in laser-engraved, hand-decorated wide-brim hats. 
The brand builds off a family legacy of diverse artistry. Williams' mother worked as a gemologist, carving stones into fine jewelry, while her father honed his artistic eye behind a camera lens. As a child, Williams learned to knit and tie macrame from her grandmother and later dabbled in painting and photography.  
Parenthood further stoked her creative spark. Raising two daughters and four sons meant there was always a birthday cake to decorate or an outfit that needed a little patchwork. 
"You know, Jack of all trades," Williams liked to joke. "I'm his sister, Jill, having six kids." 
Her home habits morphed into a side hustle in 2019, when one of Williams' sons needed custom T-shirts for his lawncare business. The lettering on the shirts he ordered from an online vendor had begun to flake and peel off after just a few months of wear. Instead of gambling with another unknown company, Williams bought her own vinyl cutter and heat press and crafted a fresh batch by hand.  
"After that, it literally snowballed," Williams said.  
She made shirts and pennants for the adaptive sports leagues her eldest son participated in and as gifts for friends and family members. She experimented with other techniques and materials, diving into research for months before purchasing her next piece of high-tech crafting equipment. First, came a dye-sublimation printer that Williams used to further expand her t-shirt menagerie. Then, she procured a portable blue diode laser capable of etching designs into wood, aluminum and acrylic.  
In February 2026, Williams purchased her workhorse: a carbon dioxide laser engraver the approximate size and shape of an office copy machine. The engraver was more precise than her smaller model, and the enlarged bed allowed Williams to imprint her designs on a wider variety of goods, including wide- brimmed hats.  
Tucked under the glass lid, plains of soft fawn and seal-gray suede bloomed with verdant gardens and western motifs. 
Williams pulled inspiration from the owls that swooped above the back fields in springtime and the Swan Range standing sentinel on the horizon. She sketched sunflowers and songbirds. One design featured a popular Bible verse atop angel wings. Another transcribed text from a whiskey bottle in a font reminiscent of an old outlaw poster. 
The paper-and-ink pictures became digital images and then bits of code that guided the pulse of the laser. Precision was key, and Williams fine-tuned the settings of the engraver through trial and error. Every ripple and ridge in the suede needed to be accounted for. If the laser was too hot or positioned too close to the hat, it could sear straight through the faux leather. Run the laser at too slow a speed, and the lines might come out blurry and indistinct. 
Williams emphasized some of the engraved designs with touches of paint or shimmering rhinestones, but most remained spartan. She preferred to let her customers choose their own finishing touches.  
When she peddled her hats at craft shows, Williams always made sure to bring a selection of ribbons and charms. She spent time with each patron demonstrating how to swap out the band on the hat for one of another color and happily amended her own designs with any suggested additions. 
For her most individualistic customers, Williams offers a fully customized design experience. A recent consultation resulted in a Montana State University themed hat. Other clients have requested specific images or sayings. 
"It's the most amazing thing to make somebody's dream come true and hand it back to them," Williams said. "They get so excited and then I get excited because they're excited. It's contagious."   
Williams will host a booth with a selection of pre-engraved hats at the Christmas at the Fairgrounds Artisan Market, Nov. 28-30. A rotating selection of hats is also available at wildemberhatco.com. Other laser-engraved goods are available at montanamountainmama406.com.

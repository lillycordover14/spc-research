“I Know I’m More Than Just a Face": Bella Hadid Opens Up About Turning 30, Walking Victoria's Secret, and Finding Herself&nbsp;&nbsp;Harper's BAZAAR

[page text]
I first met Bella Hadid on a fashion shoot I was styling more than a decade ago. We went on to shoot several stories together, including her first major American magazine cover. On one of those shoots, she arrived upset because she had just found out she didn’t get cast in the Victoria’s Secret show. I will never forget telling Bella that one day, Victoria’s Secret would be begging her to walk for them. Cut to today, and Bella is not only one of the most in-demand models, but she opened their most recent show. When I saw her hit the runway in a 
beaded floral bra and matching wings,
 I let out a scream because I knew what that moment meant to her.
A few days after the Victoria’s Secret show in October, Bella and I sat down to reflect on her career so far, forgiveness and success, turning 30, and where she goes from here. I have always felt connected to Bella because she, like me, is a heart-on-her-sleeve kind of gal. She is deeply vulnerable and human, and it’s what makes her one of the most compelling figures in fashion today.

Samira Nasr:
 This is a full-circle moment. On one of our first shoots together, you were really upset because you didn’t get the Victoria’s Secret show. And I said to you, “One day, they will be begging you to be in their show.” And I could tell you didn’t believe me. Your whole body shut down. And three days ago, you opened Victoria’s Secret. Holy shit.
Bella Hadid:
 I still don’t believe you, to be honest. I feel like people are just AI-ing me onto the runway. I think that it’s so crazy how time passes and you still have some kind of impostor syndrome. No matter how hard you work, there’s always another girl who is more deserving, more beautiful, and has worked so hard to be able to get that spot. Ten years ago, I remember that moment too, where I never thought I would get the show, but I think that God always has a plan, and I truly believe at that time I wasn’t ready. I don’t think I’ve been ready until this year, to be honest. In past years, I was going to my horse-show finals in the morning, flying straight to the Victoria’s Secret show that day, doing rehearsal right before, and then pulling what we call a Bella, and Belinda comes out, and we just act. But this year, I felt really strong.
I have had to make a decision for myself to have boundaries with my own health. And I think it’s gotten me to this point now where I’m able to do the jobs that I do want to do with people that I love, that I can feel inspired by. And that’s truly what [working in] fashion is about: being around people you feel inspired by. Until you find that inspiration, it’s hard to go back into our industry. I think I needed a little bit of time just to be able to be like, “Let me just remember why I’m doing this again,” because I know I’m more than just a face.

SN:
 We don’t really support a culture of care. I have always believed that boundaries are bridges.
BH:
 They really are. We know so many people who have been working for 30, 40, 50 years in this industry with zero boundaries. I’ve watched people over the years just get more depressed because they don’t know themselves and they don’t know who they are. And that’s how I was for 10 years, when I didn’t know what my favorite color was, what I liked to eat. I stopped listening to music. To not be able to know yourself in an industry that tells you how to be every single day is really hard.
SN:
 Going back to Victoria’s Secret, did you feel empowered, or was that your alter ego? What is her name again?
BH:
 Belinda. You know what? Fortunately and unfortunately, I think it was Bella this year.

BH:
 I made up my alter ego, Belinda, a long time ago to deal with the intensity of everything happening in my life. So she could deal with that, and I can deal with this, and we can do this together, which I’m realizing I should probably go to therapy about again. Belinda is the one that people are like, “Wow, this, that.”
I have POTS [postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome], and I have a lot of PMDD [premenstrual dysphoric disorder], endometriosis, plus my Lyme and things that affect my body very deeply. Right before I walked out on the runway for my opening look, I went to go stretch and my whole clavicle completely … I don’t even know what it was. My lungs almost collapsed, and I couldn’t breathe, and then it was like, “Five, four …” So my body goes through these different motions of feeling really good and then all of a sudden crashing on me at the worst moments. I need to realize maybe my body can’t handle all that I have going on. Because I know how to walk. One thing I can do is I can strut. But it’s a lot of pressure; we all have our own insecurities. Sometimes my body just can’t connect with my brain. My brain wants to do it, and my body is like, “No, we can’t do that today.” And I’m like, “Okay, great. So let’s figure out another way.” But I think people don’t realize how much I struggle with the fatigue and the body pain, and so I just try my best.

SN:
 You’ve achieved so much in your 20s. You just turned 30. What are you looking forward to in your 30s?
BH:
 It’s funny, today I woke up with—I wouldn’t say the word is 
anxiety,
 but this feeling of “Oh my gosh, my life is starting.” I want to get rid of what doesn’t bring me joy and bring in what does. I’ve tried to put myself in positions for work for a very long time that have made me feel out of body or like I don’t belong. Years ago, I was just grateful to get a job, and I would say yes to everything. I lacked boundaries then, but I do believe that that gave me the strength to be able to have the confidence in my own boundaries now. I’ve achieved pretty much all the goals that I wanted to when it comes to fashion as a model. And now I do want to go into other ventures to eventually get to peace and happiness and love within this universe that God has put me in.
In the next couple of years, it’s just about getting back to community, getting back to using my voice again. I got beaten down pretty heavy for a long time, which pushed me into this very big struggle with my physical and emotional health, which makes my brain turn off and makes me not be able to write or read. When you’re not around enough people that are like-minded, you feel really ostracized. That’s almost where I got to. Of course, I always want to speak from my heart, which I hope will never disrespect anybody, but still be able to have the confidence to speak my truth and hope that people will walk beside me along the way when it comes to that.

SN:
 Is there something you’re most proud of achieving?
BH:
 Well, first and foremost, starting [my fragrance line] 
Orebella
 was something that I truly needed for my own creativity and to be able to build community. I am not a 50/50 person. I’m an all-in type of girl. Being able to move forward into my 30s, really focusing on building the things that I’ve started—Orebella was something that I pushed so hard for since 2018, and the fact that it’s really now coming to fruition makes me so proud.
When I was 17, going to my first go-see with a stylist I will not name, I was basically told I was too big to wear Saint Laurent and needed to lose a couple of pounds. And that’s when I was just a baby coming out of high school. Twelve years later, now I’m the face of Saint Laurent. I’m the face of Prada, which is incredible and insane. Times have changed so much, and the people that have come into the industry to make it a better place for us as models are so important. Not that that comment ever impacted me. I went through enough struggles with my body before that, so by the time she said that, I was like, “Okay, girl, I’m actually coming out of something that I dealt with very heavily when it came to my body. And now I look at food in a different way, and I love my body, and that’s okay if you think that I don’t look the way that you think I should look.”
I just would love for everyone to think about if every day they had to walk outside and be photographed, what that would feel like, because it’s an uncomfortable feeling for anybody. My face would change so much. I would be super puffy one day, and one day I would look yellow and emaciated, and people would think I was on drugs or doing this or that. And when anybody knows about, one, hormones, just as a woman in general, and two, about immune problems and how weight fluctuates—again, I just think that there needs to be more sensitivity in our industry.

SN:
 I’m curious if you’ve always been this person—so sensitive, empathetic—from a little girl.
BH:
 I’ve always felt like my sensitivity and my openness were a disservice to myself because I felt like no one else was like me in those ways, so I shut it down. Now I realize that there is a generosity of thoughtfulness that is so much deeper than just buying somebody an expensive gift. I think sitting on the side of the street with somebody, even if you don’t have any money, is more important than just walking by without caring.

 The last time I saw Virgil was with you, actually.

 I don’t think I’ve done a real, full Fashion Month since he passed away. And Vivienne [Westwood] and Karl [Lagerfeld] passed, and I felt like all of my idols were leaving, and it felt really scary. I have all the art Virgil gave me, his letter he wrote me, his bags, his shoes he made me. And then I have my Prada area, my Cavalli area, my 
Bob Mackies
 that I’ve been collecting over the years.

 That’s incredible.

 Hopefully for a future wedding. There are just some dresses you can’t find again. And Bob did it best.

SN:
 He’s an American icon. I wanted to talk to you about Cannes. You’re such a 
queen of Cannes
 now, and I look forward to seeing you there. Is it fun?
BH:
 This past year was fun. I felt grounded. To do the red carpet and feel, in those moments, sober and happy and healthy and strong, and go to sleep not having anxiety about going out or how I’m going to look the next day, and just being with loved ones. It did really well for me this year.
Cannes has always held a very special place in my heart. It begins long before I arrive. Working closely with designers on custom gowns, exchanging references, and watching those first sketches come to life is such a beautiful part of the experience.
This year, being there to support Marie-Clémentine Dusabejambo’s 
Ben’Imana,
 the first Rwandan film in the official selection, was especially meaningful. It explores memory and reconciliation after the genocide against the Tutsi through women whose lives carry its legacy. That’s what I love about cinema: It brings us closer and gives stories too rarely heard the attention they deserve. You can enter a theater knowing nothing of someone’s world and leave carrying a little of it with you.
SN:
 I look at you and how hard you’ve worked for so long, from our first job together. To me, it feels like you’ve achieved so much. But what does success look like for you? Do you feel you’ve achieved it?
Karim Sadli
Dress, 
Chanel.
BH:
 I talked to my business manager yesterday, so that made me feel a little bit more successful. But when you don’t think about the money part of it, I think success truly comes from peace within. At the end of the day, I really don’t need much. That’s why I do give back so much, because a lot of the money that I do make, I don’t feel deserving of. There are so many people in the world, whether it’s doctors or teachers, that I think deserve my pay rate a lot more than I do.
From an external space, success is about how known you are, how much you make, how many times you can be on TV, how many covers you’re on, how many fashion shows you do. And that is really important in our industry, especially when you’re building. But for me, I am different. Success, for me, can be living in peace, in a small town, with a man that I love, with my child, having my animals. I’ve always wanted to start a farm-to-table bakery, with flowers, and everything comes from the community. I want to do things that are fulfilling for me in my soul: having a greenhouse that I can garden in, having my glassblowing and ceramic studio, and having lots of children and being a mother. It wasn’t a dream that I’ve always had, but it’s something that now is so intense for me. My intuition is screaming so loudly at me about it.
I think knowing there is a reason behind everything is so important. My sister and I donated a million dollars to Palestine last year [the donation was split between 
HEAL Palestine,
 
Palestine Children’s Relief Fund,
 
World Central Kitchen,
 and 
United Nations Relief and Works Agency
], which we could have never been able to do without the success that we’ve had.
God gave me the platform to be able to speak about the things that I hold dearly to me. And when it comes to Palestine or Sudan or Congo or Rwanda or places that mean so much to not only myself but the people around me—to give people a voice in any capacity, that’s success for me as well. To make my family wide and far proud, or to see themselves in me, it gives me strength.

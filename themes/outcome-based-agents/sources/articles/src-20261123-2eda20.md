IntelliNews 
summary:
 
 
• S&P judges that Uzbekistan's policymaking keeps improving, with energy and state-company reforms that should help the budget.
 
 
• Net government debt is modest by global standards and consists mostly of long, cheap official loans for investment projects.
 
 
• Higher gold prices, strong tourism and steady remittances have lifted the external position despite a narrow export base.
 
 
 
 
 
____________________
 
 
S&P Global Ratings has lifted Uzbekistan's sovereign rating to 'BB+' from 'BB', one notch below investment grade, with a stable outlook, according to a 
rating report
 it published on November 20.
 
 
'BB+' is the highest speculative-grade rating on S&P's scale; the next step, 'BBB-', is investment grade.
 
 
The upgrade covers Uzbekistan's long-term foreign- and local-currency ratings and its senior unsecured debt. S&P affirmed the 'B' short-term ratings and raised its transfer and convertibility assessment, which measures the risk of the state restricting access to foreign currency, to 'BBB-' from 'BB+'.
 
 
 
 
 
Source: S&P Global Ratings, November 20, 2026; boundaries and capitals: Natural Earth. IntelliNews
 
 
'The upgrade reflects what we view as improvements in Uzbekistan's fiscal and balance-of-payments positions,' the rating agency said. In its view, energy-sector reform and changes at state-owned enterprises (SOEs) show economic policymaking getting stronger, and both should support the budget.
 
 
 
 
 
S&P now rates Uzbekistan a notch higher than the other two big rating agencies: Moody's 
raised it to Ba2
, the equivalent of 'BB', in June, and Fitch Ratings 
moved its 'BB' rating to a positive outlook
 in early June.
 
 
Tashkent has made an 
investment-grade rating
 a stated goal of its reforms, and bond investors are already there: five-year dollar spreads 
price Uzbekistan as a 'BB+' credit
, ING said on November 17.
 
 
What would move the rating
 
 
The stable outlook balances resilient growth and a favourable debt profile against Uzbekistan's heavy exposure to commodity prices, above all gold, and to uncertainty over global energy prices and geopolitics, S&P said.
 
 
A downgrade could follow if fiscal and external deficits widen beyond S&P's expectations, whether through worse terms of trade, spending not matched by revenue, dearer borrowing or unforeseen liabilities from the large SOE sector. An upgrade to investment grade would need a stronger fiscal position, for example smaller headline deficits and less on-lending to SOEs.
 
 
 
 
 
The rating is still held back by low income per head, exposure to commodity price swings and limited room for monetary policy, in S&P's assessment. Gold's weaker correlation with copper gives some hedge, but close ties with Russia through remittances and trade leave Uzbekistan open to external shocks and secondary sanctions.

Reforms and a bigger GDP
 
 
The reform drive that began in Uzbekistan in 2017 has turned to the risks that state companies pose to the budget, according to S&P. New laws regulate SOE borrowing abroad, allow ceilings on the nominal non-mineral primary deficit, limit annual public-private partnership (PPP) commitments and tighten corporate governance at state companies. With energy subsidy reform, S&P expects them to cut the need for state guarantees and capital injections into SOEs and keep deficits stable at about 3% of GDP in 2026-2029.
 
 
A statistical revision has also flattered the debt ratios: on November 13 Uzbekistan raised its estimate of 2025 nominal GDP by 15% to UZS2,127 trillion ($169bn), adding UZS277 trillion ($22bn), after better business reporting and enterprise surveys found more trade and services activity, and agricultural and population censuses raised estimates of crops, livestock and housing. The revision cuts S&P's projected average net government debt for 2026-2029 to 24% of GDP, from 30% in its previous assessment. A further rebasing may follow in 2027.
 
 
 
 
 
Gold fills the reserves
 
 
Gold makes up about 30-40% of merchandise exports and 85-90% of international reserves, S&P estimates. Record prices have helped reserves more than double since 2023 to about $64bn, or 32% of GDP, at end-October.
 
 
S&P still regards balance-of-payments risks as elevated because of that concentration and disruption to trade and geopolitics, though non-gold exports, remittances and earnings from logistics and tourism offset part of it.
 
 
The agency assumes gold falls to $4,000 in 2027 and $3,500 in 2028-2029, from $4,300 in 2026 and an average $3,400 in 2025. On that path usable reserves, which exclude the external assets of the Uzbekistan Fund for Reconstruction and Development (UFRD), peak at $62bn at end-2026 and then decline. S&P forecasts current account deficits averaging 3.4% of GDP to 2029, financed mainly by concessional foreign loans and to a lesser degree by foreign direct investment (FDI).
 
 
Debt-financed projects that fail to deliver the growth and export earnings the authorities expect, while pushing up borrowing costs, could weaken the external position over the longer term, the agency warned.
 
 
 
 
 
Fast growth, low income
 
 
Growth has averaged 6% a year since liberalisation began in 2017, and Uzbekistan has been the second-fastest-growing economy in the Commonwealth of Independent States over the past decade, S&P said. The economy grew 8.3% y/y in the first three quarters of 2026, against 8.0% a year earlier, led by information and communication, construction, tourism and trade.
 
 
S&P forecasts growth of 8.0% this year and an average 6.7% in 2027-2029, carried by household demand and state investment in energy, mining and infrastructure. Investment runs at about 36% of GDP, one of the highest ratios in the world. Zero customs duties and regulated prices on key consumer goods are likely to lift consumption.
 
 
Income is the weak spot: S&P puts GDP per head at about $5,200 in 2026, up from $2,365 in 2019 but still low by global standards.
 
 
About a quarter of the population that is approaching 40mn people works in agriculture, which accounts for about 15% of the economy, and almost 90% of Uzbeks are of working age or younger. S&P doubts job creation will keep pace, so it expects many to keep looking for work in Russia and new destinations in Europe and Asia, leaving the economy dependent on remittances.
 
 
 
 
 
PPPs have grown fast: signed contracts reached an estimated 21% of GDP in 2025, and the government has approved a further pipeline of about $30bn for 2024-2030, 93% of it in energy. The authorities want renewables to supply 54% of the energy mix by 2030, up from about 20% now. A new PPP framework caps public commitments at $6.5bn in 2026, and the authorities put direct and contingent PPP liabilities at about 15% of GDP.
 
 
Iran, Russia and sanctions
 
 
S&P expects the Middle East war to hit Uzbekistan mainly through higher energy and transport costs, with a contained overall effect. Hydrocarbon imports reached $3.7bn in 2025, 8% of all imports, mostly from Russia, Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan. Direct trade with the countries caught up in the war is under 3% of the total, but before the war Iran carried 8% of Uzbek imports and 4% of exports in transit.
 
 
Trade has since shifted to the Middle Corridor across the Caspian, raising costs for producers and prompting a temporary subsidy for the meat industry worth about 0.1% of GDP. Investment from the UAE and Saudi Arabia, about $3bn or 10% of total FDI and mostly in renewables, has not been delayed so far, and Uzbekistan gains from higher world prices for fertilisers, which make up 2% of its exports.
 
 
 
 
 
Remittances rose 28% y/y to $18.9bn in 2025, or 11.2% of GDP, helped by the stronger ruble, wages and labour demand in Russia, which sends 78% of the money, and by digital transfer routes. Trade with Russia grew about 8% to $13.0bn. S&P expects a Russia-Ukraine ceasefire to have a contained effect on Russia-linked trade and capital flows through Uzbekistan.
 
 
The EU sanctioned two Uzbek firms in April 2026 for reportedly supplying cellulose and chemical inputs to Russia's military sector. S&P sees no significant direct impact on the economy or public finances, but the risk of further Western secondary sanctions on Uzbek companies trading with Russia persists.
 
 
Institutions remain weak by comparison, in S&P's view. The 2023 constitution extended the presidential term to seven years from five and allows President Shavkat Mirziyoyev to stay in office until 2037. Decision-making is centralised, checks and balances are limited and corruption, though perceived to be improving, remains high against similarly rated peers.
 
 
 
 
 
Debt rising from a low base
 
 
The budget deficit narrowed to 1.8% of GDP in 2025 after the rebasing. Record gold prices lift corporate income tax, mining tax and dividends, which together supply about 30% of revenue. Higher power and gas tariffs cut energy subsidies to $0.6bn (0.4% of GDP) in 2025 from $1.5bn (1.2% of GDP) in 2023. S&P forecasts deficits averaging about 3.0% of GDP in 2026-2029, slightly below the 3.5% average of 2021-2024.
 
 
Debt has grown faster than the deficits suggest. Reported deficits averaged 3% of GDP in 2020-2025, while net government debt rose by about 5% of GDP, as spending ran through guarantees and on-lending via SOEs and a weaker currency inflated debt that is about 84% in foreign currency. S&P projects net general government debt at about 26% of GDP by 2029, from a net asset position in 2017. Its figures include guaranteed debt of about 3.4% of GDP in 2025, and a further 5.5% of GDP in non-guaranteed state-company debt could end up on the sovereign's books.
 
 
 
 
 
Most of the debt is cheap: official lenders hold 83% of external debt, on concessional terms and long maturities. The share of local-currency debt rose to 16% of the total in 1H26 from 9% in 2022. More domestic and commercial foreign borrowing will push interest payments to about 3.6% of government revenue in 2026-2029, from 0.6% in 2019, which S&P still considers low among emerging markets.
 
 
Liquid government assets, mostly held in the UFRD, fell to 7% of GDP in 2025 from 33% in 2017.
 
 
Prices, banks and the stock market
 
 
S&P expects inflation to average 6.7% in 2026, below the 11% average of 2020-2025. Household inflation expectations dipped below 10% in August, against an average of 13% in 2025. Dollarisation is falling but still high: about 39% of loans and 19% of deposits were in dollars at July 1, which weakens the transmission of interest rates.
 
 
The May IPO of the National Investment Fund (UzNIF), managed by Franklin Templeton, was a milestone for capital markets in S&P's view. The fund holds minority stakes in 13 large state companies in utilities, energy, banking, telecoms and transport, and its dual listing in London and Tashkent raised about $690mn (0.4% of GDP) from international investors.
 
 
 
 
 
State banks held 61% of banking assets at October 1, which S&P says, together with preferential state lending programmes, blunts monetary policy. Privatisation is slower than planned: after the sale of Ipoteka Bank in 2023, the authorities plan to sell four or five state banks by 2030, including SQB and Asakabank. The central bank has tightened limits on consumer lending, and S&P forecasts credit growth of 14-18% a year in 2027-2029.

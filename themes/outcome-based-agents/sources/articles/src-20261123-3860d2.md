CAPITAL IDEAS: How a step-up in basis affects inherited assets

A step-up in basis is a reset of an inherited asset's value for calculating capital gains taxes. Specifically, the new cost basis becomes its market value at the time the previous owner died.

Written by
 
Allen Harris

November 23, 2026
 

Courtesy of K-Love.

Quick Summary

An inheritance can erase decades of taxable gains.
 A “step-up in basis” generally resets an inherited asset’s cost basis to its market value when the owner dies.

Future appreciation can still be taxable.
 Simply inheriting or keeping an inherited asset generally does not trigger federal income tax.

Giving assets away during life can produce a different result.
 Recipients generally take over the giver’s existing basis. Trust provisions and estate inclusion can also affect whether assets qualify for a basis adjustment.

Coordinate investment decisions with estate planning.
 Review what assets originally cost, their current values, and whether you intend to sell, gift, or leave them to heirs. Inherited assets can also receive a lower basis if their value falls. Tax savings matter, but they should not dictate keeping an investment or property that no longer fits your family’s needs.

What is a step-up in basis anyway? First, “basis,” sometimes known as the “cost basis,” is the amount the IRS considers to be your overall investment in an asset. For example, if you bought a stock for $20,000—no matter when you bought it, how much it is worth at any time, or how much you sold it for—the “basis” is $20,000. So, if it grows to $100,000, you’ve got an $80,000 gain you’re going to be taxed on.

A step-up in basis is a reset of an inherited asset’s value for calculating capital gains taxes. Specifically, the new cost basis becomes its market value at the time the previous owner died.

The person receiving the inheritance wants a 
step-up in basis
 because it can reduce their tax obligation. If the property has gone up in value, the increase during the previous owner’s lifetime is removed from the heir’s capital gains calculation. Any future increase in value can still be taxable.

(Pro note: As stated earlier, “basis” is the amount the IRS considers to be your overall investment in an asset. I simplified it by equating the basis to the purchase price, which is often the case. However, 
basis can change
 with certain expenses or adjustments, such as commissions, reinvestment, or transfer fees.)

Suppose your mother paid $20,000 for a stock. It is worth $100,000 when she dies and leaves it to you. Your new basis generally becomes $100,000. Sell it for that amount, and you have no capital gain. Sell it later for $110,000, and your gain is $10,000.

Courtesy of Berkshire Money Management.

The $80,000 increase during your mother’s ownership has disappeared from your capital gains calculation. The step-up changes how much basis you subtract from the sale price to calculate the gain. A step-up in basis can also apply to inherited real estate.

The house Dad bought in 1965

Let’s consider a brother and sister, Daniel and Sarah. Their father bought his house in 1965 for $30,000. Let’s also assume that when he dies, its fair market value is $1 million. Further, each sibling receives 50 percent ownership of the house.

To keep things simple, let’s assume Dad’s adjusted basis is still $30,000. We’ll skip improvements, depreciation, selling expenses, etc.

When Dad passes, the house’s basis is stepped up from $30,000 to $1 million. Daniel’s half has a $500,000 basis, and Sarah’s half has a $500,000 basis.

If they keep the house, they usually will not owe federal income tax just for inheriting it. They can leave it empty, use it themselves, or rent it out (but rental income has its own tax rules).

Let’s suppose Daniel and Sarah sell the house for $1 million—the same price as the new cost basis. Each has $500,000 of sale proceeds and $500,000 in basis. Each would owe zero capital gains tax under our assumptions.

Courtesy of Berkshire Money Management.

If they sell the house later for $1.2 million, each gets $600,000 and subtracts their $500,000 basis. That means each has a $100,000 gain. The step-up removed the larger gain from Dad’s ownership, but the extra $200,000 that grew while the children owned the house is still taxable.

Courtesy of Berkshire Money Management.

Compare that with receiving the same house with Dad’s old basis. That can happen if he gives it to the children during life or transfers it to a trust whose assets remain outside his gross estate when he dies. In those circumstances, 
the original basis generally carries forward
.

Courtesy of Berkshire Money Management.

Without the step-up, just keeping the house does not create a capital gains tax bill. However, it means there is a much bigger potential gain if the house is sold in the future.

If they sell the house for $1 million, the difference is $970,000 in total taxable gain (or $485,000 each, in this example). Of course, this is just the gain; the actual tax savings from a stepped-up basis depend on their respective tax rates.

Why a gift can have a different tax result

A bequest is a transfer at death through a will. Qualifying inheritances can also pass through trusts or beneficiary designations. They generally receive a basis adjustment to the value at death, regardless of whether a will directs the transfer.

When you give someone an appreciated asset during your life, your adjusted basis usually transfers to them—not the stepped-up basis. This is called carryover basis. (Usually, there is 
no federal income tax
 just for getting a gift, though the person giving the gift may need to report it for gift tax.)

Suppose Dad gives Daniel and Sarah the house one year before he dies. If this is a completed gift and he keeps no rights that would bring the house back into his estate, his death later does not restore the lost step-up. (Carryover basis has exceptions, but we’re not going to complicate this further by discussing those here.)

If you are deciding what to give your family now, giving cash or investments that have not grown much in value may give you more flexibility than giving away something that has increased a lot over many years.

Other rules can also affect potential tax savings

The basis adjustment can also decrease. For example, if Mom bought stock for $100,000 but it is worth $70,000 when she dies, the heir’s basis is usually $70,000. The heir does not get to keep Mom’s $30,000 unrealized loss.

You usually do not have to wait a year to sell an inherited asset to get long-term capital gains treatment. Qualifying inherited assets 
get this treatment
 no matter how long the heir has held them.

Consider the cost basis and value of your assets, then consider what happens if you give them away, sell them, or leave them to heirs. Tax benefits should help guide your decision, but they should not make you keep an investment or a house your family does not want.

Beyond traction: How adaptive shoe tech could complement falls prevention strategies&nbsp;&nbsp;McKnight's Senior Living

[page text]
Left: An older adult uses the adaptive footwear researched in the study. Right: The footwear. (Photos courtesy of Cadense)                            

Footwear has the potential to address persistent barriers to traditional falls-prevention approaches, according to the results of new research.

The authors of a study 
published last month
 in the journal 
Assistive Technology 
suggested that adaptive footwear could become a practical complement to existing mobility and rehabilitation strategies and wellness programs in senior living communities.

The paper, involving adaptive footwear company 
Cadense
, examined the biomechanics and performance of the patented variable friction technology. The researchers evaluated how the technology responded to unintended toe contact during walking and explored potential applications in rehabilitation for people recovering from strokes.

For people living with mobility challenges, an unexpected toe catch can disrupt their movement and lead to trip-related risks, according to the investigators. The study highlighted a different approach to footwear design: addressing the mechanics of an unintended scuff rather than simply maximizing traction.

In lab simulated scuff testing, conventional footwear produced 77 toe-catch or trip events, compared with none with the variable friction shoe. The technology also produced lower tripping events across five common walking surfaces, including hardwood, concrete and carpet, according to the researchers.

In an additional pilot test involving three individuals who had stroke-related foot drop, all of them increased their walking speed and walked farther during the six-minute walk test following a two-week trial. At the one-year follow-up, all three participants said they were still using the variable friction shoes as their primary footwear.

A fall-prevention approach?

Traditional falls prevention programs are effective, but they face practical challenges, including requiring ongoing participation in exercise, rehabilitation or changes to the home environment, according to Cadense Vice President of Clinical Nathan Estrada, DPT.

“Footwear presents an interesting opportunity, because it travels with the person,” he said. “Rather than requiring someone to remember another exercise or change how they walk, we can design footwear that responds to a common contributor to falls: unexpected contact between the toe and the ground.”

With variable friction footwear, Estrada said, that contact can produce a controlled glide rather than an abrupt stop. He said he sees the footwear as a complementary intervention, not a replacement for physical therapy, strengthening or environmental modifications. 

Although footwear has been studied for decades in relation to balance, stability and falls prevention, most research focused on improving traction, changing shoe geometry or providing additional support, according to Cadense Chief Technology Officer and co-founder Tyler Susko, PhD, who was one of the researchers on the study. 

What makes the company’s approach different, he said, was recognizing that there are moments in walking when less friction is beneficial. The technology is designed to reduce resistance during light toe contact while maintaining traction when someone places their weight on the shoe.

Adaptive footwear in senior living

Estrada said that the walking challenges addressed by the technology are relevant to senior living because many older adults experience reduced foot clearance, shuffling or changes in walking mechanics related to aging, neurologic conditions or decreased mobility.

“In a senior living community, I see adaptive footwear as something rehabilitation and wellness teams can introduce as part of an individualized mobility plan,” he said. “Residents might use the shoes during walking activities, rehabilitation or exercise programs, with the opportunity to carry that support into everyday activities, such as walking to meals, attending social events, or getting out into the broader community.”

One of the most important lessons for senior living providers from the study, Estrada said, is that falls prevention should look beyond balance and strength alone. Although senior living operators have made their communities safer by addressing environmental hazards, improving light and reducing opportunities for trips and falls, they don’t have the same ability to manage the cracked sidewalk outside the community, an uneven parking lot, or other surfaces residents encounter when they leave the community, he said.

“Rather than relying entirely on making the environment safer, we can also provide something that travels with the resident, helping address a common walking risk wherever they go,” Estrada said. “For operators, this introduces another potential tool alongside rehabilitation, exercise, environmental safety and existing fall prevention programs.”

Rehabilitation and wellness teams, he added, can help identify residents who are experiencing frequent toe scuffing or reduce foot clearance, and they can have conversations with residents about whether variable friction footwear might be appropriate.

Next steps

The study established mechanical and early clinical evidence for variable friction footwear for a rehabilitation outcome on walking. Susko said that a larger, four-year clinical trial supported by the National Institutes of Health is underway to look at rehabilitation outcomes.

Before the study, Cadense collaborated on another study with the 
Shirley Ryan AbilityLab
, a not-for-profit physical medicine and rehabilitation research hospital in Chicago, to assess the effects of the shoes on people who are prone to falls. That study found an 87% reduction in falls. Susko said that plans exist to follow up that research by studying people who are at high risk for falls, before and after they wear the adaptive footwear.

Estada added that the company plans to move beyond clinical studies into real-world testing to objectively assess falls, walking speed, endurance, mobility and participation over time. In addition, the form is advancing research examining fall-related healthcare system use and costs.

“Ultimately, we want to understand not just whether the technology changes the mechanics of a step, but whether those changes translate into meaningful improvements in everyday life and reduced fall-related injuries,” Estrada said.

IT leaders aren’t sold on outcome-based software pricing&nbsp;&nbsp;CIO

[page text]
AI and traditional software vendors have begun experimenting with pricing models tied to outcomes, but IT leaders largely remain wary of the change.

Some 
SaaS providers
 have explored outcome-based pricing 
due to competition
 from AI-based tools, but IT leaders say the emerging cost model leaves them with many questions. CIOs say sticking to budgets may be more difficult with outcome-based agreements than traditional software pricing models, and the new approach may set up huge disagreements over defining good outcomes between customers and vendors.

Although outcome-based pricing hasn’t caught on yet, IT leaders should be prepared for its nuances and challenges, says Manuj Aggarwal, founder and CIO at AI software vendor TetraNoodle Technologies.

“Outcomes are the right north star, but pricing every seat on an agreed outcome is hard when the outcome depends on people, data quality, and process change the vendor does not control,” he says.

An 
outcome-based approach
 forces vendors to define success in business language, not feature lists, something Aggarwal sees as an advantage. When the outcome is measurable, attributable, and mostly in the vendor’s control, outcome pricing can be a strong incentive to deliver, he says.

But outcome-based pricing will make software budgets unpredictable, Aggarwal adds.

“Attribution fights start — ‘Was that the model, the data, or the team?’” he says. “Teams game metrics, and finance loses a clean forecast. If the outcome needs human judgment under pressure, a pure outcome contract pushes risk onto the CIO without giving control.”

Solid idea, questionable result

Sachin Gupta, senior IT manager at a global electronics distribution company, likes the idea of outcome-based incentives, but he’s wary of the billing model.

“Outcomes are absolutely the right way to judge software,” he says. “Nobody buys a planning tool for the licenses; they buy it for better forecasts and fewer stockouts. So I understand why CEOs and CFOs like hearing, ‘You only pay when it works.’”

But attribution for outcomes is a major challenge, he adds. “Say forecast accuracy goes up eight points after we roll out an AI tool,” he says. “Was that the tool? The master data cleanup we did the same quarter? Or just a calmer demand cycle?”

At many enterprises, outcomes come from data, processes, and several systems working together, Gupta adds. “Pinning the result on one vendor is rarely clean, and that’s where the arguments start,” he says.

In addition, the outcome depends on who’s doing the measuring, he notes. “If the vendor’s own dashboard defines the outcome, they’re grading their own homework,” he explains. “Any outcome clause I sign has to be measured in our system of record, against a baseline we agree on before go-live.”

One of the main benefits of outcome-based pricing is that it gives customers a lower barrier to entry because they don’t have to commit to a big license contract before seeing results, says Andy Boettcher, CIO of AI and data infrastructure consultancy DoubleTrack.

But he also sees outcome-based pricing as a half-baked trend, particularly when pushed by AI vendors.

“It’s a marketing buzzword that could technically work, but only if the outcome is defined precisely and the effort that it takes doesn’t get spent,” he says. “And one of the most common failures with AI I see is that it fails having a clearly defined purpose that has no clear answer to ‘What do you actually want to accomplish with this?’”

The downside of the approach is everything that happens after the lower barrier to entry to AI software, Boettcher says.

“AI systems don’t answer the same way twice because they’re running different processing and logic with their equations — and models change and update all the time,” he adds. “You’re paying for outcomes that depend on many factors you can only control so much, and there’s significant risk.”

Idea hasn’t taken off

The good news for those IT leaders skeptical of outcome-based pricing is that it’s still in the early proof-of-concept stage. While AI vendors are moving from per-seat pricing to other models, including consumption and output pricing, outcome-based pricing represents a small percentage of the market, says Josh Sandberg, partner at management consulting firm Bain & Co.

Only about 10% of AI-native software vendors and seat-based SaaS vendors that have introduced hybrid pricing now rely on outcome-based meters, Bain & Co. says in 
an August blog post
 coauthored by Sandberg.

Because of the costs involved in delivering AI solutions, 
vendors have looked to tie pricing
 to some kind of usage metric, including outcomes, but use-based models can create headaches for customers, Sandberg says.

“Anytime you start talking about usage-based, you get into all these challenges of a budget predictability and forecastability,” he explains. “All of that gets even worse when you start talking about outcome-based pricing because it does get very tricky to come up with a good baseline for outcome-based pricing.”

While outcomes are the ultimate goal, they are difficult to pin down, he adds.

“To actually agree on what a real outcome is and how do you actually attribute that outcome to a software product that you’re buying, as opposed to many, many other confounding variables in the business, starts to introduce a whole lot of complexity into the buying process and into the budgeting process for customers,” he says.

Outcome-based pricing for AI tools has taken off in only one industry vertical, contact centers, Sandberg adds. Resolution of a customer contact is a relatively straightforward determination, he suggests.

A second potential use case for outcome-based pricing involves software packages that directly touch dollar flows, such as procurement or cloud infrastructure management, he says. “Vendors will say, ‘We can actually track baseline, and here’s what you would have spent because we’re actually measuring the dollars that are coming in and out, and we can measure the decisions that our software suggests in order to save you money,’” he adds.

Despite the limited use cases so far, Sandberg expects outcome-based pricing to slowly expand as vendors continue to experiment.

“It will be very use-case specific where one vendor has to go first and work through enough of these nuances and details to really get to a customer and industry accepted definition of what that outcome looks like, and then you see a tipping point,” he adds.

A good contracting process can also achieve similar benefits for software customers as the goals of outcome-based pricing, he notes.

“Think about doing a POC and making sure that you as a buyer are actually seeing value before you commit to a longer-term contract,” he says. “There are a lot of other contracting best practices that can help deliver some of the same goals that you would want to get as a buyer out of outcome-based pricing, but maybe with a little bit less of the incremental complexity.”

    UNC is not ADA compliant for digital accessibility, faculty raise concerns

    Chelea Porter, UNC’s head of digital accessibility, says UNC could already face a lawsuit under other accessibility laws if a complaint were raised.

Caden Yao
, 
Staff Writer
                 
             •  
            
November 23, 2026
 

 

Wren Silman
 

Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act requires UNC to comply with accessibility guidelines for digital content by April 26, 2027 — bringing new attention to issues that the University could already be liable for. Faculty members have acknowledged the importance of these guidelines but also raised concerns about the increased workload they may present. 

This comes as UNC 
settled
 18 federal civil rights investigations in October. The reported violations fell under Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, which require compliance from institutions that receive federal funding, as well as the Americans with Disabilities Act. 

Chelsea Porter, UNC’s head of digital accessibility, said the guidelines apply to all state and local government-funded institutions. The rule was initially established by the U.S. Department of Justice in 2024, codifying digital accessibility into the ADA. Its deadline was set for April 26, 2026, before being extended to 2027, as of April. 

Porter said the requirements include accurate captions for videos and alternative text on images for students with screen-reading devices.

She clarified that the University is already held to many other existing accessibility standards — including Sections 508 and 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 — under which it could theoretically face a lawsuit already.

Section 508 requires federal agencies to make their electronic and information technology accessible to people with disabilities, while Section 504 prohibits disability-based discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance from the U.S. Department of Education.

The new guidelines would primarily bring more public attention to any existing violations, rather than establish new punitive methods. Porter explained that as a civil rights law, the ADA is not actively policed but rather relies on individuals making complaints to launch investigations.

Porter said that if there were to be an ADA complaint, UNC would likely already be able to demonstrate progress made toward meeting the standards.

Biology professor and Faculty Executive Committee representative Mark Peifer said he believes digital accessibility is essential, and he’s trying to improve in ways like posting recordings of lectures with captions.

However, Peifer said he was caught off guard when Porter reminded faculty of the April deadline during a recent Faculty Council meeting, which he said was news to him.

He acknowledged he might have been told in the past, as faculty receive hundreds of emails a day and he may have missed any prior notice.

“What I heard at the Faculty Council meeting was the first thing I had internalized and understood about there being a deadline, and that that meant that I had some responsibilities,” he said.

Porter said her office had sent out emails and Canvas announcements to faculty since they first learned of the deadline, as well as a recent report to deans about their departments’ accessibility scores. However, she said communication can still be a struggle with tens of thousands of students and faculty on campus.

Peifer said when he recently opened Canvas’ Panorama tool — which scans for potential accessibility issues — he saw more than 200 issues pop up in one PowerPoint. Fixing that could take weeks’ worth of full workdays, he said.

Porter said she understands the challenges professors might face when trying to make their content accessible, adding that Panorama is like a “handhold wizard” that can help guide them through the process.

“But I understand if they are teaching multiple classes, it could easily be hundreds of documents, and I get that that could be daunting,” she said.

Porter said it would be a huge help if professors could take the time they have to go through Panorama and try to fix a couple of things.

She said her office is also here to help, comparing them to customer service workers of digital accessibility, and suggested that instructors might be able to get work-study students or teaching assistants to help as well.

She also said a large part of making improvements will be applying this knowledge going forward, as it’s much easier to make new content accessible having learned the tools to do so, rather than trying to retroactively fix past material.

Peifer said he is planning to reach out to Porter’s office in addition to coordinating with his team in the biology department.

Peifer also said he was concerned due to a lack of clarity regarding the consequences of the deadline.

Peifer related those confusions to broader communication challenges this year, saying the provost’s and deans’ policy changes often don’t “trickle down” to ordinary faculty, especially those who do not attend Faculty Council meetings. He said that a recent Collaborative on Academic Careers in Higher Education (COACHE) faculty survey suggested concerns around a lack of transparency from administration.

Porter said she hopes to draw attention to resources like the Digital Accessibility website and awareness training module in Canvas, which is open to both students and faculty. She said students may also use the site to report digital accessibility issues so the office knows to reach out and help instructors who need it.

UNC first-year Maxwell Tan hadn’t heard about the University’s digital accessibility tools and resources before. While Tan said he likely wouldn’t need the tools himself, he could imagine their benefits for other students.

“Just having the access to those kinds of accessibility resources would be useful for someone who might not even know that they need it,” Tan said.

Porter said she hopes students and faculty know about the resources available to them in making the University more accessible.

“It’s a giant iceberg, and we all have these tiny little pickaxes, and we’re all working at it together,” Porter said.

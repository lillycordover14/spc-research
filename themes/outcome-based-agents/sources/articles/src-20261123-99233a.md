Top-landing is a challenging feat in which paragliders use thermals and updrafts to gain altitude, eventually landing in the same place they took off from. 

In Bellingham, Washington, Grayson Luther concluded a sunset flight with a successful top-landing.

This launch site is “well known for getting to soar around with bald eagles,” Luther said. 

“I got to thermal around and share the sky with some beautiful birds,” he said. “I remember getting to top-land back where I launched, which was super cool to be able to crush that, which is kind of hard to do, and like ‘I’m back at it and flying again.'”

But this was no ordinary flight. It marked Luther’s return to the skies after a speedflying accident left him paralyzed. 

“It was super scary,” Luther said. “I was paralyzed fully. I couldn’t move at all.” 

With exceptional grit, hard work and support, Luther learned to adapt to life with his spinal cord injury, and eventually paraglide again. 

Nine months into rehabilitation, Luther regained his ability to walk again using ankle-foot orthoses, which provides ankle support and stability, and forearm crutches. It was at that point that he knew he wanted to return to the skies. 

“I knew at that point I definitely wanted to fly again,” Luther said.

Unlike most other injuries, recovery from a spinal cord injury can be a lifelong process. When Luther is in the air, however, it feels like nothing has changed. 

“Nothing’s really different for me once I am in the air, because it’s all in your weight shifting and in your arms, which I didn’t lose a ton too,” Luther said. “So once I’m in the air, I feel like the playing field is even.”

The High Fives Foundation, an organization dedicated to preventing life-changing injuries and supporting those who experience them, reached out to Luther just days after his accident. 

A year later, Luther attended a High Fives camp, riding dirt bikes with other adaptive and professional athletes through Return to Dirt, a part of High Fives. 

Since then, High Fives has played a significant role in helping Luther return to the skies. The foundation has provided numerous grants, specialized skiing equipment, a trike for taking off on a paraglider, and a distinctive High Fives-branded paraglider often seen soaring above Glenwood Springs. 

After years of receiving support from High Fives, Luther has begun giving back to the organization. He now takes people with spinal cord injuries, traumatic brain injuries and intellectual disabilities on paragliding flights, sharing the freedom he experienced when he returned to flying. 

“It’s been super cool and full circle to start giving back to High Fives,” Luther said. “That’s kind of huge, because they’re the ones that are making all of this happen.”

For Luther and other adaptive athletes, High Fives offers more than equipment and opportunities. It also fosters a tight-knit community of people who understand the challenges of living with disabilities.

“After having firsthand experience with a spinal cord injury and a disability and what that means, now I’ve full force been able to start sharing the love and giving back and connecting on so many levels with all these other people that have to live on the daily with these circumstances,” Luther said. 

Luther also credits Chris Santacroce, founder of Project Airtime, and Pine Pienaar, owner of Adventure Paragliding in Glenwood Springs, with playing integral roles in his return to flight.

For Luther, the opportunity to fly again — and now share that experience with others — is difficult to put into words. 

“It’s like no other feeling,” Luther said. “It’s super hard to explain flying, and once someone experiences the freedom of free flight, it’s hard to go back, and it’s super addicting, and it’s definitely taken over my life.”

Bret Taylor Bets Sierra Can Become the AI Front Door for Big Business

Bret Taylor, chairman of OpenAI and co-founder of Sierra, describes plans to sell AI agents to large companies, argues that context will separate brands after intelligence becomes common, and acknowledges that enterprise deployments and revenue durability remain untested.
Bret Taylor, chairman of OpenAI and co-founder of Sierra, says AI agents will become the digital front door for companies. He says growth in AI does not guarantee durable revenue.

Taylor helped build Google Maps and FriendFeed, the latter credited with the Like button and real-time social feeds. He sold Quip to Salesforce and served as co-CEO there. He chaired Twitter during Elon Musk's purchase. He chairs OpenAI. Taylor co-founded Sierra in 2023 with Clay Bavor. Sierra, his third startup, has reached a valuation above $30 billion after a new funding round. Taylor says Sierra added $150 million in annual recurring revenue in the last three months.

Sierra sells AI agents to large companies. The agents answer customer questions and complete tasks. Taylor says the company targets the biggest 2,000 companies in the world, because those firms have millions of customers and cannot afford human support for each one.

Taylor told Reid Hoffman in 2024 that "the greatest achievement for an entrepreneur is to create a company and a brand that outlives you." FriendFeed and Quip did not survive as independent companies. Facebook bought FriendFeed and shut it down. Salesforce bought Quip and shut it down. Sierra is the furthest Taylor has taken a company of his own.

Taylor says every company's digital front door will become an AI agent. He means one agent can power a website and a phone line. He points to Next, a U.K. retailer, where Sierra runs one agent across 83 countries and different channels.

Sierra's pitch rests on context. Taylor argues that model intelligence will become cheap and common, so brands cannot win on intelligence. They win by using data from purchases and service calls. Each agent interaction adds to that record. A conversational commerce agent learns why a customer bought unicorn candles for a daughter's birthday. The next interaction can use that detail.

Personal agents such as Muse and Instinct have gained attention in the past few months. Dots belongs to the same wave. These products act on behalf of consumers and can rebook flights before the customer asks. Taylor compares them to aggregators such as search engines and app stores. Companies will face a choice: build a first-party agent or rely on third-party agents that do not share their interests. Taylor showed a video of Muse filling out a mortgage application with an agent Sierra built for Rocket Mortgage.

Taylor recommends that brands keep a strong first-party agent. He says customers talk to companies that earn the right through brand equity. Retailers such as Walmart and Nike have earned space on his phone. Most retailers reach customers through search. Personal agents could repeat that pattern.

The interview exposes limits. Taylor describes a Sonos customer service call that moved from a Sierra agent to a privacy notice and a hold queue. The call ended with a hang-up. Taylor uses the example to argue that enterprise deployments depend on the customer's process, not Sierra's software alone.

Taylor's claims about revenue durability remain unverified. Sierra selects its own case studies. Figure, a digital lender, reported conversion at 140% of its previous rate. Uber uses Sierra agents for driver and courier onboarding. These are company-reported figures, not independent audits.

Taylor says Sierra's contracts and client relationships create durable revenue. Large companies switch vendors at lower rates than small businesses, because purchases go through more review. That logic matches common B2B patterns, but the AI agent market has not existed long enough to test it.

Taylor chairs OpenAI's board. He says his work at Sierra and OpenAI does not conflict, because engineers sort themselves between applied AI and AGI research. He points to an incident he calls the Hugging Face incident, which occurred during model training and changed his view of risk. Before that incident, he focused on deployment-phase alignment. After it, he treats training as an equal stakeholder in safety work. He says alignment must advance as a science alongside model development.

Taylor's record includes failures. At Facebook, the company built its mobile app in HTML5 instead of native code. Mark Zuckerberg called the decision the company's biggest mistake in 2012 and said it cost two years. The app received a 1.5-star rating. At Salesforce, Taylor became co-CEO in 2021 instead of sole successor. He left in 2022. Marc Benioff distanced himself from the Slack acquisition Taylor orchestrated and said he knew nothing about Sierra.

Taylor says he has felt intense stress before product launches, including at FriendFeed's launch, when he had pulled all-nighters and doubted the product. He compares pre-launch anxiety to starting blocks in track.

Taylor argues that software may lose value as AI lowers the cost of writing code. Features that took years to build can be generated in hours. Sierra has thrown out guardrail code after newer models followed instructions. That change pushes companies to decide what to keep and what to discard.

Taylor's larger claim is that AI will reshape the relationship between a business and its customer. If agents become the interface, context and trust become the product. Sierra's ability to turn that claim into an enduring company remains open. Taylor has not done that before. Sierra's next test is keeping customers as models and competitors change.

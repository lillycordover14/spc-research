On November 22, 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration announced that federal and international regulatory agencies will convene on December 3, 2026, for the fifth session of the international 
Eco-NAMs Webinar Series
, focusing on enhancing the regulatory application of ecotoxicological Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship models to replace vertebrate fish and aquatic animal testing across chemical and pharmaceutical safety dossiers.

Regulatory Coalition Convenes on Computational Ecotoxicology

The upcoming technical session brings together an unprecedented coalition of international authorities to establish common scientific standards for non-animal ecotoxicity assessments. Co-organized by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, the European Medicines Agency, the Health and Environmental Sciences Institute (
HESI Global
), Japan’s National Institute for Environmental Studies, and the PETA Science Consortium International, the Eco-NAMs forum operates as a quarterly venue designed to bridge computational innovation and statutory safety reviews.

Scheduled for December 3, 2026, from 8:00 a.m. to 9:30 a.m. EST, the fifth webinar addresses one of the most persistent bottlenecks in environmental safety evaluation: gaining formal regulatory acceptance for in silico algorithms that predict chemical toxicity in freshwater and marine environments. While 
computational QSAR models
 have gained substantial ground in human health hazard identification, their routine use in ecological risk assessment has faced stricter validation hurdles due to biological variability across diverse aquatic species.

The session features two distinguished European regulatory scientists who have spearheaded cross-jurisdictional computational frameworks. Dr. Edoardo Carnesecchi, a leading cheminformatician at the European Food Safety Authority, will present on international harmonization criteria under the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. He will be joined by Dr. Salvador Moncho Escrivà, Regulatory Officer at the European Chemicals Agency, who will present operational case studies showing how algorithmic hazard evaluations are executed in statutory review settings.

Methodological Standards and the OECD Assessment Framework

A central pillar of the regulatory session is the formal operationalization of the OECD QSAR Assessment Framework, known as the QAF. Developed under the OECD’s Series on Testing and Assessment, the framework sets explicit principles that predictive models and their generated outputs must satisfy to support statutory decisions. Dr. Carnesecchi will detail how regulators evaluate predictive credibility by measuring structural applicability domains, mechanistic transparency, and statistical goodness-of-fit across heterogeneous chemical inventories.

To demonstrate these validation principles in active hazard reviews, Dr. Moncho Escrivà will showcase the practical implementation of the 
OECD QSAR Toolbox
. The software platform, maintained collaboratively by the OECD and ECHA, serves as the premier computational workbench for industrial and pharmaceutical filings in Europe. The presentation will illustrate how assessors leverage structural profilers, automated workflows, third-party QSAR integration, and trend analysis to construct reliable aquatic toxicity profiles without generating new vertebrate testing data.

In parallel, the session will examine how North American tools align with the international framework. The U.S. EPA has long relied on its 
ECOSAR Predictive Model
 to estimate aquatic toxicity during pre-manufacture reviews of industrial substances. By comparing the structural class equations within ECOSAR with the mechanistic profilers in the OECD Toolbox, the organizers aim to harmonize how toxicological endpoints are categorized and defended before regulatory bodies worldwide.

Background, Animal Testing Reduction, and Regulatory Harmonization

Ecotoxicity testing under classical environmental regulations has historically represented one of the largest demands for vertebrate animal subjects in regulatory science. Standard testing protocols, such as OECD Test Guideline 203 for fish acute toxicity and OECD Test Guideline 210 for fish early-life stage toxicity, require large cohorts of freshwater species including rainbow trout, fathead minnows, and zebrafish. Over the past decade, statutory mandates in both North America and Europe have directed regulatory bodies to reduce, refine, and replace animal use under the 3Rs principles.

For the pharmaceutical sector, environmental risk assessment is a mandatory component of marketing authorization applications submitted to both the FDA and the EMA. Drug developers must characterize the potential impact of active pharmaceutical ingredients released into aquatic environments via patient excretion and manufacturing discharge. Where empirical data have historically been demanded, the integration of validated 
chemical read-across frameworks
 and computational QSAR predictors offers a scientifically rigorous route to satisfy environmental safety requirements while eliminating unnecessary in vivo fish assays.

The convergence between the FDA, EPA, and EMA reflects a broader transatlantic effort to align data requirements. Discrepancies between how European and American regulators treat computational predictions have previously forced multinational sponsors to conduct duplicative animal testing to satisfy conflicting jurisdictional standards. By anchoring discussions to the OECD QAF, the participating agencies are actively defining what constitutes adequate validation, mechanistic plausibility, and uncertainty characterization in formal filings.

Stakeholder Perspectives and Scientific Consensus

The initiative highlights growing alignment between regulatory authorities and non-governmental scientific consortia. The PETA Science Consortium International and HESI Global have played instrumental roles in bridging commercial research interests with regulatory science. Consortia leadership has pointed out that computational ecotoxicology models often demonstrate higher reproducibility than traditional in vivo aquatic bioassays, where biological variability, water quality fluctuations, and chemical volatility can introduce significant experimental noise.

Regulatory officials from participating agencies have emphasized that model acceptance does not imply a lowering of environmental safety standards. Rather, modern QSAR models and consensus algorithms provide granular mechanistic insight into specific modes of action, such as narcosis, oxidative stress, and endocrine disruption. By coupling computational screening with emerging in vitro cellular models within the 
toxicological sciences and safety pharmacology
, regulators can make conservative, protective decisions without relying on whole-organism vertebrate lethality testing.

Operational Implications for Academic Laboratories and Research Offices

The progression toward standardized in silico ecotoxicity models carries immediate practical implications for academic investigators, translational drug development centers, and university research offices. Principal investigators designing novel synthetic chemicals, polymers, or pharmaceutical candidates must increasingly demonstrate environmental compatibility early in the translational pipeline to secure federal funding and industrial licensing partnerships.

Research offices and technology transfer units must ensure that computational ecotoxicology data generated in grant-supported laboratories adhere to formal documentation standards. Regulatory submissions relying on in silico data require detailed reporting packages, specifically the QSAR Model Reporting Format and the QSAR Prediction Reporting Format. Aligning university-generated computational predictions with recognized 
Good Laboratory Practice standards
 ensures that data packages will withstand statutory scrutiny during patent filings, investigational new drug applications, and environmental assessments.

Furthermore, academic research facilities working with aquatic models can anticipate evolving oversight standards. As non-animal alternatives gain formal statutory acceptance, institutional animal care and use committees are expected to apply tighter scrutiny to proposals requesting vertebrate fish for routine acute ecotoxicity testing, requiring researchers to justify why validated computational methodologies or cell-based assays cannot serve as primary evidence.

Timeline and Registration Details for Upcoming Sessions

The December 3, 2026 session will run virtually via the 
LiveWebinar platform
 from 8:00 a.m. to 9:30 a.m. Eastern Time. The organizers have made attendance open to the global scientific community, welcoming academic researchers, industrial regulatory affairs specialists, environmental consultants, and regulatory reviewers.

Following the live presentations, an extended question-and-answer period will allow participants to engage directly with regulatory officials from EFSA and ECHA regarding specific implementation challenges. Archived recordings and presentation materials will subsequently be distributed across participating agency portals to support continuing education in regulatory toxicology. The Eco-NAMs consortium will announce its 2027 webinar roster in early January, with upcoming sessions slated to address high-throughput in vitro testing cascades and ecological threshold of toxicological concern frameworks.

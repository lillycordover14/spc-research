The morning of Nov. 7, 2026, 
Polk County Emergency Management Agency
 Director Landon Hendrix was still getting used to missing the “interim” tag in his title. He’d been promoted the week before, the culmination of years of training under his recently retired predecessor, Robert Dockery.
Now Hendrix was officially one of the directors within Georgia Emergency Management’s Area 6, which encompasses all counties north and west of Atlanta.
“Any time one of us is in trouble, sinking, needing help, we all come together in that time,” Hendrix said.
That morning, everyone, including Hendrix and his team, was assembling for Fannin County.
Area 6’s northeasternmost member, Fannin was overwhelmed by the devastation caused by a 1,000-year flood – an event with an estimated 0.1% chance of occurring in any given year. Nearly a foot of rain hit the county in just 12 hours. Hours later, the resulting runoff from the Toccoa River surged 14 to 16 feet and crested at 18 feet, surpassing its previous 1990 record.
The flood moved homes off their foundations, swept away vehicles and washed out local roads. Fannin County EMA received roughly 150 rescue calls at the flood’s onset.
Joining the stream of fellow Area 6 members, Hendrix and his team set out to support the disaster-stricken area.
Fannin’s flood wasn’t the first time Hendrix served the role of EMA cavalry. The Cedartown native vividly recalls the devastation wrought in Treutlen County by Hurricane Helene in 2024. Hendrix was on assignment at the state EMA offices when the hurricane hit. The operations chief sent Hendrix down to help.
The scene was daunting. The entire Treutlen power grid was down. Roads were blocked. Wells were flooded.
Hendrix’s knack for planning and prioritizing led to his being named deputy incident commander. His action plans gave direction to the mass-response operation. Less than a week later, the county was able to segue out of response and into long-term recovery. Treutlen residents still keep in touch with Hendrix.
“There’s that friendship, you know?” Hendrix said. “I always say we’re trauma-bonded from going through something like that.”
Hendrix didn’t wait until after graduation day to get involved in public safety. When he turned 18, he enrolled as a volunteer Polk County firefighter while completing his senior year at Cedartown High School.
His work with Fire-Rescue produced an invitation to a full-time position with Polk EMA. He started in January 2020 – right as the COVID-19 pandemic began sweeping over the nation.
“Going into it, you think you’ve got an idea of: you go out, you deal with it, you go home,” Hendrix said. “But coming into EMA and working during COVID, seeing how long-term events can actually be, really the biggest thing for me is seeing there’s more to it than just by the sirens blaring.”
The unique nature of that danger and the emergencies it produced impressed upon Hendrix the value of emergency prevention and recovery.
“I always like to say, if you do 90 percent of the front end, you’re going to be doing less on the back end of a disaster.”
Fannin County had done its part on the front end, but the magnitude of the flood still necessitated a 100-percent response from fellow EMA Area 6 counties.
Hendrix and his group waded through displaced sewer water and refuse. Washed up on the side of the road was an industrial shipping container, a reminder of how even 10,000-pound objects or structures are no match for nature unleashed. Polk EMA joined forces with its counterparts from other counties, assisting in damage assessment, recovery operations and cleanup.
Amid all the damage and emergency response efforts, displaced residents stood out the most.
“That’s the tragic part of it,” Hendrix said. “These people have built their lives around these places. Then, to see the power of nature itself…”
But Hendrix then saw what he has seen countless times before – the power of the people’s response to such adversity. Uniforms representing Paulding, Bartow, Cherokee, Pickens, Carroll, Gilmer and many more counties formed their own flood, one of recovery and outreach.
“It’s all a team effort,” Hendrix said.
Hendrix’s main duties remain in his own back yard of Polk County. There’s an extra weight that comes with responding to an emergency in his own community, driving with the sirens sounding down roads he knows from childhood, occasionally helping those he personally knows.
But that weight, Polk’s new EMA director says, is worth it.
“I’ve always been a person who believes that, you know, this is your community,” Hendrix said. “This is the place you grew up in. These are the people you get to serve… I get to be a part of something big. It’s hard at times. But it’s rewarding with all that.”

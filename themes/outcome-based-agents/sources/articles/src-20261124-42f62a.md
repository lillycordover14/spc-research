More tenants, shorter leases, and higher operating costs make small bay more complex to manage. But those challenges can also create the return; . for owners equipped to manage complexity, the higher yield may reward expertise rather than added real estate risk.
Small bay is not a scaled-down version of big-box industrial. It generally includes buildings under 100,000 square feet, often with suites of roughly 2,000–15,000 square feet. The tenants range from tradespeople and local manufacturers to service firms, light assembly users, and last-mile operators that need to stay close to customers. These are neighborhood warehouses, not logistics mega-hubs, and they behave differently.
Institutional Capital Is Moving into the Space
Institutional capital is moving into small- and mid-bay industrial. BKM Capital Partners and Kayne Anderson formed a $1.5 billion joint venture focused on the segment in 2025. In 2026, the partnership acquired 51 multi-tenant properties from Link Logistics for $1.81 billion, adding about 8.5 million square feet. The transaction does not prove every small-bay asset is attractive. It does show that scale, operating expertise, and durable tenant demand can support a long-term investment strategy.

Fundamentals Do Not Look Like Big-Box Industrial
Vacancy tells part of the story. Buildings under 100,000 square feet averaged 4.9% vacancy in the fourth quarter of 2025, about half the rate of larger buildings at that time, according to BKM Capital Partners. By the third quarter of 2026, vacancy in buildings of 500,000 square feet or more had fallen to 7.4%, down 280 basis points from a year earlier, according to Cushman & Wakefield. The gap is narrowing, so investors should not treat it as permanent. The more durable question is how difficult the space is to replace and how consistently local tenants need it.
Supply remains one of the strongest parts of the small-bay story. In the third quarter of 2026, just 0.5% of small-bay inventory was under construction, compared with 3.0% for large-bay buildings, according to BKM Capital Partners. That limited development pipeline matters. Even as industrial demand improves, development economics continue to constrain new small-bay construction, while e-commerce, onshoring, and manufacturing continue to support leasing demand. Limited supply alone does not guarantee performance, but for well-located, functional small-bay assets, it creates a meaningful competitive advantage.
Rent growth is broad, not exclusive to small bay. Cushman & Wakefield reported national asking rents up 4% year over year in the third quarter of 2026, while JLL reported 12.1% growth for Class A buildings over one million square feet. Small bay has outperformed over the broader cycle, but its current case rests more on constrained supply and tenant depth than on rent growth alone.
Interpreting the 176-Basis-Point Spread

Our internal analysis of 61 industrial transactions shows an average cap rate of 6.14% for properties under 100,000 square feet versus 4.38% for properties of 250,000 square feet or more, a 176-basis-point spread. The sample includes 18 transactions under 100,000 square feet, 21 between 100,000 and 250,000 square feet, and 22 at 250,000 square feet or more. The assets are located in 36 MSAs throughout the West, South/Southeast, Texas/Central, Midwest, Northeast, and Mid-Atlantic. That gap reflects real differences in management intensity, tenant credit, rollover, capital needs, and liquidity. Across these markets, the key question is whether the spread simply compensates investors for those risks, or offers an additional reward where supply is scarce and local demand is deep.
The spread between cap rates widened from about 169 basis points in 2025 to 192 basis points in 2026 on the same size comparison. Because the 2026 large-building sample includes only seven sales, the change is directional, not conclusive.
Small bay still requires more leasing, more tenant management, more turnover, and often a smaller buyer pool. Even with strong fundamentals, many investors do not want that complexity. This helps explain why the spread has not disappeared and why a yield premium can persist for owners equipped to handle it.
Rollovers are both the risk and the opportunity. Small-bay leases typically run three to five years, compared with five to ten years for many large warehouses. More frequent renewals increase downtime and leasing costs, but they can also move below market rents sooner. The value depends on the rent mark-to-market, rollover schedule, and cost of releasing the space.
Tenant diversity reduces concentration, not credit risk. At a multi-tenant small bay property, losing one tenant usually has less effect on income than losing a single tenant at a large warehouse. The tradeoff is a larger roster of smaller businesses that may have weaker credit and require more leasing work. Underwriting therefore needs to look beyond tenant count to how much income is exposed to any one industry, lease year, or credit profile.
Scarcity supports value only when the location and building work. Limited construction and high development costs constrain new supply, but a functionally obsolete property in a weak location does not become attractive simply because little new space is being built. Small bay performs best where tenants need proximity to customers, labor, and established business networks.
Financing costs are the swing factor
Financing is the pressure point. The 10-year Treasury yield rose from about 4.5% in early June 2026 to 5.31% on October 5, while fixed-rate CMBS loans in 2026 pools carried a median rate of about 6.3% on 10-year terms as of mid-October. With small-bay cap rates near 6%, initial leverage may not create much positive spread. That does not erase the opportunity, but it raises the burden of proof: rent growth must survive turnover costs, and the exit cap rate must leave room for a more expensive capital market.
Five questions that can change the valuation
A roughly 6% cap rate does not make a small-bay property inherently cheap or risky. The answer turns on five property-level questions:
Is the rent roll diversified in ways that matter?
 Look beyond tenant count to revenue concentration by tenant, industry, lease expiration, and credit quality.
Does rollover create opportunity or exposure
? Compare expiring rents with market rents, then test downtime, concessions, tenant improvements, and renewal probability.
Is local demand truly deep?
 Low vacancy is more meaningful when leasing activity comes from multiple industries and replacement space is difficult to build.
How much rent growth survives the costs?
 Underwrite the net result after turnover, leasing commissions, tenant improvements, bad debt, and operating expense growth - not the headline rent increase.
Who is the next buyer?
 A strong operating story still needs a credible exit. Test the likely buyer pool, portfolio scale, financing environment, and the discount a future buyer may apply for management intensity.
The definitions still require care. Outside research often uses "shallow bay," which overlaps with—but is not identical to—the small bay segment used in our analysis. Market comparisons are useful context, but they are not a substitute for consistent property-level classification and underwriting.

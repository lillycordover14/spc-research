TWU partners with adaptive ensemble ahead of statewide performance

Nov. 23, 2026
 — DENTON — The one-hour ensemble music class on Fridays in the music building is Erika Knapp and Teresa Stewart’s favorite hour of the week. 

At 1 p.m., more than a dozen nontraditional students pour into a third floor rehearsal room to practice as the adaptive ensemble group, Galaxy Fresh. And every week, Texas Woman’s students volunteer — on a day they don’t have scheduled classes — to help lead the group through learning and partnering to play together.

Knapp, an assistant professor of music education at TWU, and Stewart, the founder of Musicology Denton, are the founders of Resonance Sound Collective, a nonprofit organization that supports inclusive music making for all types of people. And every week there is rehearsal. 

“For us, it’s not a music therapy experience; we aren't doing any clinical intervention,” Knapp said. “It’s about community, it’s about belonging and it’s about every single person being deserving and valued and has the right to make beautiful music together.”

The relatively new nonprofit came to be after a friend on the Committee on Persons with Disabilities for the City of Denton approached Stewart to help facilitate some music classes. Everyone she spoke to about it told her she needed to talk to Knapp, who specializes in adaptive music. Once the two met and clicked, the group met for the first time the following month, October 2025. 

“TWU has this amazing space and amazing resources, why don’t we see if we can bring this partnership over?,” Knapp said. “All of the people at TWU were very supportive. We moved to this space after a couple of weeks and it has evolved organically since then. Now we have these education students, these music therapy students, who are all coming and volunteering their time to build their own learning and experience working with people with disabilities, but they’re also just making new friends and making music.”

The current musicians in the ensemble are between the ages of 18 and 40 and have a range of physical and cognitive disabilities. 

Now, Resonance Sound Collective is the first adaptive ensemble group in the region and will be the first adaptive ensemble to perform at the Texas Music Educators Association Convention next year. 

First, they have to have the funding to go, though. Currently, 
the nonprofit is raising funds
 to travel to the conference in San Antonio in February, where the group will perform during Knapp and Stewart’s presentation about adaptive music curriculum. The group just got news they received support from TWU’s Creative Arts and Humanities grant program, but still has some fundraising to go to make the trip happen. 

Going to the conference will expose the students to high level performances, and will help build their performance confidence, Knapp said. 

So, how does a music collective become adaptive? Well, you make instruments more accessible. 

Most students use resonator bar instruments, where bars can be removed so only the ones that are used in that particular song are in front of the student. Pianos and other instruments also have some modifications to make playing them easier. 

“Essentially, you’re creating a space where everything that they do is going to sound great and have instant success,” Knapp said. “We can meet every single person where they’re at — there’s a variety of needs.”

Looking beyond the convention performance in February, Stewart and Knapp have big hopes for Resonance Sound Collective, from forming a choir and a band to expanding the programming to other North Texas school districts. The goal? Building confidence and self expression through music. 

“This is so special because this group represents 20% of the population and so when we’re training teachers to do this, it isn’t some niche 1% of the population, 20% have some difference,” Stewart said. 

Knowing that, the end experience from TWU students is more confidence and understanding before they begin their careers, Knapp said. 

“The absolute joy of watching TWU students as they evolve, as they develop their comfort, as they’re kind of unpacking those things that live under the surface for us — the fear of the unknown — a lot of people are afraid of people with disabilities because they aren’t sure what the right thing to do is, there’s no malice. So it’s helping them find a space where they’re becoming more confident as educators and musical therapists.”

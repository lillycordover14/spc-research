Microsoft Ignite 2026: Is Your Enterprise Ready to Let AI Agents Act?

Delegating more work depends on trusted context, clear limits, and controls that hold up in production.

By Anukool Rege, Chris Green,  and Greg Callahan

Progress:

en

Two moments at Microsoft Ignite 2026 put the enterprise AI challenge in focus. In a hands-on lab, we built a data lakehouse and applied sentiment analysis to customer interactions, with Copilot helping write and troubleshoot the code. Its suggestions and corrections were consistently on target. In another session, an IT agent handled a service ticket: diagnosing a device problem, checking policy and identity context, changing group membership, syncing the device, and verifying the fix.

The lab showed how quickly teams can build. The service-ticket demo raised a tougher question: Would you let that agent change someone’s access rights inside your business tomorrow? And if you did, how would you know it made the right call?

Our takeaway: Better models aren’t enough. Agents need to understand how a business actually works, which definitions to trust, what actions they may take, and how their work gets checked. Microsoft is building much of its enterprise AI architecture around those needs.

Here’s what to get right as you scale agents across the business.

Before agents act, agree on what the data means

Ask five executives what counts as a customer, and you may get five defensible answers. Sales, finance, and support can work through those differences in a meeting. An agent operating across those teams’ systems needs to know which definition governs each decision. The same is true for revenue, account status, permissions, and policies. Conflicting answers can send a capable agent in the wrong direction.

Microsoft’s announcements aim squarely at this problem. Copilot is moving toward a common interface for work. Microsoft IQ is its broader context architecture, with Fabric IQ providing governed business data and meaning. Business plug-ins are intended to make context, skills, and tools reusable across agents and processes. The larger opportunity is to treat organizational intelligence as a reusable enterprise asset, continuously refined through feedback and evaluation rather than rebuilt for every new agent.

Building on OneLake and Power BI semantic models, semantic views and Fabric IQ ontologies—both of which are in public preview—are designed to make business metrics, entities, relationships, rules, and available actions reusable across analytics, agents, and applications. 

But a shared platform doesn’t settle which version of a metric or rule is authoritative. The same policy might still live in a semantic model, an ontology, an agent instruction, and a security setting. Someone has to own the definition, approve changes, and make sure those changes reach every place they should be used. The CIO can’t solve that ownership question alone.

Pair faster building with standards, tests, and owners

The lab made the development trade-off clear. Copilot helped write and repair scripts quickly. Other sessions showed assistance extending into models, business measures, reports, and applications. That can open data-product development to more teams, and it can multiply duplicated metrics, inconsistent models, and insecure code. Applications may also end up with no clear owner.

Our lab was a controlled exercise. Production comes with legacy systems, security constraints, and far less predictable conditions. A session on retrieval-augmented generation made a practical recommendation: Test the full retrieval path under realistic load. Faster development should come with reusable patterns, automated tests, semantic standards, named owners, and clear release controls. Otherwise, what gets built can outpace what’s worth running.

Decide how much authority agents get

We see a useful progression in AI’s role: worker, workflow, and workload. At the worker level, AI helps a person complete a task. At the workflow level, it coordinates a series of steps with people overseeing. At the workload level, it can monitor conditions and act continuously within defined boundaries. The potential value rises at each level, and so does the cost of a wrong decision.

Go back to the service-ticket agent. It needed reliable device, policy, and identity information. It also needed permission to change group membership, plus a way to verify that the change worked. Before putting that capability into production, leaders need to set approval thresholds, define exceptions, monitor what the agent does, and work out how to undo a bad action.

That’s where the observability and resilience sessions come in. Microsoft described unified monitoring across jobs, applications, agents, alerts, and capacity, though several of those capabilities remain in preview. It also emphasized isolating workloads, reducing single points of failure, containing problems, and testing recovery paths. Satya Nadella’s reference to the recent Microsoft 365 outage added context to those sessions. The ability to see a failure, limit its impact, and recover belongs in the plan before agents start taking consequential actions.

Three questions to take back to your team

Which workflow are we prepared to let an agent complete? Be specific about the steps that still require human approval.

Which definitions, systems, identities, and policies can that agent rely on? Name the business owners who decide what is authoritative and how it changes.

How will we spot a bad decision and recover? Test realistic conditions, monitor outcomes, and agree on escalation and rollback before deployment.

Ignite made the technical ambition easy to see, and the challenges it raised reach well beyond Microsoft. The management conversation is harder: Which decisions can your agents be trusted to make, and what would you need to change before you let them act?

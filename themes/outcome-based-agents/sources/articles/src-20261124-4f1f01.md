Freehand Direct Composite Diastema Closure: Creating the Illusion of Natural Proportions

November 24, 2026

10 minutes read
 

7 hours ago

Written by: Amey G. Patil, BDS, MSD

The Patient’s Concern

An adult male presented with long-standing spacing in the maxillary anterior dentition, most notably a prominent midline diastema between the central incisors (Nos. 8 and 9). Additional spacing was present between Nos. 10 and 11, with slight spacing between Nos. 6 and 7; however, the central diastema had the greatest perceived effect on his smile. He reported avoiding broad smiles and feeling self-conscious when being photographed. The patient stated that porcelain veneers had been recommended during previous consultations, but he remained hesitant because of the anticipated cost and the possibility that treatment could require removal of sound enamel. His principal concern was whether the central diastema could be closed while preserving as much natural tooth structure as reasonably possible. This preference became an important component of the shared treatment-planning process.

Diagnosis and the Conservative Decision

Clinical and radiographic examination revealed unrestored maxillary anterior teeth without evidence of caries, fracture, or periapical pathology. Periodontal examination demonstrated clinically healthy gingival tissues and adequate periodontal support in the treatment area. Pulp sensibility testing of teeth Nos. 8 and 9 produced responses within clinically normal limits. A prominent midline diastema was present between teeth Nos. 8 and 9, with additional spacing between Nos. 10 and 11 and slight spacing between Nos. 6 and 7. The principal restorative considerations were the width of the central diastema, the effect of additive restorative material on central-incisor proportions, and the development of smooth, cleansable proximal emergence profiles (Figure 1).

Figure 1a. Preoperative retracted frontal intraoral view showing the maxillary midline diastema between the central incisors (Nos. 8 and 9).

Figure 1b. Pre-op open-retracted view showing the maxillary midline diastema in unrestored central incisors. The clinically intact facial enamel supported consideration of an additive restorative approach.

The spacing was reported to be long-standing and stable. Clinical and radiographic findings did not indicate active periodontal migration or another pathologic process requiring treatment before restorative closure.

Treatment options discussed with the patient included observation, orthodontic space redistribution or closure, direct resin-composite additions, and indirect ceramic restorations. The relative advantages and limitations of each option were reviewed, including treatment duration, extent of tooth preparation, repairability, aesthetic limitations, anticipated maintenance, and the potential need for future repair or replacement. Following this discussion, the patient elected direct resin-composite closure of the diastema between Nos. 8 and 9. He chose not to address the remaining anterior spaces at that appointment and prioritized treatment of the central diastema.

Direct resin composite was selected because it permitted a primarily additive and repairable approach, could be completed in a single clinical appointment, and allowed the developing contours and proportions to be evaluated and refined intraoperatively. Shade selection was performed before isolation, while the teeth remained hydrated to facilitate assessment of value, chroma, and translucency. Treatment was completed on May 31, 2024.

The Optical Challenge: Closing the Space Without Excessive Apparent Width

Closure of a maxillary midline diastema requires more than the symmetrical addition of restorative material to the mesial surfaces of the central incisors. Increasing the mesiodistal width of these teeth without corresponding control of facial contour may cause them to appear excessively broad relative to their height and to the adjacent dentition.

The apparent width of an anterior tooth is influenced by the position of the facial transition line angles, the distribution of reflected light and shadow, facial surface convexity, proximal contour, contact-area dimensions, incisal embrasure form, and the cervical emergence profile. In this case, the mesial contours were extended toward the midline to close the diastema, while the mesial facial transition line angles were recreated closer to the center of each facial surface to limit the perceived increase in central-incisor width. 

The proximal contact area and incisal embrasure between teeth Nos. 8 and 9 were developed to preserve visual separation between the 2 central incisors. These contouring strategies were intended to achieve complete space closure while maintaining natural-appearing tooth proportions. However, the aesthetic assessment was based on clinical evaluation and photographic documentation rather than formal digital morphometric or smile-proportion analysis.

Clinical Technique

Minimal enamel recontouring was limited to areas necessary to refine the preexisting facial transition line angles and facilitate controlled additive contours, with the intention of preserving sound enamel wherever clinically feasible. At the mesio-cervical aspects of teeth Nos. 8 and 9, limited enameloplasty was performed to improve access and facilitate the development of smooth, cleansable emergence profiles. Any minor gingival bleeding associated with cervical access was controlled before initiation of the adhesive procedure (Figure 2).

Figure 2. Clinical appearance after limited enamel recontouring performed to refine the facial transition line angles and facilitate the planned additive contours.

Relative isolation and circumferential retraction of the lips and cheeks were achieved using an OptraGate lip-and-cheek retractor (Ivoclar). The enamel surfaces intended to receive composite, including the mesial and mesiopalatal surfaces and the facial enamel extending from the cervical through the middle and incisal thirds, were etched with 3M Scotchbond Universal Etchant containing 32% phosphoric acid by weight (Solventum) for 15 seconds. The conditioned enamel surfaces were then thoroughly rinsed and dried in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

The 3M Scotchbond Universal Adhesive was delivered using the single-dose L-Pop system, with a separate unit-dose applicator used for each central incisor. The adhesive was actively rubbed onto the conditioned enamel for 20 seconds and gently air-thinned for approximately 5 seconds, until the adhesive layer no longer exhibited visible movement, to promote solvent evaporation and formation of a uniform adhesive film. Each adhesive layer was then light polymerized for 10 seconds using a VALO LED curing light (Ultradent Products).

The proximal additions were developed primarily using a freehand technique. A clear polyester matrix strip was used as a separator and contouring aid rather than as a preformed anatomic template. The proximal walls were established first to facilitate control of the midline, proximal contact area, cervical adaptation, and emergence profiles before development of the facial anatomy.

Because closure of the diastema required an increase in the mesiodistal dimensions of both central incisors, particular attention was directed toward limiting the perceived increase in tooth width. The new mesial contours were extended toward the midline, while the mesial facial transition line angles were recreated more distally, closer to the center of each facial surface, to maintain a relatively narrow facial reflective zone. Excessive facial convexity and proximal bulk were avoided. The proximal contact area, incisal embrasure, cervical emergence profiles, and facial reflective zones were developed incrementally so that teeth Nos. 8 and 9 remained visually distinct and did not appear disproportionately broad.

The facial surfaces were subsequently layered to full contour. After the proximal and facial anatomy had been established, the restorations were evaluated before final contouring, finishing, and polishing (Figure 3). The proximal contact, midline symmetry, incisal relationships, and occlusion were verified and refined as necessary.

Figure 3. Immediate appearance of the direct composite restorations after layering and before final contouring, finishing, and polishing.

Material and Layering Strategy: Each Shade Chosen for a Reason

The composite shades and optical modifiers were selected according to their intended roles within the layered restorations. Estelite Omega DA2 Dentin (Tokuyama Dental America) was placed as the principal body shade, while Estelite Omega EA1 Enamel (Tokuyama Dental America) was applied as the superficial layer. This layering sequence was intended to reproduce the transition from a more chromatic internal dentin body to a relatively translucent external enamel surface.

Because the dark intraoral background could influence the appearance of the newly created proximal surfaces, Estelite Color Medium Chroma Opaque (MCO) (Tokuyama Dental America) was placed selectively within the central and cervical portions of the composite buildups. This material was used to increase local opacity and reduce visible gray show-through from the oral cavity.

A narrow vertical application of Estelite Color Yellow (Tokuyama Dental America) was incorporated into the facial layering, extending from the cervical region toward the middle third. The tint was used to introduce subtle chromatic characterization and reinforce the vertical orientation of the facial surface. It served as an adjunct to the contouring strategy; the perception of narrower central incisors was achieved primarily through controlled facial convexity, mesial facial transition line-angle positioning, contact-area development, embrasure form, and management of the facial reflective zones rather than through color modification alone.

The completed restorations were evaluated clinically under operatory and ambient illumination for contour, value, chroma, translucency, surface texture, and visual integration with the adjacent tooth structure.

Finishing, Polishing, and the Immediate Outcome

Following final contouring and polishing, the restorations exhibited smooth surfaces and clinically acceptable luster. From the frontal view, the maxillary midline diastema was closed, and the central incisors appeared proportionate relative to their height and to the adjacent anterior dentition. The position of the facial transition line angles and the contour of the facial surfaces helped confine the principal reflective zones and limit the perceived increase in central-incisor width (Figure 4).

Figure 4. Immediate postoperative retracted frontal view after final contouring and polishing. The maxillary midline diastema is closed, with clinically acceptable proximal contours and natural-appearing central-incisor proportions.

From the right lateral view, the proximal and facial surfaces appeared to transition smoothly from the cervical region without an obvious ledge or marked overcontouring. The cervical emergence profile and proximal contours appeared clinically acceptable and visually integrated with the adjacent tooth structure (Figure 5).

Figure 5. Immediate post-op right lateral view showing the cervical emergence profile and proximal contours of the direct resin-composite additions.

The immediate postoperative full-face photograph demonstrated closure of the central diastema and an overall smile appearance that was acceptable to both the patient and the clinician. A comparison of the preoperative and immediate post-op full-face photographs illustrates the visible change in the patient’s smile following closure of the central diastema (Figure 6).

Figure 6. Pre-op and immediate post-op full-face photographs illustrating the change following direct resin-composite closure of the maxillary midline diastema.

The patient reported satisfaction with the immediate restorative outcome. A photograph with the treating clinician was obtained immediately after treatment to document the completion of care and the patient’s initial response to the result.

Clinical Follow-up Appointment at Approximately 23 Months

The patient returned in April 2026, approximately 23 months after treatment. Clinical examination and photographic documentation indicated that both direct resin-composite restorations remained intact, with maintained closure of the maxillary midline diastema. No clinically evident fracture, chipping, marginal discoloration, or loss of restorative contour was observed. Shade integration and surface luster remained clinically acceptable (Figure 7).

Figure 7. Close-up smile at approximately 23 months showing maintained closure of the maxillary midline diastema, clinically acceptable shade integration, and preservation of surface luster.

The full-face, follow-up photograph showed maintenance of the overall aesthetic appearance. The composite additions remained visually integrated with the surrounding dentition, and the central incisors continued to appear proportionate relative to their height and to the adjacent anterior teeth (Figure 8).

Figure 8. Full-face smile at approximately 23 months showing maintenance of the overall aesthetic appearance and natural-appearing central-incisor proportions.

 At the follow-up visit, the patient continued to report satisfaction with the treatment and greater comfort when smiling and being photographed. Because no validated patient-reported outcome measure was administered, these observations should be interpreted as informal patient reports rather than as quantified changes in self-confidence or oral health–related quality of life.

CONCLUSION

In an appropriately selected patient with predominantly intact anterior enamel, direct resin-composite buildups provided a minimally invasive approach for closing a maxillary midline diastema while maintaining natural-appearing central-incisor proportions. At the follow-up appointment approximately 23 months after treatment, the restorations remained intact, with maintained space closure and favorable clinical findings. Longer-term observation is required to evaluate the durability of the outcome and the need for maintenance or repair.

Adaptive capacity—the ability to cope with disturbances and environmental change—offers a useful perspective for sustainable forest management. However, applying this concept to logging remains challenging, and a key question is how different logging practices affect forest adaptive capacity.

According to adaptive cycle theory, selective logging generally disturbs species composition and structure less than clear-cutting does and may therefore better preserve forest adaptive capacity. Yet experimental evidence from subtropical forests remains limited.

A new study led by researchers at the South China Botanical Garden (SCBG) of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) now provides empirical support for that prediction. Drawing on data from a long-term logging experiment in an evergreen broad-leaved forest in Wuping County, Fujian Province, China, the team examined changes in forest adaptive capacity between 1998 and 2017.

To assess adaptive capacity, the researchers focused on three key attributes: potential, reflecting the accumulation of species resources; connectedness, representing the rigidity of species interactions; and resilience, describing the ability of forests to recover toward the condition of mature natural forests after disturbance.

"Both potential and connectedness were at least 50% higher in selectively logged forests than in clear-cut forests," said You Zhangtian, a Ph.D. graduate of SCBG and co-first author of the study. "After 22 years of natural recovery, the adaptive capacity of selectively logged forests had approached that of mature natural forests, whereas clear-cut forests had yet to recover fully."

What explains this persistent difference?

"Compared with selectively logged forests, clear-cut forests had higher species evenness, more light-demanding trees and simpler tree height structures," said Wu Guilin of the Chinese Academy of Forestry, another co-first author of the study. "These differences were associated with lower forest adaptive capacity."

The researchers also identified dynamic adaptive mechanisms in logged subtropical forests. During early recovery (three to five years), species evenness, the abundance of light-demanding species and tree height inequality each reduced potential and connectedness. During later recovery (10–22 years), the influence of species evenness strengthened, while that of the other two factors weakened.

These findings suggest that logging affects forest adaptive capacity primarily through changes in species diversity, functional group composition and stand structure.

"The key finding is that the factors shaping forest adaptive capacity are not static but change as forests recover from logging," said Ye Qing of SCBG, corresponding author of the study. "This highlights the importance of long-term monitoring for sustainable forest management."

This study provides experimental insight into how different logging practices affect forest adaptive capacity and offers a scientific basis for sustainable forest management in subtropical regions. The findings were recently

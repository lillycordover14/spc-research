Sidney Crosby
 seemed like a prime NHL player to ask, and we thought it would be a tough question to answer. The Pittsburgh Penguins captain, however, didn’t hesitate.

So far, CTE (chronic traumatic encephalopathy), the progressive brain disease that causes cognitive problems and has been found in many athletes who played high-level sports – particularly contact sports – can only been diagnosed during a postmortem examination of the brain.

Now, there might be a breakthrough that would eventually allow for a CTE diagnosis among the living. In the current issue of the Journal of the American Medical Association, 
two studies identified
 a promising blood biomarker.

A test using that marker is not approved at this point. But the question – the one posed to Crosby on Tuesday – becomes, would you want to take the blood test; would you want to know if you have early-stage CTE, which can cause sometimes devastating cognitive deficits, especially later in life?

Crosby, 39 and a surefire future Hall of Famer, had a well-chronicled stretch during the prime of his career where he dealt with concussion-related issues that forced him to miss more than 100 games, mostly in 2010-11 and ’11-12.

But he didn’t have to think long about whether he would want to take that CTE blood test.

“If I had symptoms that led me to think that there was an issue, yeah, maybe,” he said. “But I think with how I feel right now, it’s not something I’d be seeking out.”

He said it was not a tough question for him to answer.

“When you think about CTE, you’re talking about repetitive, like multiple head traumas,” he said, citing boxing and football as fitting that description more than hockey. “With us, I’ve had instances where I’ve had concussions, but they haven’t been repetitive things. It’s happened over 22 years. If you’re playing sports or you’re just active, you’re going to bump your head once in a while, too.

“It’s one of those things where I think it would be kind of symptomatic-based.”

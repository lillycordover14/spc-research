Heber City adaptive athlete Erica Smith to tackle White Rim with a new bike&nbsp;&nbsp;Park Record

[page text]
Heber City resident and adaptive athlete Erica Smith is preparing to rip down White Rim Trail in Moab with renewed confidence, thanks to a new bike. 

“Biking has become like my whole personality at this point,” Smith said with a laugh.

Today, Smith works as the marketing manager for the National Ability Center in Park City. Over the past few years, she’s immersed herself in the world of mountain biking, but it wasn’t always that way. 

In 2008, when she was 17 years old, Smith was in a car accident that caused a T6 spinal cord injury that left her paralyzed from the mid chest down.

During a vacation to Colorado, Smith rented an adaptive mountain bike, but it would take her over a decade to find a community of adaptive athletes.

“It took me a long time to find out about adaptive recreation because that was, like, 18 years ago, so social media wasn’t like at all what it is now,” Smith said. 

In 2020, she moved to Colorado and a few years later decided to attend a ladies-only adaptive mountain biking camp. 

“That was the first time I was really involved in the disability community, or surrounded by other people with disabilities,” Smith said. “So I fell in love not only with adaptive mountain biking, but (with) the adaptive recreation community.”

While she spent a few years only getting to mountain bike on vacations, she knew she wanted to be able to mountain bike on her own. The first step was purchasing her own bike, which would cost $15,000. 

“I didn’t know about grants or anything like that,” Smith said. “I was like, ‘Okay, this is just something I get to do like once a year, but I’m never going to be able to afford equipment of my own.’ But then through the ladies that I met at that camp, I found out about foundations like High Fives and some others that give out grants, and so then I was able to get a grant for my own equipment, and then it just became a huge part of my life.”

 

 

Four years ago, the High Fives Foundation provided funding for Smith to buy her first bike. Today, Smith has outgrown it. While her first bike was great for gravel or cross- country riding, she’d tip over riding more technical terrain. It’s also front-wheel drive, which has made her slide on steep or loose terrain. 

“I did the White Rim in 2025, and there were like a lot of sections on that that I ended up having to get help with because I was losing traction,” Smith said, “and I also just wasn’t as confident of a rider as I am now.”

For her next bike, Smith has her eye on the Bowhead RX adaptive mountain bike. She said the bike has a “one-of-a-kind” front suspension that will articulate with Smith. With a narrower frame and more traction, the bike will help her to take on more technical trails.

Erica Smith plans to try the White Rim Trail in Canyonlands National Park with her new mountain bike in April.

 Courtesy of Erica Smith and Kate McMahon

The $5,000 High Fives Foundation grant will go toward the bike’s $25,000 price tag. The foundation awarded 235 grants this year totaling more than $925,000 in grants. 

Additional grants have helped Smith fund about half of the total, with the rest coming from her own pocket. She’s put a deposit on the bike, which should be completed in one or two months.

 

 

“I’ll hopefully just be able to be much more independent on this bike, and it’ll open up a bunch more terrain,” Smith said.

She plans to return to White Rim to complete the trail in April. The trail, which is in Canyon Lands National Park, is about 100 miles long with an 8,800 foot elevation gain.

Seth Horowitz, a friend of Smith’s, has watched her transition from gravel trails and push herself with more technical terrain over the past year. He’s seen her confidence and skill grow through those experiences. 

“She’s just really learned to really embrace the challenge of getting out there and trying new terrain,” Horowitz said. “The fact that she’s got this new bike is going to really allow her to get to the next level of riding.”

Smith moved to Heber City and began her position at the National Ability Center in January. Through her marketing and social media posts, Smith helps spread awareness with hopes of reaching other adaptive athletes.

“It took me so long to find it,” Smith said. “But now that social media and stuff are more prevalent, just getting to get the message out there and hoping that other people can find their own version of the community that I found, like hopefully or sooner on, is kind of why I wanted to get into it as a career.”

Horowitz works at the National Ability Center as an instructor. He said Smith has developed into a “quiet leader” and an active member in the adaptive community.

“The way she’s embraced being a leader within the community and representing the kind of … possibilities that other athletes can have is just amazing,” Horowitz said.

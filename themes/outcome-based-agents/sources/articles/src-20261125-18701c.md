The CIO's guide to finding talent for agent orchestration
Enterprises are clamoring to hire professionals to close the agent orchestration gap, but there is no clear blueprint for finding this emerging skill set.
Carrie Pallardy
,
Contributing Reporter
November 25, 2026
7 Min Read
Getty Images
Agent orchestration is among the top AI skills in demand, as enterprises look to nurture this knowledge within their ranks. The novelty of a lone, proactive AI agent is giving way to a need for scalability through multiple agents to break down 
complex tasks
. The search for professionals to lead such orchestration is not always simple or clear.
For example, Rhonda Baldwin, CIO of LaunchDarkly, a feature management platform, tackled the problem by shifting a data engineer into an AI and automation engineer role.
"We have agent orchestration around putting together dashboards, putting together monthly reports, QBRs and automating that whole process," she told InformationWeek. "Someone with a strong data background made sense there."
Rhonda Baldwin, LaunchDarkly
That is just one route being taken to meet the growing need in the enterprise to elevate AI agents from isolated operations to more cohesive collaboration.
Recruiting in a new frontier
The desire to move from individual AI use cases to scaled, enterprise-wide initiatives is growing. WSJ Intelligence and digital transformation agency Code and Theory 
surveyed 801 C-suite executives
 at U.S. companies with at least $500 million in annual revenue, and 94% said that orchestration of people and tools will help to grow their business. Yet, the report found just 15% of AI investments operate as a unified system.

Figuring out how to take disparate use cases and funnel them into a coordinated, unified system to drive enterprise value is a core business imperative. How can CIOs lead the effort to understand agent orchestration gaps in their organizations and find the right people to close them?
The AI orchestration gap is a problem tech leaders are still defining as they try to find the right talent to address this evolving need.
"No one knows what digital transformation means. Orchestration is starting to sadly fall in this same bucket," said Dan Gardner, co-founder of Code and Theory.
From his perspective, orchestration is about breaking down barriers within an organization. "The silos of data, products, people, and silos to customers are all choke points that slow down decision making," Gardner explained. "Orchestration, in its perfect state, removes a lot of that bad friction."
Dan Gardner, Code and Theory
Achieving that level of AI agent orchestration means moving past individual use cases.
"Handing everyone an AI tool is actually the easy part. The hard part is that you have to redesign the workflow," said Manuel Schoenfeld, chief strategy and AI officer at Qu POS, a restaurant commerce platform.

Enterprises need the right people acting on a clear, shared mandate to figure out how to redesign workflows across different departments and teams. Who owns AI orchestration and sets that mandate?
That accountability question doesn't have a clear answer in a lot of enterprises. CEOs might think they own it. Tech leaders might think they do; 30% of tech leaders think no one currently has accountability, according to the survey from Code and Theory and WSJ Intelligence.
"There's talent and there's leadership. And right now, we're seeing most organizations actually have gaps in both of those areas," Gardner said.
Where does the right talent come from
As enterprises establish a mandate and leadership to guide orchestration, they need the support of the right talent.
At Qu, Schoenfeld is vibe coding training materials, facilitating lunch-and-learns and holding one-on-one meetings with his team members. One of the challenges is that there is no industry standard for upskilling in agent orchestration yet. Furthermore, any formalized training could become outdated within days, given the pace of the tech's evolution.
"It has to be almost ad hoc, in the moment," Baldwin said.
Finding internal talent eager to take on orchestration and to upskill people with the potential to fill those roles is valuable, but outside hiring may still be necessary.

"It's really hard to take an objective look at your own systems, your people, your organization, and be able to make sometimes hard decisions that may affect yourself or other people in the organization," Gardner said.
New talent can inject an outside perspective and skills that don't exist within an organization. While enterprises look for potential hires with such titles as AI platform engineer or AI governance lead on their resumes, Baldwin cautions against getting too hung up on buzzy phrases.
"In today's landscape, there are so many bright engineers, data scientists, data engineers, strong business analysts that could fit that position just as well," she said.
If an organization is hiring for agent orchestration, Baldwin suggested looking for a candidate with a systems-first mindset, a background in understanding workflows, strong communication skills and an ongoing commitment to curiosity.
The talent attraction challenge
Competition is fierce to hire in this nascent space, with the likes of Meta offering 
$100 million signing bonuses for AI talent
, a number most companies cannot match. While that isn't the norm, orchestration talent can still demand high pay that midsize companies struggle to offer, according to Schoenfeld. As a result, he has looked to hire outside the U.S.
"In Turkey, you get a $10 million engineer for $250,000. And you get a very decent one for $160k," Schoenfeld said.
Even if companies can lure orchestration talent with enough pay, they run the risk of disappointing hires.
"AI is gaming the system of people's ability to articulate responses to questions. I know there's been a gap [between] reality versus the person you think you're hiring," Gardner said.
Tech leaders are still developing their vetting processes for these candidates, which can vary with individual enterprises' needs and, despite their best efforts, can lead to floundering hires.
"Getting the wrong person is much, much easier than before because you don't even know what to check for, what questions to ask," Schoenfeld said.
Manuel Schoenfeld, Qu
What does that mean for the talent search? Enterprises will likely take a combined approach: evaluating current staff, upskilling where possible and looking outside for the skills the organization doesn't already have.
"I wouldn't say there's a magic bullet. There's not a head of orchestration expert or team of experts that exists out there," Gardner said.
The talent search also includes a dimension beyond technical skills. AI is putting incredible pressure on people to reinvent themselves constantly. The right talent for agent orchestration is willing to learn and adapt quickly.
"It's really a psychological search for talent, much more than 'let me know your skills,'" Schoenfeld said.
Agent orchestration — long-term trend or fad
Not that long ago, "prompt engineering" was all the rage in the AI talent market. That skill set has since fallen out of favor. In an industry that changes so quickly, is a hunger for agent orchestration here to stay?
Gardner described orchestration as an ongoing function. "In some ways, [it's] a function that never ends," he said. "Just like finance is a function. Marketing is a function."
That doesn't mean enterprise leaders will think about orchestration in the same way they do today. As companies release more agents and some of those agents even take on orchestration tasks themselves, enterprises likely will see the emergence of new challenges and talent needs.
"If we still talk about orchestration in two years, it will be a very, very different orchestration skill set compared to today," Schoenfeld said.
As enterprises move down this path of 
agent orchestration
, AI is upending the traditional career path. And Schoenfeld worries about the future of the talent pipeline. "It pushes out the young and the old," he said.
How will junior engineers be trained? Will senior talent's skills be considered obsolete? Baldwin said she is thinking about that problem right now, and urges CIOs and other enterprise leaders to engage in workforce planning.
"We can't let AI dictate how our workforce will look without us participating," she said.

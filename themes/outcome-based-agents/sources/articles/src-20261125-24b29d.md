AI support agent cost per resolved ticket runs 2 to 4 times the sticker&nbsp;&nbsp;Startup Fortune

[page text]
The $0.99 on the pricing page covers one line of your support bill. Here's how to add the other four before you decide who stays on the team.

Quick Summary - TLDR:

Intercom lists Fin at $0.99 per resolution and Zendesk has listed automated resolutions at roughly $1.50 to $2, but those prices cover only the vendor's invoice.

Escalations, reopened tickets, QA review and knowledge base upkeep are all labor costs the sticker price leaves out.

On a worked example of 10,000 tickets a month, the true AI cost per resolution comes to $2.34, about 2.4 times the $0.99 price, and a weaker deployment pushes it to $3.39.

Blended across every ticket, the example saves 27% against a human-only team, and the saving falls to 3% if escalated tickets get harder and take 18 minutes instead of 12.

Run a 60 to 90 day pilot with the human team intact before cutting anyone, and track reopen rate and escalation handle time weekly.

Search for a cost per resolved ticket AI support agent number and you'll land on a pricing page. Intercom charges $0.99 per resolution for its Fin agent. Zendesk has listed its automated resolutions at roughly $1.50 on a commitment and $2 pay-as-you-go. Divide your monthly ticket volume into those prices and your support budget starts to look like a rounding error. Don't do that math yet.

That figure is real, and for plenty of tickets it's a bargain. Frankly, it's also only one line on the bill. A person still picks up every ticket the bot can't close, reads the transcript before replying, and cleans up when a customer comes back annoyed three days later. None of that appears in the $0.99. This guide gives you the formula, runs it on 10,000 tickets, and shows why the true number tends to land at two to four times the price tag.

Start with what 'resolved' means. Intercom's documentation describes two kinds of resolution for Fin: a confirmed one, where the customer says the answer helped, and an assumed one, where the customer leaves without asking for more help or a human. Both are billable. Assumed resolution is a fair definition for a vendor, who needs a metric it can measure. It's a loose one for you, because a customer who gives up on the bot and tries another channel can still be counted as served.

Four costs sit outside the vendor invoice. Escalations come first: every conversation the agent can't close lands with a person, and that person starts with a transcript instead of a blank ticket. Reopens are next. These are tickets marked resolved that come back within a week, usually in a worse mood, and usually handled by a human. Then QA, because someone has to read a sample of AI conversations every week or you won't know whether the bot is wrong 2% of the time or 12%. Last is upkeep. The help articles the agent reads from need an owner, since a stale refund policy gets repeated to every customer who asks.

How to calculate cost per resolution for an AI agent

You need two formulas, because they answer different questions. The first tells you what the AI really costs per ticket it closes: true AI cost per resolution equals vendor fees, plus the human cost of handling reopened tickets, plus QA time, plus knowledge base upkeep, all divided by billed resolutions minus the ones that came back. The second tells you whether you're saving money: blended cost per ticket equals everything you spend on support for those tickets, escalations included, divided by total tickets. Escalations go in the second formula and stay out of the first, because a human would have handled them with or without the bot.

The inputs below are assumptions for a mid-sized software company, not data from any named business, so swap in your own. Say 10,000 tickets arrive each month and all of them pass through the AI first. The agent resolves 6,000 of them and bills you $0.99 each, which is $5,940. Your human team costs $4,800 a month per person all-in (salary, benefits, tools, management share) over 160 hours, so $30 an hour. A typical ticket takes 10 minutes, which makes $5.00 a ticket and a human-only month of $50,000. That baseline ignores idle time, so it flatters the human side if anything.

Now the reopens. Suppose 12% of the 6,000 AI resolutions come back within seven days. That's 720 tickets. A person handles each one in 12 minutes, two more than usual because they first have to read the failed bot conversation, so $6.00 apiece and $4,320 in total. Those 720 weren't resolved, which cuts your true resolutions from 6,000 to 5,280. Your vendor has probably billed for them anyway.

QA is next. Review 10% of AI conversations, which is 1,000 a month at three minutes each. That's 50 hours and $1,500. Then 20 hours a month of someone keeping the knowledge base current, another $600.

Add it up: $5,940 in fees, $4,320 in reopen handling, $1,500 in QA and $600 in upkeep makes $12,360. Divide by 5,280 true resolutions and you get $2.34.

That's 2.4 times the sticker. And it's the friendly case.

Change two inputs to something plausible for a first quarter on thin documentation: the agent resolves only 4,000 tickets and 20% of them come back. Fees drop to $3,960, but 800 reopens cost $4,800, and QA and upkeep stay at $2,100. The total is $10,860 over 3,200 true resolutions, or $3.39. The vendor's price never moved. Your cost per resolution rose by almost half.

Two inputs move the result more than any others: the reopen rate and the handle time on reopened tickets. You can pull both from your helpdesk. Zendesk and Intercom both report reopened and follow-up tickets, and handle time is a standard export. If you can't get a reopen number, you can't calculate cost per resolution, and you shouldn't sign a volume commitment.

AI support agent vs human agent cost comparison

Now the question you actually care about. The 4,000 tickets the AI escalated cost $6.00 each for a human to handle, which is $24,000. Add that to the other costs ($5,940, $4,320, $1,500 and $600) and the month comes to $36,360. Spread over all 10,000 tickets, that's $3.64 each against $5.00 for a human-only team. You save $13,640 a month, or 27%.

That's a real saving worth having. It's also nowhere near the near-total cut the $0.99 headline suggests.

And it assumes the leftover tickets stay as easy as they were. They won't. The bot takes the password resets and order-status questions, and what's left is billing disputes, bugs and customers who are already upset. Suppose handle time on escalated tickets rises from 12 minutes to 18. Those 4,000 tickets now cost $9.00 each, or $36,000. The month totals $48,360 and the blended cost is $4.84 per ticket. Your saving is 3%.

Same vendor, same price, same resolution rate. The saving is gone.

This is why cutting headcount first gets the order wrong. The tickets the AI removes from your queue are the cheapest ones your team handled, so the average cost of what remains goes up, and the people you let go are the ones you needed for exactly that harder work.

What Klarna learned the expensive way

Klarna is the best-known test of this. In February 2024 it said its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, two-thirds of its customer service chats, doing the work of 700 full-time agents. In May 2025, CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski told Bloomberg that the company had leaned too hard on cost, that quality had suffered, and that Klarna would start hiring human agents again. The fix wasn't to drop the assistant. It was to put people back alongside it.

Cost per ticket was never the problem on Klarna's dashboard. It was the only number anyone was steering by.

What to check before you touch the team

Run a 60 to 90 day pilot with your human team fully in place. Pull four numbers from your helpdesk every week. First, the share of AI conversations that end in a handoff. Second, the share of AI-resolved tickets that get reopened or followed up on within seven days. Third, handle time on escalated tickets compared with the same ticket types before the AI arrived. Fourth, customer satisfaction scores split by AI-only conversations and human-handled ones. If the fourth number drops while your blended cost falls, you haven't saved money, you've moved the cost onto your customers and your renewal rate.

Put questions to the vendor before you sign. Does a ticket that reopens within seven days get billed twice? What counts as an assumed resolution, and can you audit a sample? Can you cap monthly spend? Pricing units also move. Salesforce launched Agentforce at $2 per conversation, according to the company, and later added Flex Credits priced at $0.10 per action, so the number you budget against today may not be the unit you're billed in next year. Check the current price list on the day you decide.

My view is that the formula says AI support is worth buying and not yet worth firing for. Set a rule you can hold yourself to: reduce the team, through attrition rather than layoffs, only after your blended cost per ticket has stayed below the human-only baseline for two consecutive months and satisfaction scores are flat or better. Move your most experienced agents onto the escalation queue first, since that's where the AI sends the hard work and where mistakes cost the most.

Everything you need for the first calculation is already in your helpdesk: ticket count, handle time and the reopen flag. Export last month's tickets this week and run the numbers before the vendor's sales deck does it for you.

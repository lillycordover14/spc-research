As enterprises move from AI experimentation to production deployments, business context is emerging as a critical factor in determining whether AI agents can deliver reliable results. Dhruv Saharya, Director of Customer Success at Atlan, discusses why increasingly capable models still struggle with enterprise-specific knowledge, the governance challenges of autonomous agents, and how organisations can build a shared, governed context layer to support AI at scale.
Context is the differentiator
Why has enterprise AI struggled with context even as models become more capable?
The models got dramatically better. Between 2023 and 2025, scores on graduate-level science questions jumped from 35% to 87%, and API prices fell from $60 to $0.40. Enterprise results went the other way: the share of companies scrapping AI projects before production rose from 17% to 42% in a single year.
Those benchmarks measure raw reasoning. They can’t tell you whether that reasoning works inside a specific company. We think of it as P = f(I, C): performance depends on intelligence and context. A model can know what “revenue” means in general and still have no idea that your finance team counts bookings while your sales team counts ARR. Strong intelligence with weak context gives you confident hallucination at scale.

Is context now a bigger bottleneck than compute for enterprise AI adoption? What are you seeing among customers?
Yes. Intelligence is becoming table stakes: every company will soon have roughly the same models at roughly the same price. Context is what separates them. A CDO at a Fortune 500 retailer put it this way: “Building the agent takes 5 minutes. Giving it business context takes 5 months.”
Another customer, the head of AI at a global bank, rolled out a thousand analytics agents in a month, and 90% were abandoned because the business didn’t trust the answers. Compute was never the issue in either case. The agents were missing the definitions, rules, and the know-how the business runs on.
As AI agents move from answering questions to taking actions, what are the biggest governance risks?
An agent that answers wrongly produces a bad answer. An agent that acts wrongly issues the refund, changes the record or sends the email.
Take a contact-centre agent handling “I was charged twice.” It has to know whether that’s a duplicate charge or a hold that will release on its own. It has to follow the dispute playbook. And it has to know it can approve $50 without escalating but nothing more. The risks sit in those last two layers: agents acting outside their authority, and actions nobody can trace.
As one VP of AI platforms at a top five insurer told us, “When an agent gets it wrong, I can’t trace which piece of context caused it, let alone roll it back.” Governance has to live inside the context itself: versioned, approved and auditable.
How should enterprises make their data, business rules, and institutional knowledge ready for AI agents?
Every agent needs three kinds of context. Knowledge is what things mean. Expertise is how work gets done. Norms are what’s allowed. Most of it already exists, scattered across warehouses, BI tools, SOPs, tickets and Slack threads.
Don’t write it from scratch. Mine it from the systems where it already lives, then have the people who know the business review and certify it. That’s how our context agents work. Customers accept 90% of the descriptions they write, and they’ve saved more than 209,000 hours of manual work.
Are you seeing enterprises shift from AI experimentation to deploying agents in production? What has changed in the past year?
Yes. Around 45% of enterprises are already putting agents into production. The question has changed from “can we build an agent?” to “can we trust it, and can we run thirty of them?”
That shift was also evident to us during the Accenture Tech Next Challenge 2026, an annual challenge for early- and growth-stage deep-tech startups in India, where Atlan won the Digital Core category. Our conversations with Accenture’s business leaders and technology ecosystem increasingly centred on what it takes to deploy AI reliably at enterprise scale, rather than simply experiment with it.
That’s also where the complexity starts to increase. Enterprises hit three walls as they scale. First, getting a single agent accurate enough that people actually use it. Second, managing context once several agents depend on it: who owns a definition, who approves a change, what breaks when it changes.
Third, keeping one version of the truth across dozens of agent platforms that each learn independently. A year ago, most companies were at the first wall. Now many are at the second.
With agent sprawl becoming a concern, will enterprises need to manage AI agents like employees, with identities, permissions, and audit trails?
Identities and permissions are necessary, but sprawl creates a deeper problem: every agent platform builds its own version of your business. One agent thinks revenue means bookings, another thinks it means ARR, and a third doesn’t know. You didn’t build five agents. You built five silos.
So enterprises need to manage the context their agents run on as carefully as they manage the agents themselves, with one shared, governed source every agent draws from, and every correction flowing back into it. People should stay on the loop, setting policy and reviewing exceptions, rather than approving each step.
Do you see the context layer becoming a distinct part of the enterprise AI stack, alongside data, compute, and models?
It already is. Data platforms store the data, models reason over it, and the AI control plane routes and monitors it. None of them is responsible for meaning. The context layer sits between your data estate and your agents.
It holds the data graph, the semantics and the skills, and serves them to any agent through MCP, APIs or SQL. It has to stay independent, because no enterprise runs on a single AI platform, and in the words of one Fortune 100 CTO, “I don’t want my business IP locked into any one of these platforms.”
What is one thing enterprises are currently underestimating about building an AI-ready data foundation?
That context compounds. Most teams treat AI readiness as a one-off cleanup project: tidy the data, document the tables, move on. But metrics change, policies change and products get renamed. A CMO we work with found their sales agent still pitching last quarter’s positioning, and nobody could say what else depended on it.
The enterprises that pull ahead treat context as a living asset. Every agent interaction, correction and edge case feeds back into a shared layer, so each new agent starts smarter than the last. Our participation in the Accenture Tech Next Challenge 2026 reinforced this view: the organisations moving fastest have stopped treating context as documentation and started treating it as intellectual property. Your context is your IP. Keep it yours.

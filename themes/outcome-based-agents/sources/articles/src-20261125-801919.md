More than 
2,000 GW of renewable energy projects are awaiting grid connections
 worldwide, according to industry leaders who warn that infrastructure constraints — not generation costs — are becoming a major obstacle to the energy transition.

Ahead of COP31’s Energy Day, representatives from the Global Solar Council (GSC), Global Renewables Alliance (GRA), Ember and Australian Renewable Energy Agency (ARENA) warned that grid constraints, insufficient storage and lengthy interconnection queues threaten to slow renewable energy deployment. 

The 
International Energy Agency (IEA)
 estimates that at least 1,700 GW of advanced-stage renewable projects and 600 GW of utility-scale battery projects were awaiting grid connections in 2025.

“The price question has been settled,” Global Solar Council CEO Sonia Dunlop said. “So it’s really time to look at that wider system.”
 

Dunlop said standalone solar averages $40 to $50 per MWh globally, with annual investment reaching approximately $500 billion in solar and another $100 billion in battery storage.

Global Renewables Alliance CEO Bruce Douglas called for 
interconnection queue reforms, faster permitting and better use of existing transmission infrastructure
.

“The fastest and cheapest grid is the one you don’t build,” Douglas said.

IEA estimates that grid-enhancing technologies and regulatory reforms could free enough capacity to connect 1,200 to 1,600 GW of projects currently stalled in interconnection queues, potentially reducing the need for some costly network expansions.

Battery installations are accelerating, but national storage targets remain below international commitments.

According to 
Ember, an independent global energy think tank
, battery installations reached approximately 104 GW in 2025, bringing cumulative global battery capacity to roughly 267 GW.

Including pumped hydro, total global storage capacity reached approximately 430 GW, according to figures presented during the briefing.

Meanwhile, 
national 2030 storage targets have nearly tripled since COP29, reaching 822 GW
 — still 45% below the 1,500 GW international storage pledge adopted in 2024.

The gap highlights a disconnect between national policy commitments and international ambitions, even as actual storage deployment accelerates.

In the United States, developers added approximately 15 GW of utility-scale battery storage in 2025, according to the 
U.S. Energy Information Administration
.

“Governments need to catch up with the reality,” said Ufuk Alparslan, Ember’s regional lead for Türkiye and the Caucasus.

Battery duration is also becoming increasingly important as developers shift toward longer-duration configurations to manage variable renewable generation and meet electricity demand beyond peak solar production hours.

Australia illustrates both the opportunities and challenges of integrating distributed energy resources at scale.

Nic Dorward, ARENA’s general manager of public affairs, said one in three Australian homes has rooftop solar, and more than 700,000 residential batteries have been installed.

The 
Australian Energy Market Operator’s 2026 Integrated System Plan
 identifies opportunities to reduce some transmission investments through strategically located batteries and generation closer to electricity demand.

Electric vehicles could provide additional flexibility.

ARENA modeling cited by Dorward suggests that bidirectional charging using 10% of Australia’s projected EV battery capacity could meet approximately 37% of the National Electricity Market’s storage requirements.

But coordinating millions of distributed energy assets presents operational challenges for grid operators.

“It is a great problem to have,” Dorward said.

The four organizations support the 
COP31 presidency’s goal of increasing electricity’s share of global final energy consumption to 35% by 2035
, but argue that achieving it will require corresponding investment in grids, storage and system flexibility.

For industry leaders, the priority at COP31 is moving beyond targets toward policies that accelerate construction and attract private capital.

“The pledge is a pledge,” Douglas said. “We now need to see national policies and frameworks in place.”

As Dunlop put it, “The constraint is now the wider system.”

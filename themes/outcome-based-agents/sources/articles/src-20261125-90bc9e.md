    Little Free Library: The story behind the pop-up ‘book nooks’

Maryam Moss
, 
Political Editor
 

November 25, 2026

 

Maryam Moss
 
The first little free library at Oakland University was created by Kresge Library’s Sustainability Committee. It is located to the left of the Suzanne M.
Frankie Cafe on the first floor of Kresge. 

 

Oakland County, MI 
 — 
A small bookshelf sits next to the Creation Station at Kresge Library, just beyond the booths and tables where OU students congregate to study. Its spines teeter like a row of dominoes along smooth wooden shelves and include everything from rare classics to guides on U.S. coins to thick reference volumes. It is a collection of unlikely candidates — a literary museum within the four-floored cavern of the library. 

Founded by
 Katie Greer
 (Ph.D)
, an OU professor, Fine and Performing Arts librarian at Kresge Library and chair of its Sustainability Committee, the mission of this little library is simple: to promote reading as leisure. 

The informal book swap isn’t the only one of its kind. 

The Little Free Libraries (
LFL
) nonprofit, which provided the inspiration for Kresge’s own chartered little library, supports a growing chain of communally managed book sharing boxes across the globe. As of 2025, there were 200,000 registered little libraries worldwide.

Most use an honor system, which recommends visitors to take books, return them at their convenience or restock a book sharing box with an equivalent number of titles. 

Michigan hosts an estimated total 
of 2,398 LFLs
. 

82% of these are located in the Metro Detroit area across parks, under the awnings of buildings and within public or school libraries like Kresge. 

Greer illustrated how the little library supplements the services Kresge Library already offers including programs, open-house hours for incoming students, the Makerspace and items for checkout at the 
Library of Things
. 

She said: “We try and make sure there are opportunities for students to have things to do like step out of that academic headspace and just relax, because it’s important to be able to know when to take that break.”

When it comes to official library collections, however, Greer identified a gap between books that serve an academic purpose and those that offer an imaginative, leisurely experience to readers. 

“[A lot] of people read for pleasure and escapism,” Greer said. But because we’re an academic library, we don’t have a lot of the material that might fill that need.”

Kresge’s volunteer-supported little free library offers a range of selections to the OU community, at a time when most reading may take place in academic or professional, 
rather than informal,
 
contexts
. 

Over 20 years, 
the share of American adults who say they read for pleasure
 
dropped by 12 percentage points. At the same time, a growing number of public schools nationwide report a 
literacy crisis
 in which students 
struggle to achieve grade-level proficiency
 in reading. 

Although little free libraries are relatively small in both capacity and frequency, enthusiasts believe the philosophy behind them can help restore the sense of novelty 
long associated with reading
. 

Renee Perkins
, owner of the children’s resale 
The Funky Frog
 in Downtown Rochester, summarized this effect. 

“You never know what you’ll find,” she said of the mystique of the cottage-shaped little libraries. 

Perkins’ own fuchsia and teal “book nook” may look like a fairy house but was formerly a kitchen cabinet.

The Funky Frog Little Free Library on Main St. in Downtown Rochester, MI contains dog treats and books for all ages.

Established with the help of her father in 2016, 
the Funky Frog Little Free Library
 became the first of its kind in the lively business district of Main Street. Perkins sees it as a way to make a positive impression on kids and parents, who are her primary customers, while meeting the wider community’s needs for quality, accessible books that can be borrowed on the go. 

People typically do not approach a little library with a certain title in mind, she explained, but an encounter often begins with simple curiosity — particularly for the youngest visitors who arrive excited to search for picture books. 

For parents shopping at the Funky Frog, the promise to hunt for books after a visit can redirect an impatient child’s attention. 

Perkins’ shared observations evoked scenes of passersby slowing to skim pages under lampposts and children — intrigued by the pink and blue-toned fairy house — reaching up to discover their next read. 

There is evidence to suggest that 
consistent reading habits are reinforced when
 a first interaction with a book
 
is not defined
 by
 privilege
, borrowing timelines or
 other barriers to access
.

When it comes to 
rising literary censorship
 across U.S. public and school libraries, the informal borrowing system of the little libraries 
may make a difference
.

Beth Wallis
, associate dean of Kresge Library, noted that these self-styled boxes can operate as sites of positive protest, at which frequently banned or challenged books are introduced and potentially exchanged within a community: an idea she is enthusiastic about.

She recalled a time when an individual checked out 100 books from a library, intending to limit access to particular titles.

Community members, who recognized this as a quiet act of censorship, brought an equivalent number of books to replace them.

Wallis, however, believes that while little libraries enable individuals to act as stewards of reading choice, policy-based strategies are needed.

“I fear that Little Free Libraries will not serve that purpose [of preventing book bans] thoroughly,” she said. 

She drew attention to the quantity of chartered little libraries, which number at 200,000 worldwide, and the conscious effort needed to change reading habits through LFL educational resources and collection development practices. 

Although the little libraries are free, their “take a book, leave a book” motto is meant to inspire attitudes of giving back. 

Perkins expressed that visitors’ contributions to her little library help support its mission of literacy, describing it as “self-managed, self-fulfilling.” 

On a stop at her little library, they might clean out the interior, bring new titles to restock the shelves after taking a book or bring a few dollars to refill its jar of dog treats — also available at the Funky Frog Little Free Library.

Delia Chandler, executive director of the nonprofit 
Let Us R.E.S.T.
 (Respect Each Other and Stand Together) works with volunteers, book-suppliers and local organizations to sustain the impact of her little free libraries in Benton Harbor.  

One of her most frequently visited little libraries is a dedication. 

Sponsored by 
Present Pillars
, a Benton Harbor-based nonprofit that empowers fathers through community service and parenthood resources, the little library was established in honor of an infant who passed away from a heart condition. 

According to Chandler, its debut brought communities together to have supportive conversations about navigating loss and grief. The library’s design, which included a photo collage, was also coordinated with the family’s input — presenting an example of how responsive stewardship can transform a book box into a personal endowment for literacy.

The dedication little free library in Benton Harbor MI, sponsored by Present Pillars. Photo provided by Delia Chandler, Executive Director of the nonprofit Let Us R.E.S.T.

She also drew attention to the framework of social consciousness behind little libraries, describing them as miniature-sized public libraries. Although borrowing is informal and need-based, the responsibility to replenish or replace items taken from a book box is understood. 

Chandler said: “That’s how it keeps going — through that consciousness … it does help with sustainability and recycling and different areas if you want to tie it into that.”

Betsy Raczkowski works as a librarian at the Rochester Hills Public Library (
RHPL
), which resides less than a mile away from Perkins’ own little library.

As a librarian, I think it’s beautiful … communities take it into their own hands to ensure book access for people who don’t have access to a public library,” she said, explaining how the independently run LFLs 
can support the services
 larger institutions already provide.

She highlighted the importance of inclusive literary spaces — whether they are book-sharing boxes or traditional libraries. 

 “A big part of collection development is knowing the community you live in,” she said. 

Chandler agrees. 

“It’s more of a community input because it’s not like you’re trying to do all the work by yourself,” she said. 

                                                               “You’re actively doing it in partnership.”

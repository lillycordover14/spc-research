AI Agent Insurance Reaches Law Firms as Testudo Covers Crosby&nbsp;&nbsp;FinanceX Magazine

[page text]
Testudo
, a specialist AI liability insurer and 
Lloyd's of London
 coverholder, has agreed to insure the AI agents working inside 
Crosby
, a New York law firm that pairs licensed attorneys with autonomous software to review commercial contracts. The policy is among the first to place standalone AI agent insurance behind agents carrying out billable professional work, rather than the consumer chatbots and content tools that have defined the market so far.
The cover, drawn from Lloyd's capacity, is designed to absorb third-party liabilities created when Crosby's agents handle contract review, a task the firm says it completes in hours rather than weeks. For finance teams and investors watching AI move into regulated professional services, the deal is a concrete signal that insurers are starting to price agent risk as a category of its own.
What does the Testudo and Crosby deal cover?
Testudo is insuring the professional output of Crosby's AI agents, not the firm's software licences. Crosby operates as a registered law firm that uses AI agents for a first pass on commercial contracts, with licensed attorneys reviewing the work and the firm taking direct liability for the finished product. Its agents handle master service agreements, data processing agreements and non-disclosure agreements, supported by paralegal agents that answer routine requests on demand.
Testudo, which describes itself as the largest specialist writer of standalone AI liability insurance for US companies, has provided cover backed by A+ rated Lloyd's capacity. Chief executive and co-founder George Lewin-Smith said AI-native firms are the core market the insurer was built to serve, and that fresh liabilities will surface as agents take on more sophisticated work. Crosby chief executive Ryan Daniels framed the policy as a way to stay ahead of regulation, arguing that firms able to show their agents are already insured will hold an advantage as the rules catch up with the technology.
Testudo's programme reaches $9.25m per insured, after the insurer expanded capacity in March 2026 with additional Lloyd's syndicate support alongside its anchor syndicate, 
Apollo
. Its panel also includes 
QBE
.
Why can't standard insurance policies cover AI agents?
The standard market has spent 2026 writing AI out of its policies rather than into them. Effective January 2026, the Insurance Services Office introduced generative AI exclusion endorsements for commercial general liability forms, giving US carriers a route to strip AI-related exposure from the cover businesses already hold. 
Verisk
, ISO's parent, reported strong carrier interest in adopting the language.
That leaves a gap. A company whose AI system causes harm may find its general liability policy silent or expressly excluded, and the problem is sharper for agents than for chatbots. Agents act on instructions and take steps on a user's behalf, which makes their outputs harder to slot into wordings written for advisory tools. Testudo positions its product as excess and surplus cover, the corner of the market built for risks that are new and poorly suited to standard lines.
Litigation is moving the same way. Generative AI lawsuits in the United States surged by roughly 978% between 2021 and 2025, on figures drawn from Testudo's litigation monitoring and reported across the insurance trade press, as copyright, data and output-quality claims multiplied. A contract-reviewing agent sits close to exactly that kind of exposure.
How big is Crosby, and who is backing it?
Crosby is well capitalised for a firm founded in 2024. It raised a $60m Series B in March 2026, co-led by 
Lux Capital
 and 
Index Ventures
, taking total funding to about $85m. Earlier rounds included a $5.8m seed led by 
Sequoia Capital
 and a $20m Series A in October 2025, with law firm 
Cooley
 among the backers alongside 
Bain Capital Ventures
 and investor Elad Gil.
The firm, led by co-founders Ryan Daniels and John Sarihan, counts 
Cognition
, 
Ramp
 and 
Cursor
 among its customers, and says it closes deals in hours rather than weeks. The AI liability policy adds a layer on top of the malpractice cover Crosby already carries as a registered law firm, a structure that matters as clients, investors and regulators weigh how far to trust agent-led legal work.
What does the deal mean for professional-services AI?
Agent risk is becoming insurable, and that shifts the adoption calculus. Businesses deploying AI already lean on governance policies, technical guardrails and human review, but residual liability remains, and insurance is the mechanism that transfers it. As agents move from drafting assistants to systems that act, the firms deploying them will face a sharper question about who pays when an agent gets something wrong.
Insuring a law firm's agents is a pointed test of that model, because legal work carries clear professional-liability stakes. The arrangement gives both sides a template: an AI-native firm can point to affirmative cover when selling to cautious enterprise buyers, and an insurer gathers underwriting data on a frontier exposure. Similar deals are likely to follow across accountancy, consulting and other professional services where agents are starting to carry billable work.
Why this matters to FinanceX readers
For investors and finance professionals, this is an early read on how the AI economy gets underwritten. Standalone AI liability cover is becoming a gating factor for enterprise adoption in regulated sectors, and the firms that can show affirmative, Lloyd's-backed protection will clear procurement and compliance faster than those that cannot. The deal also marks a commercial opening for specialist insurers: as standard carriers exclude AI, the risk migrates to the excess and surplus market, where pricing power and proprietary claims data accrue to first movers. 
The question for the rest of the market is whether traditional carriers follow Testudo into affirmative agent cover, or cede the category.

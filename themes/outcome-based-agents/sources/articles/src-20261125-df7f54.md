Neighbors Abigail Erickson-Torres and Rosalie Wade are helping students discover career paths in aviation and aerospace on a daily basis
 
By 
Syra Castillo
|
2026-11-25T17:47:57-06:00
November 25th, 2026
|
 

Photography by Tanner Garza

A
 
student’s first visit to the Frontiers of Flight Museum might look like exploring a replica of the Apollo Seven, a demonstration of aerodynamics or a glimpse inside a portable planetarium.

Abigail Erickson-Torres, president and CEO of the 
Frontiers of Flight Museum
, and Rosalie Wade, the museum’s director of education, share a commitment to making aviation and aerospace accessible to students and visitors of all ages.

Wade, a lifelong Lake Highlands neighbor and Lake Highlands High School alumna, joined the museum in 2016 after previously working at the Perot Museum of Nature and Science.

Erickson-Torres joined in July 2023, after finding out about the president vacancy at the museum.

“I (had) started getting my private pilot license, and my husband’s very involved in aviation, and I wanted to do something really fun,” Erickson-Torres says. 

“I’m a turnaround CEO, able to turn around nonprofits who are coming out of something and need a boost to rebrand and reset people in the pathway,” she says. 

The museum welcomes approximately 75,000 students each year through school field trips, campus outreach programs and public educational offerings. Wade says the education department has worked to make those experiences more interactive. 

“We have a variety of aerospace STEM-themed camps for students during the summer,” she says. “We also have a variety of public events that we host throughout the year for everybody. Moon Day is one of our most popular events.” 

Held annually to mark the anniversary of the Apollo 11 moon landing, 
Moon Day
 highlights the past, present and future of space exploration. Wade says the event typically draws more than 1,000 attendees. This year’s event, held July 18, featured former NASA astronaut Carl E. Walz as its keynote speaker. 

Wade says the education department team has refreshed its programming to give students more chances to experiment and learn through participation. 

“We think about how can we make a paper fly,” Wade says. “Well, you have to fold it into a shape to provide more lift to fight gravity, all that stuff, and then we introduce the concept of launching the plane with a little rubber band launcher so that we provide more thrust so that it can go farther.”

For example, the department hosts teacher professional development sessions to educate teachers on different ways to explain scientific topics like forces and gravity.

One special program that students and the public can expect in 2027 is the museum’s Aerospace Launchpad. 

The space will feature career exploration pods, developed in partnership with PPG Aerospace, Airbus, the Texas Space Commission, Texas Woman’s University and Dallas College. Erickson-Torres says the project is unlike anything else in the country. 

Additional resources will introduce visitors to smaller aviation businesses and employees at Dallas Love Field, including potential careers as flight attendants, mechanics and other aviation professions. 

The Launchpad will be open seven days a week with museum staff on site to help with any questions. 

The biggest component of the Launchpad is not the pods, but the Launchpad’s Mission Crew, which will consist of youth volunteers ranging from eighth grade through college. 

“These students can earn volunteer hours with us. If they earn enough volunteer hours, they can get paid internships to help us with camps, which will lead into mentorship opportunities for them,” Erickson-Torres says. “Another piece of it is that these students are going to get access to a flight plan. That way, they can kind of discover what they truly want to do.” 

The Mission Crew’s flight plan will consist of students lining out their career interests to help them identify their strengths and explore the next steps in their journey. 

Erickson-Torres vividly remembers a conversation she had with a young girl from Irving who had never been on a plane before or visited a flight museum before. She says the young girl was skeptical about careers in aviation since she was interested in becoming a chef. 

She explained that airlines also need catering professionals and that working in airline hospitality could give her the opportunity to develop culinary skills while traveling the world.

“I could see her brain firing off all this imagination of what she could potentially do,” Erickson-Torres says.

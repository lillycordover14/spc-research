The Democratic Adaptive Advantage and Its Limits&nbsp;&nbsp;SLDinfo.com

[page text]
The last installment closed on the erosion of knowledge — the quiet, cognitive form of the peace dividend that leaves institutions holding their weapons while losing their capacity to understand the environment those weapons operate in. This installment turns to a question that follows naturally from that erosion: what, if anything, do democratic systems actually have going for them in a sustained strategic competition, and what has to happen for that advantage to become real rather than assumed?

The Authoritarian Speed Argument, and Its Limit

Preziosa starts by taking the opposing case seriously rather than dismissing it. There’s a real temptation to assume authoritarian systems hold an inherent strategic advantage — they can mobilize resources rapidly, concentrate decision-making, suppress political opposition, and impose costs on their own populations without needing consent. Under some conditions, particularly when competition rewards speed of mobilization and concentrated resources around narrow objectives, these characteristics genuinely matter.

But Preziosa draws a distinction that does real work here: the capacity to mobilize rapidly should not be confused with a permanent capacity to adapt. Speed and adaptability are not the same thing, and conflating them is exactly the mistake that makes authoritarian systems look more formidable than their long-run track record actually supports.

What Democracies Have Instead

Democratic systems carry a different set of potential advantages. Their societies hold distributed knowledge. Their economies bring together competing firms and technological ecosystems. Their universities, research institutions, armed forces, markets, and civil societies can generate innovation through decentralized experimentation rather than top-down direction. Alliances among democracies extend this diversity across national borders entirely — distributing capabilities, technology, intelligence, production, operational experience, and strategic risk across networks far larger than anything available to a single state acting alone.

Here Preziosa inserts the qualification that gives this section its real force: none of this is self-executing. Diversity becomes strategic power only when a system can connect, scale, and operationalize what that diversity produces. Otherwise diversity just remains fragmentation — plenty of good ideas and capable institutions, none of it converted into coherent collective action. The real democratic advantage, on his account, doesn’t lie in pluralism by itself. It lies in the potential capacity to turn distributed knowledge and decentralized initiative into coherent action without destroying the diversity that made adaptation possible in the first place.

Why the Architecture of Relationships Matters as Much as the Resources

This is why, Preziosa argues, the connections between institutions matter as much as what sits inside any one of them. The contemporary defense ecosystem increasingly crosses boundaries that used to be treated as separate — armed forces and defense companies now sit alongside civilian technology firms, universities, infrastructure providers, energy systems, logistics networks, financial institutions, national governments, NATO, the EU, and operational partners, all functioning as part of the same system whether or not they’re organized that way on paper.

Widening this ecosystem doesn’t require militarizing civilian society. It requires recognizing that systems which remain politically and institutionally distinct have nonetheless become strategically interdependent. Transportation networks built for civilian commerce also determine how fast military forces can reinforce a front. Energy grids sustain civilian life and defense production simultaneously. Digital infrastructure carries commercial activity while also enabling military logistics, communications, and command. Semiconductor ecosystems built largely for commercial markets condition what advanced military systems are even possible. Commercial space-based services increasingly intersect with military sensing, navigation, communications, and situational awareness. The line between military capability and the wider systems that make military capability usable has become very hard to hold in place.

Deterrence, as a result, has to be understood in increasingly systemic terms. Military credibility doesn’t reside only in the forces immediately available or the sophistication of individual platforms — it lives in the resilience of the networks that let those forces move, communicate, receive information, replenish losses, and stay politically sustainable. A brigade can be tactically excellent and still strategically fragile if the energy, data, logistics, transportation, industrial, and political systems underneath it can’t keep functioning under sustained pressure.

The democratic adaptive advantage, where it actually exists, comes from combining distributed initiative with this kind of systemic connectivity. Democracies don’t need to copy the centralized mobilization mechanisms of authoritarian systems to compete with them — their real strength is connecting a wider diversity of knowledge, institutions, technologies, firms, allies, and operational experience than any single-actor system can match. But that potential only becomes strategically relevant once institutions can actually identify useful innovation, connect it to operational requirements, scale it through industry, protect the infrastructure it depends on, and sustain the political legitimacy needed for prolonged effort. The democratic advantage, in the end, isn’t diversity itself. It’s the capacity to convert diversity into adaptive power.

Europe Inside a Wider Strategic Network

Preziosa carries this argument directly into a claim about what a stronger European pillar can and can’t be. It cannot be conceived as a geographically self-contained European defense system, because Europe’s security already depends on wider networks of technology, production, intelligence, finance, energy, critical materials, and innovation — many of which extend well beyond both the EU and the formal geography of the Atlantic Alliance. The Ukraine war has made these connections harder to miss, reinforcing what Preziosa reads as Laird’s broader argument: NATO is a regional military alliance embedded inside a strategic system whose technological, industrial, and economic relationships are becoming genuinely global.

That doesn’t reduce the case for greater European responsibility — it clarifies what that responsibility actually means. European strategic agency, Preziosa argues, should be measured less by separation from the United States or by eliminating external dependencies, and more by Europe’s capacity to take on greater responsibility within a changing Atlantic system while still preserving freedom of action when specific resources, capabilities, or relationships become constrained. The real question isn’t whether Europe can become independent of the networks its power depends on. It’s whether participation in those networks strengthens Europe’s capacity to act, or instead creates dependencies capable of producing strategic paralysis.

This distinction only gets more important as strategic networks cross theaters. European and Indo-Pacific security aren’t interchangeable, but their industrial, technological, economic, and geopolitical foundations increasingly intersect — through semiconductor supply chains, critical materials, advanced manufacturing, space systems, digital infrastructure, maritime routes, and defense-industrial cooperation. Interdependence, Preziosa is careful to note, can create efficiency and strategic depth, but it can equally create vulnerability when critical relationships become concentrated, irreplaceable, or politically unreliable.

The objective he lands on is neither autarky nor dependence, but what he calls resilient interdependence: staying deeply connected to the United States and a wider network of democratic partners, while holding enough political, military, industrial, technological, and cognitive capacity to retain freedom of action when parts of that network are disrupted or allied priorities temporarily diverge. Properly built, greater European strategic agency wouldn’t weaken the Atlantic relationship — it would make it more resilient, precisely by reducing how much collective security depends on any single actor remaining continuously available.

Abstract
Cellular adaptation requires a delicate balance between preserving distinct cell identity while maintaining the ability to sense and respond to environmental changes. This is particularly critical during developmental transitions when cells must adapt to ensure survival without losing their function and fate. The transition between naive and primed pluripotency serves as a unique model for investigating cellular plasticity and adaptability. The transcriptional signatures of naive and primed states are well characterized, yet the mechanisms that balance state-specific identity with cellular adaptability remain poorly understood. These distinct pluripotent states can be captured and manipulated in vitro using the widely established culture system of mouse embryonic stem cells. Here, we examined the transcriptional response to osmostress in naive and primed stem cells at single-cell resolution as a function of cell identity. Upon stress, primed cells exhibited a canonical response characterized by widespread gene upregulation and increased transcription burst size. In contrast, naive cells, displayed a constitutive stress-like transcriptional program associated with elevated JNK activity in the absence of stress. These unique transcriptional properties and differential JNK signaling confer a broad stress-resistant phenotype that serves as a hallmark of naive cells. Our findings demonstrate how cell state-dependent transcriptional profiles generate diverse adaptive responses to stress and shed light on the mechanisms that integrate and balance pluripotency maintenance with stress adaptation.

Introduction
Pluripotent Stem Cells (PSCs) are characterized by their ability to self-renew and differentiate into all embryonic cell types [
1
]. These cells are largely classified into two types: naive and primed, which represent the pluripotent states of the pre- and post-implantation epiblast, respectively [
2
,
3
,
4
]. The transition from naive to primed stem cells is one of the earliest cell fate decisions. Naive cells are undifferentiated cell-autonomous systems that maintain ground-state pluripotency through symmetric self-renewal and blastocyst colonization potential [
2
,
3
,
4
]. Primed cells acquire a formative state marked by distinct molecular and epigenetic features that enable their response to lineage-inducing signals. Maintaining stem cell transcriptional identity and cell fate during the naive to primed stem cell transition is crucial for organism development [
1
, 
4
]. For example, the loss of naive stem cell identity can lead to abnormal differentiation, hamper embryonic implantation, or cause death [
1
, 
3
]. However, this transition is accompanied by dramatic changes in the extracellular environment and cellular morphology during embryonic development [
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]. PSCs must respond robustly to environmental changes while maintaining their identity [
10
]. Therefore, understanding the transcriptional response of stem cells to stress is essential for understanding stem cell identity and fitness potential.
Mouse embryonic stem cells (mES) inhabit a highly dynamic microenvironment ranging from mechanical stress to changes in both extracellular stimuli and cell morphology that challenge cellular homeostasis [
8
]. Exit from naive pluripotency is accompanied by significant morphological alterations as cells experience changes in membrane tension and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which leads to enhanced ERK signaling activity, a crucial step for pluripotency exit [
11
,
12
,
13
].
During early embryonic development, cavitation formation is mediated by a change in osmolarity that drives blastocyst expansion and increases membrane tension [
3
, 
14
,
15
,
16
]. These changes represent multifactorial stressors that cells of the inner cell mass must withstand during this critical developmental window [
3
]. Osmostress is a potent disruptor of the cellular state, capable of inducing dramatic changes in chromatin organization, altering nuclear morphology, and mimicking the mechanical stress generated by membrane deformation resembling that faced during the naive state transition [
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]. Although stem cells possess remarkable adaptive capabilities, the precise mechanisms underlying their adaptive strategies, the extent of heterogeneity in their responses, and how this affects adaptive strategies or impacts cell fate remain less understood [
21
, 
22
].
Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, particularly the ERK1/2 signaling pathway, plays a pivotal role in governing the progression from naive to formative pluripotency [
11
, 
12
]. These distinct pluripotent states can be maintained in vitro under specific culture conditions: naive cells can be stabilized using a two-inhibitor cocktail (2i) comprising GSK3 and MEK inhibitors, whereas primed cells are maintained in serum-containing medium supplemented with leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), which supports a more heterogeneous and developmentally advanced state [
2
]. These culture conditions reflect the developmental changes that occur during early embryonic development and have been validated across various species, including mice and humans [
2
].
Here, we investigated the transcriptional responses of 2i-naive and primed cells to osmostress using high-resolution single-cell RNA-seq and discovered that cell state influences the transcriptional response to stress. Upon stress, primed cells exhibited a canonical response that included the upregulation of many genes, as well as a global increase in transcription burst size, which resulted in an increase in mRNA molecules. In contrast, naive cells in the absence of stress exhibit a basal stress-like transcriptome and higher basal JNK activity. These unique transcriptional properties confer a stress-resistant phenotype to naive cells. Our findings reveal how different pluripotent cell states employ distinct transcriptional and signaling strategies to respond to stress in vitro. These observations provide mechanistic insights into stem cell plasticity and stress adaptation in culture systems, with potential implications for understanding early developmental processes.
Results
Cellular identity determines the type of transcriptional response to stress and supersedes environmental stimuli
To study the interplay between the transcriptional response to stress and identity, we performed scRNA-seq transcriptome profiling of 2i-naive (grown in the presence of 2i cocktail, hereafter referred to as naive) and SL-primed (grown in serum/LIF hereafter referred to as primed) cells under control and low osmostress (65 mM NaCl 2 h) (Fig. 
1A
). A dose of 65 mM NaCl was selected as a sub-lethal osmotic perturbation that falls within falls within a similar order of magnitude as the combined ionic gradient sustained between the blastocoel and the surrounding medium in vivo (~60–80 mOsm across Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺) [
23
] and the continuous Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase-driven transport that accompanies cavitation [
15
], allowing us to probe osmoadaptive capacity in a regime relevant to the pre- and peri-implantation epiblast while preserving viability (Supplementary Fig. 
1A
). These conditions are sufficient to activate to a similar extent the stress activated protein kinase (SAPK) p38 in both primed and naive states (Supplementary Fig. 
1B, C
). As a model, we used a hybrid mouse ES cell line (F123) that has been extensively used to characterize allele-specific expression enabled by a high-density single nucleotide polymorphism [
24
]. To obtain full length transcriptome coverage, we applied SMART-seq2 [
25
] by isolating single live cells by Fluorescence Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) into 96 well plates (Supplementary Fig. 
1D
). From these, we generated full-length cDNA libraries and performed sequencing at a high depth of >1 million reads/cell to ensure the coverage of SNPs (see Methods) (Supplementary Fig. 
1E
). After removing low-quality cells, the resulting dataset included 705 cells in total, with >150 cells in each condition, with an average of 4500 genes/cell and at least 11,000 genes per condition expressed in at least 10 cells (Fig. 
1B
, Supplementary Data 
1
). We used these cells to generate a gene expression matrix and performed cell cycle regression to alleviate the effects on transcriptional heterogeneity (see Methods).

To validate the robustness of our dataset, we assessed the expression of hallmark genes upregulated in naive cells (naive signature) or primed cells (primed signature), as reported for bulk RNA-seq [
26

1C
, Supplementary Fig. 
1F
). We also assessed the comparability between bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data under control conditions by calculating the correlation coefficients for genes with detectable expression in both assays under basal conditions. The correlation was 0.63 (
p

27

28

1G

To investigate the clustering patterns of primed cells, we performed dimensionality reduction and UMAP projection using highly variable genes (see Methods). This analysis revealed that cells predominantly clustered according to cell identity (Fig. 
1D
), rather than stress (Fig. 
1E, F
). These results suggest that cell identity-dependent transcriptional signatures predominate over expression changes induced by stress.
Stress induces coordinated transcriptional heterogeneity in primed cells
To understand how stress-induced transcriptional changes affect cellular coordination and population heterogeneity, we used two approaches. First, we calculated the Global Coordination Level (GCL) for each cell identity, which quantifies the extent to which the expression of different genes is interdependent [
29
]. Upon osmostress, primed cells displayed a substantial increase in GCL, whereas naive cells showed reduced coordination in response to the same stress treatment. (Fig. 
2A
). We next hypothesized that osmostress could generate heterogeneity within the population. Thus, we quantified gene expression diversity per cell using Shannon entropy [
30
] across all conditions and identities based on normalized gene expression values. We found that primed cells displayed an increase in heterogeneity, which was not detectable in naive cells suggesting primed cells undergo stronger changes in transcription upon stress (Fig. 
2B, C
).

Stress globally modulates transcriptional dynamics that are dependent on cell identity
Given that transcriptional heterogeneity is significantly influenced by the stochastic nature of gene expression bursting [
31
], we sought to determine whether the observed stress-induced heterogeneity reflects changes in the underlying transcriptional dynamics. To this end, we leveraged allele-specific expression resolution of the hybrid F123 ES cell line and used SNP coverage to calculate the kinetic properties of transcriptional burst size and frequency (>6500 genes) as reported before [
32
] (Supplementary Fig. 
2A
, Supplementary Data 
2
). We first examined genome-wide bursting distributions to compare naive and primed cells under control and stress. Naive cells exhibited a higher bursting frequency than primed cells under basal conditions, and this difference persisted upon stress, as bursting frequency remained stable in both cell states (Fig. 
2D
, left). In contrast, bursting size changed with stress. While naive cells displayed larger burst sizes than primed cells under control conditions, they showed a small increase upon stress. Strikingly, primed cells exhibited a pronounced genome-wide increase in bursting size in response to stress (Fig. 
2D
, right). This stress-induced shift toward larger bursts was most evident by looking at the fold change of bursting size and frequency, where primed cells displayed a particularly strong global increase in burst size but not burst frequency upon stress when compared to naive cells (Fig. 
2E
). We next examined the interplay between burst size and frequency and its relation to differential expression patterns. As expected, we observed a global anticorrelation between the fold change in transcription burst size and frequency across all comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 
2B–D
). These results demonstrate that while naive cells maintain stable, high-frequency transcriptional bursting regardless of environmental conditions, primed cells exhibit a stress-dependent increase in burst size, suggesting distinct transcriptional regulatory mechanisms.
To examine how transcriptional dynamics relate to gene expression changes, we performed differential expression analysis between naive and primed control conditions, as well as between control and stress samples within each cell state (Supplementary Data 
3
, see below). We then stratified genes by their expression pattern (upregulated, downregulated, or unchanged, (|log2FC|≥ 1 or ≤-1, respectively; adjusted 
p
-value < 0.05)) to assess changes in burst parameters. In the comparison between naive and primed cells under control conditions, upregulated genes in naive cells displayed an increased burst frequency compared to downregulated and unresponsive genes (Fig. 
2F
, left), but showed modest differences in burst size (Fig. 
2F
, right). Upon osmotic stress, naive cells displayed minimal transcriptional changes, with only 28 genes being upregulated (see below) (Supplementary Data 
3
) when compared to unstressed naive cells. Despite this limited gene set, these genes showed increased burst frequency without changes in burst size (Fig. 
2G
). In contrast, primed cells exhibited a substantially larger transcriptional response, with 784 genes upregulated. Those showed a slight increase in bursting frequency and a more significant increase in burst size (Fig. 
2H
, Supplementary Data 
3
). These findings reveal that naive and primed cells employ distinct transcriptional strategies. Under basal conditions, naive cells primarily modulate burst frequency to regulate gene expression. Upon osmotic stress, naive cells maintain this strategy, but primed cells mount a more extensive transcriptional response characterized by a prominently genome-wide increased burst size. Thus, primed cells exhibit greater capacity for burst-size modulation in response to stress.
Cell identity influences the topology and degree of heterogeneity of the transcriptional response to stress
Given the different transcription dynamics, we next sought to dissect the architecture of the cell-state-specific response to osmostress by analyzing the dataset of naive and primed cells individually. In primed cells, UMAP visualization showed slight separation of control and osmostress samples into partially distinct clusters (Fig. 
3A
). The prototypical transcriptional response to stress across eukaryotes is characterized by the coordinated upregulation and downregulation of genes to promote adaptation. Consistent with this, primed cells mounted a large transcriptional response with 784 upregulated genes (|log2FC|≥ 1, adjusted 
p
-value < 0.05) (Fig. 
3B
). The fold changes of upregulated genes remained modest (log2FC ~ 1–1.5), likely reflecting the mild stress conditions employed (65 mM NaCl, 2 h treatment). Interestingly, almost no transcriptional repression was observed in this response (|log2FC|≤-1, adjusted 
p
-value < 0.05).

To understand the nature of the osmostress response in primed cells, we performed Gene Ontology enrichment analysis (GO) of the top upregulated genes, which were identified as enriched in categories related to: translation and ATP metabolism-related genes, including those involved in aerobic respiration, among the most overrepresented biological processes in primed cells, in agreement with the notion that osmostress triggers global changes in translation and metabolic patterns [
33
] (Supplementary Fig. 
3A
, Supplementary Data 
4
). Notably, we observed upregulation of aerobic respiration, a metabolic program characteristic of naive cells that rely on oxidative phosphorylation rather than the glycolysis typically employed by primed cells. Indeed, several naive pluripotency markers displayed slight stress-dependent upregulation in naive cells (Supplementary Fig. 
3B
), but not the hallmark naive signature (Fig. 
1C
).
Remarkably, the clustering of naive cells exhibited a more continuous distribution with lack of separation between the control and stress conditions (Fig. 
3C
). This strong overlap between conditions in naive cells is consistent with minor or nearly inexistent induction of the stress responsive program, with only 28 upregulated genes (adjusted 
p
-value < 0.05, |log2FC|≥ 1) (Fig. 
3D
). The small set of 28 genes induced in naive cells belong to the generic process of gene expression (GO analysis), suggesting that naive cells display an atypical reduced induction of the stress responsive genes (Supplementary Fig. 
3C
). Consistent with this, qPCR time-course profiling of stress-responsive genes (i.e. Clcn3, Gadd45b, Jun and Rsrp1) showed a transient induction in primed cells while it remained unchanged in naive cells (Supplementary Fig. 
3D
, Supplementary Table 
1
). These findings demonstrate divergent transcriptional responses among pluripotent cell states. Primed cells orchestrate a robust transcriptional program involving hundreds of genes related to metabolic adaptation and translation. In contrast, naive cells exhibit a remarkably constrained response with minimal gene induction. This difference could reflect either a fundamental lack of stress responsiveness in naive cells or the presence of a constitutive stress-adaptive in the absence of stress.
To understand the variability in gene expression, we exploited the single-cell resolution the fraction of primed cells expressing each of the 784 genes upregulated in primed cells (Fig. 
3E
). Most of these genes were already detectable under basal conditions (694/784, 88.5%), with 86% expressed in over 50% the population before stress and further induced upon stress (Fig. 
3E
). For 73% of the genes, stress increased the proportion of expressing cells by less than 10% (Supplementary Fig. 
3E
). In contrast, the remaining 290/794 (11.5%) of genes showed restricted expression of specific cell subsets under basal conditions (Fig. 
3E
). Similarly, in naive cells, despite the average log2FC of the induced genes is weak (log2FC 1.5) (Supplementary Fig. 
3F
), the frequency of expression is also defined under basal conditions, and the expression increases upon osmostress with only 16% genes restricted to less than 50% of the population (Fig. 
3F
). This suggests that cellular heterogeneity in stress responses is largely pre-established under basal conditions and osmostress primarily amplifies by increasing the fraction of cells inducing these genes.
Besides transcriptional reprogramming, one of the main outcomes of adaptive responses is cell cycle arrest. To this end, we scored and assigned each cell to a cell cycle phase according to the expression of phase-specific genes [
34
, 
35
]. In agreement with the degree of transcriptional response, primed cells displayed an accumulation of cells in G1, consistent with the reported arrest in this phase across several mouse and human cell lines, but not detectable in naive cells (Supplementary Fig. 
3G
) [
36
,
37
,
38
], further supporting the abrogated stress response in naive cells. We next asked whether the cell cycle stage influences the degree of transcriptional response to stress. We measured the average expression of upregulated genes in response to stress for the corresponding cell state (784 genes for primed and 28 genes in naive), as a function of the inferred cell cycle phase. We found no major differences in expression across all phases, and the induction of stress-responsive genes was cell cycle independent (Supplementary Fig. 
3H, I
, respectively). Overall, our data indicate that transcriptional responses are dependent on the cell state, but independent of the cell cycle stage.
Naive cells display basal stress-like transcriptome expression
Osmostress is a potent stressor that induces massive changes in chromatin structure and transcriptional reprogramming in many cell lines and organisms. At the transcriptional level, within the first few minutes, the outcome of osmostress involves the transient dissociation of chromatin-bound proteins, driven by alterations in ionic balance [
20
, 
39
,
40
,
41
]. This is followed by the re-association of these proteins as homeostasis is reestablished. To probe global changes in transcription, we expressed GFP-tagged BRD4, a chromatin-binding coactivator that helps connect Mediator with RNA Pol II. BRD4-GFP clusters at active transcription sites [
42
], forming condensates that are traceable using super-resolution microscopy. Upon exposure to stress, transcription condensates rapidly declined and to a comparable extent in both naive and primed cells. This suggests that both states perceive osmostress, as evidenced by similar p38 activation (Supplementary Fig. 
1B, C
). However, the two states diverged in their recovery kinetics, with condensate intensity returning to basal levels faster in naive cells (Fig. 
4A
, Supplementary Fig. 
4A
). This accelerated recovery, although an indirect readout, could align with a naive pre-adapted state.

Next, we sought to understand why naive cells display lower induction of the transcriptional response to stress. Having excluded deficient p38 SAPK signaling as the cause (Supplementary Fig. 
1B, C
), we reasoned that the lack of induction could be the result of an already higher basal expression of stress-responsive genes in naive cells, thereby limiting the necessity for further transcriptional upregulation upon stress. To test this hypothesis, we performed differential expression analysis comparing stressed naive cells with control primed cells and identified a large number of upregulated genes (
n
 = 1957) (Supplementary Fig. 
4B
). These genes corresponded primarily to the expected naive program (e.g., 
Nanog
, 
Tbx3
) but also included genes associated with the osmostress program detected in primed cells upon stress.
To validate whether the stress program was significantly expressed in untreated naive cells, we analyzed the overlap between genes upregulated in naive cells under unstressed conditions and those upregulated upon stress in primed cells. We found that 41% of the stress-upregulated genes in primed cells (322 out of 784) were already highly expressed in naive cells under control conditions (Supplementary Fig. 
4C
). Visualization of this basal stress naive signature in a heatmap (Fig. 
4B
) revealed that stress triggers a coordinated, global upregulation of the stress-response program in primed cells, while naive cells already exhibit higher expression of those genes in basal conditions. When we scored the expression of the basal stress naive signature (322 genes), across all conditions and found that while expression of these genes was low in unstressed primed cells and increased upon stress treatment, naive cells already displayed higher expression even in the absence of stress, reaching levels comparable to those achieved by stressed primed cells (Fig. 
4C
). Interestingly, the expression of this gene subset relied on increased bursting size (Supplementary Fig. 
4D, E
), suggesting that induction of stress genes upon stimuli genes may specifically require enhanced transcriptional bursting regardless of the cell state. The expression of the 322 intersecting genes (basal stress naive signature) was spread throughout the UMAP and did not belong to a specific subpopulation of cells (Fig. 
4D
). To understand the nature of the basal stress naive signature, we performed GO and found that functions like translation and oxidative phosphorylation were the most enriched terms (Supplementary Fig. 
4F
) in addition to an enrichment in stress-related transcription factors (Supplementary Fig. 
4G
). These findings demonstrate that naive cell transcriptomes already exhibit basal stress-like gene expression under control conditions, explaining their lack of transcriptional response to additional stress stimuli.
Next, we aimed to validate whether the basal-stress signature of 2i naive cells in vitro reflects the embryonic expression pattern in vivo by analyzing available scRNA-seq data during embryonic development [
43
]. We measured the expression of the basal-stress signature (
n
 = 322) across developmental stages in vivo using cells from early to late blastocyst-stage embryos (Nowotschin et al., 2019) [
43
]. First, we confirmed the transcriptional identity of these cells by the expression of naive marker genes (Tbx3 and Klf4), which, as expected, displayed a gradual decrease in expression over time (Supplementary Fig. 
4H
). To score the basal-stress signature, we used the 144 genes overlapping between datasets (see Methods). The expression of the signature was higher in early than in late stages of blastocyst formation (Fig. 
4E
), suggesting the stress program is at least partially recapitulated during embryonic development in vivo.
JNK signaling drives the basal stress-like transcriptome in naive cells
We then hypothesized that the basal stress-like transcriptome of naive cells could originate from increased SAPK signaling. To this end, we scored the signatures for the major SAPK, p38, and JNK, using publicly available gene sets associated with the activity of each MAPK (see Methods). As expected, due to the presence of MEKi in the 2i cocktail, the signature for ERK in naive cells under both basal and stress conditions was very low, consistent with the inhibition of MEK1 (MEKi). For p38 and JNK signatures, we detected a stress-dependent increase in expression in primed cells, but the expression of these genes was already notably higher in naive cells even in control conditions (Fig. 
4F
).
Across most cell types, p38 is the major MAPK responsible for osmoadaptation, and JNK, albeit activated, is often thought to contribute to a lesser extent [
44
, 
45
]. To determine the role of JNK in establishing the basal stress-like transcription program in naive cells, we performed qPCR to analyze the expression of cJUN genes (e.g., Rsrp1 and Clcn3). These genes were identified as upregulated upon stress in primed cells and, as expected, were also basally induced in naive cells in our scRNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. 
4I
). Preincubation (4 h) with the JNK inhibitor in the absence of stress specifically lowered the expression of both genes in naive cells (Fig. 
4G
). These results suggest a role for JNK signaling in shaping the transcriptome of 2i- naive cells.
To validate the implication of JNK activity in shaping the transcriptional response to stress, we measured the expression by qPCR of a classical stress-inducible gene for osmostress (
Fos
), which is upregulated in our scRNA-seq dataset for both naive and primed cells, and 
Hsp70
 as a heat stress-inducible gene [
46
]. Upon stress, both naive and primed cells induced 
Fos
 and 
Hsp70
 in response to osmostress and heat stress, respectively. However, this induction was selectively lost in naive cells when pretreated with JNK inhibitor, while primed cells maintained their stress response (Supplementary Fig. 
4J
). These findings further underline the importance of JNK activity in the adaptive MAPK pathway in naive cells.
Increased JNK activity is required to stablish the naive identity and stress-resistance phenotypes
Our in vitro data suggested that JNK signaling is engaged in 2i-naive cells and partially appears during the naive developmental window (Fig. 
4E, G
). However, transcriptomic signatures provide only an indirect readout of pathway activity. To measure JNK activity directly in vivo at single-cell resolution, we used the JNK Kinase Translocation Reporter (KTR), a genetically encoded biosensor that converts MAPK signaling into a change in subcellular localization that has previously been used to measure ERK activity in developing embryos [
47
]. Active JNK drives the reporter out of the nucleus, such that the cytoplasmic-to-nuclear fluorescence ratio (log2 C/N) provides a quantitative measure of kinase activity (Fig. 
5A

We then generated a transgenic mouse expressing the JNK KTR together with an H2B marker in mouse embryos and imaged JNK activity across preimplantation development, from the 8-cell stage through the morula (16-cell), the early and late blastocyst (Fig.

To understand the signaling environment of naive and primed cells, we followed the activation of the major MAPKs in primed cells and in the transition to naive identity upon the addition of the 2i cocktail. As expected, primed cells grown in serum/LIF displayed high basal ERK phosphorylation, which was rapidly and persistently suppressed upon addition of the 2i cocktail [

Shortly after the exposure of primed cells to the 2i cocktail, p38 phosphorylation increased, peaking at 100 min, but decreased to a lower but detectable level in stabilized cells. On the other hand, phosphorylation of JNK also occurred immediately after 2i addition and remained constant over time, even in 2i-stabilized naive cells (7 days) (Fig.

We sought to explore the correlation and functional impact between elevated JNK/p38 signaling and the basal stress-like transcriptome. To this end, we subjected primed and naive cells to increasing concentrations of osmostress ranging from mild (65 mM) to high (150 mM NaCl). While both identities displayed similar survival at low concentrations, naive cells displayed an osmostress-resistant phenotype by showing overall higher survival at most concentrations and remained nearly insensitive up to 115 mM NaCl (Fig.

We next tested if stress resistance is a widespread feature of the naive identity by testing the viability of primed and naive cells to a panel of stressors, each of which imposes distinct cellular adaptive strategies, including heat-, oxidative-, osmostress, and DNA damage (cisplatin). Remarkably, naive cells consistently displayed increased survival across all conditions, whereas primed cells showed a strong decrease in cell viability (<30% living cells) (Fig.

To assess whether sustained activation of JNK signaling (and, to a lesser extent p38 signaling) could mediate the naive stress-resistant phenotype, we measured cell viability following pretreatment with or without a chemical JNK inhibitor (JNKi, 2 h) prior to exposure to the four stressors. JNK inhibition significantly reduced the survival of naive cells upon stress to levels comparable to those of primed cells. This effect was stronger for osmostress and heat stress, but it also reduced their survival upon oxidative stress and cisplatin (Fig.

Discussion
Cells exist in a permanently fluctuating environment, making growth under steady-state conditions more of a theoretical scenario than a reflection of physiological reality. MAPK signaling presents a paradox: these pathways are essential for determining developmental cell fate decisions while simultaneously integrating and orchestrating adaptive responses to stress [
49
, 
50
]. Yet our molecular understanding of how developmental cell identity and stress adaptation interact and balance remains limited.
Our study reveals a fundamental principle governing stress adaptation in pluripotent stem cells in vitro, in which cellular identity determines both the transcriptional dynamics and the strength of the response to stress, influencing adaptive fitness. Through high-resolution single-cell analysis, we demonstrated that 2i-naive stem cells exhibit a constitutive stress-like transcriptional program that confers a broad spectrum of stress resistance, challenging the conventional view that stress responses are exclusively reactive adaptations. This defensive strategy manifests as naive cells maintaining a basal stress-responsive transcriptome under unstressed conditions, with 41% of osmostress-induced genes in primed cells already expressed in the absence of the stressor. Constitutive stress-responsive gene expression has been noted among populations in different biological systems, including yeast and certain mammalian cell types. Our observations suggest that 2i naive pluripotent stem cells display similar strategies in culture, though whether this represents a broadly conserved mechanism across organisms requires further investigation [
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
,
55
].
Osmotic stress conditions have recently been reported to help maintain pluripotency of mESCs, even after removal of the 2i-inhibitor cocktail [
40
]. Building on this observation, recent studies using genetic screens have identified JNK-JUN signaling as both a critical barrier to pluripotency exit and a mechanistic basis for enhanced stress resistance in naive pluripotent stem cells. JUN co-occupies stem cell enhancers with core pluripotency factors (OCT4, NANOG, SMAD2/3), inhibiting enhancer decommissioning [
56
]. Our findings in the 2i culture system are consistent with this mechanistic framework, suggesting that sustained JNK activation correlates with constitutive expression of stress-protective genes, including genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation and translation regulation. While previous studies have suggested that naive pluripotent stem cells possess enhanced resistance to various cellular stresses compared to their primed counterparts [
40
, 
57
], the molecular mechanisms underlying this protective phenotype remain largely elusive. Therefore, our data suggest that the molecular mechanisms promoting 2i naive-stress-resistant phenotypes are promoted by a constitutive stress-responsive state under basal conditions that provides a broad stress-protective phenotype across multiple stressors.
In addition to distinct stress-resistant phenotypes, our data further demonstrate that the different states maintain an intrinsic different transcriptional regulatory architecture over fundamental aspects of transcriptional dynamics. Naive cells predominantly regulate gene expression through the modulation of burst frequency, which is maintained upon stress. However, stress-responsive genes display higher transcriptional bursting even when only a small subset is upregulated (
n
 = 48). By contrast, primed cells exhibit globally lower transcription frequency and burst size under basal conditions but mount a strong transcriptional response to stress that increases burst size across the transcriptome, reflecting a more dramatic transcriptional reorganization, including higher-frequency upregulated genes. This suggests that stress-responsive genes might require an increase in burst size for upregulation regardless of the cell state. Although we did not directly test JNK inhibition on burst parameters, our findings are consistent with a model in which JNK’s distinct downstream effects could influence distinct kinetic parameters. For example, sustained JNK-AP1 occupancy at enhancers in naive cells may support constitutively accessible chromatin, favoring frequent but modest firing (burst frequency), whereas stress-induced JNK activation in primed cells may instead drive de novo coactivator and Pol II recruitment, favoring burst size [
58
,
59
,
60
]. In addition, the mechanistic difference between cell states parallels recent findings showing that pluripotent state transitions are characterized by distinct transcription kinetics, with naive states exhibiting globally reduced RNA synthesis rates and polymerase elongation velocities compared to primed conditions [
61
]. These transcriptional differences may explain how primed cells undergo more pronounced transcriptional reprogramming. Unlike ERK signaling, which drives differentiation, JNK signaling has an emerging role in pluripotent cell biology [
56
, 
62
, 
63
]. Here, we identified JNK activity as a critical regulator of stress resistance in 2i-naive pluripotent cells, and live KTR imaging in mouse embryos shows that JNK activity is dynamically engaged during the morula-to-early blastocyst window and declines toward the late blastocyst, mirroring the temporal expression of the basal stress-like signature in vivo. These in vivo observations remain correlative, and direct evidence that JNK drives this signature, or that it is functionally required for blastocyst survival under stress, will require targeted in vivo perturbation. Given the dynamic environmental and mechanical stresses encountered during implantation, this stress-resistance program may nevertheless represent a fundamental evolutionary adaptation that ensures embryonic survival during early development.

Mouse endoderm analysis in vivo
Gene expression analysis was performed on single-cell RNA-seq data from the mouse endoderm dataset from Nowotschin et al., 2019 [
43

]. Cells across all three time points were extracted from the complete dataset, resulting in a subset that was normalized using log-transformation. The data underwent quality control filtering and downstream analysis, including identification of highly variable features (
n 
= 2000), principal component analysis, and unsupervised clustering (resolution=0.5). Gene signature enrichment was quantified using UCell module scoring on curated gene signatures corresponding to stress-primed genes. The 322-gene signature was filtered to retain only those genes present in both the control 2i and NaCl SL datasets, yielding 144 overlapping genes for further analysis. Finally, a subset of the original object was created to generate a new object with an equal distribution of cell numbers across all timepoints. This was achieved by randomly subset from the original object to the point with the lowest number of cells (
n
 = 232). Visualization across developmental stages was conducted using ggplot2 [

That moment. That question. “What are you thankful for this year?” You are at the Thanksgiving Day table. Maybe it’s been a good year. Solid joys. Good wins. Joyful news. Then, too, maybe 2026 has not been the best year. The cute turkey photo accompanying this column isn’t enough for a smile to meet your face.
It’s in those “not best years” that the question of what we are thankful for holds some hurt or carries a sting. There may come a pause as you ask yourself, “What I am thankful for?” or you answer something vague with little investment or authenticity in your answer.
I am writing this column on November 12th as my time leading into Thanksgiving will be filled. I am early because I want to give this—I want to give you—time, reflection, honesty, and a way to be thankful that is genuine, heartfelt, and real.
My dad died suddenly and unexpectedly on November 12, 1999. He was the age I am now. We shared the same last name, of course. We also shared the first name, William, although he was called Butch.
My dad was butch. By this, I mean he was rugged and resilient with strength inside and out. With a work ethic I have not seen again, he pushed his way through the challenges any small business owner experiences. You name it. Lean years. Labor costs. Labor shortages. Demanding clients. External circumstances beyond his control. And never enough time in each day.
My dad was a full-time Christmas tree farmer. November 12th was the first day of the harvest season. He had spent the morning cutting Fraser Fir on a steep bank. (No worries on that cut date anyone, given enough water and the right space in your home, a Fraser will do very well through Christmas day and beyond.)
Beside his iconic Ford 150 and his trusted chainsaw, he died on the logging road right beside the tree field. It was his heart. A gash on his forehead from falling never bled.
I will go out to visit the site where he died after I finish this writing. I will carry memories, sorrow, purpose, love, direction, and what is best of all—hope.
Hope was a word that surfaced that November. My immediate and extended family was so hurt, so lost. The business had to go on, however. We couldn’t stop to grieve.
You can imagine how awful Thanksgiving Day was that year. The business could not pause. The holiday, as always, was the eye of the storm: a brief blip of peace in an otherwise demanding schedule.
I don’t know about you all, but we were (and are) turkey eating people on Thanksgiving. In 1999, Thanksgiving fell on the 25th, thirteen days after dad had died. There was a botch in the meal plan. An understandable miscommunication led into the day. Roast beef was served.
Our time to give thanks was shared around the table, though it was understandably dimmed. I share this because thanks may be dimmed for you this year. Giving thanks may not seem as profound for you as it could be or as it once was.
This is where the hope I mentioned comes not with a pop of celebration, but with a pang a broken heart feels too well.
Yet here’s the truth. For those in Christ, there is good news. There is always good news. Our hope is not found in anything but Christ Jesus. Our hope is not found in the now. Our hope is found in ourselves.
No, our hope is only found in the One who appeared as the Rescuer after the fall of humankind in Genesis 3 where sin entered all of us through Adam and Eve. We cannot get ourselves out of our sinful state. Only Jesus can, and only Jesus did when He died on the cross for our sins.
And even death did not keep Jesus down. He rose from a stone-closed grave three days later to give you eternal life not because you deserve it but because He gives it when You know who He is.
I am not writing in a Christian magazine. This column comes through a secular paper. That I can write what I have about sin, Jesus, death, and resurrection is more than a gift here. I call this a miracle.
There is a reason you are reading this. There is a God, specifically our God who carries a message to you through these words. Here comes that moment. That question. "What are you thankful for this year?"
Answer this way. "God. I am thankful for God this year." You may say more. Then, too, this may be enough for this year. Why I come down strongly here is because of the incredibly strong, resilient, never-stopping, always-reaching love God has for you.
Your thanks may not abound this Thanksgiving, but thanks is still there this Thanksgiving because God is with you always. Always. And this? This is far more than enough.
Rev. Dr. Hagenbuch is the pastor of the Harford First Congregational-UCC Church.

EDITORIAL: Keep close tabs on Hawaii Builds

Published 12:02 am Friday, November 27, 2026

By Star-Advertiser

Governments, both here and across the country, seem to be pulling out all the stops to get more housing built quicker, with fewer bureaucratic stops on the way.

In Hawaii, some safeguards are in place to avoid too many speed bumps, but it’s still imperative to make sure the overall housing marketplace is healthier. That’s the intent and hope behind a new five-year pilot program called “Hawaii Builds.”

Concern about the lack of affordable housing nationwide has driven legislation in the U.S. Senate, including the just-passed Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act that now goes to the House. Though not a standalone housing bill, its federal permitting reforms are aimed at making such projects easier to complete, as well as various infrastructure initiatives. Additionally, a specific bipartisan housing bill was enacted in July to boost the housing supply.

Locally, the state Department of Hawaiian Home Lands has deployed factory-built units to help whittle its deficit of homes for beneficiaries at a lower cost — subdivisions in Maui, Waianae and Kapolei among the current examples.

But the most potentially useful effort to address the housing shortfall was set in motion by lawmakers in the spring, when they passed Senate Bill 2544 to fast-track the development of homes. Signed into law as Act 216, the Hawaii Builds pilot will be a five-year test of the concept, tapping the Dwelling Use Revolving Fund (DURF), a state housing financing resource, to fund construction on an accelerated timeline.

That’s accomplished by dispensing with the normal review protocols. Usually undertaken by the county councils, those duties are turned over to the “expedited review team” comprising state and county officials with permitting roles.

This seems promising, especially when some pre-planning already has been invested in the project.

The first developer in line is Kobayashi Group, which the state Hawaii Housing Finance and Development Corp. (HHFDC) tapped as best qualified for a Kapolei project. At least one Hawaii Builds development will be planned for each county.

The Kapolei project will sit on 2.8 acres zoned for agricultural use, but it’s surrounded by urban land uses and was noted as part of the Villages of Kapolei community master plan. That ag zoning is why bypassing the lengthy rezoning process presents some challenges in oversight, but manageable ones.

The particulars on the project, to be located on a strip of state land between the Ka Makana Ali‘i and Kapolei Middle School, are still being evaluated. Among the build-out options: between 60 and 120 apartments, to be operated as rentals, for-sale units or a rent-to-own project.

The law specifies that more than half the homes must be affordable at or below 140% of the area median income (AMI). That higher income cap is a goal that developers would see as within easier reach than the more-traditional “affordable” housing target: households closer to 80-100% of AMI, or lower.

The problem, of course, is that state financing resources are limited and finite, and Hawaii Builds could eat up a large chunk of the revolving fund — funds that typically go for low-interest loans to developers, for lower-income households. Under Act 216, a maximum of $20 million for each of the five years could be used for Hawaii Builds; that $100 million total represents nearly a third of roughly $379 million in DURF’s estimated cash and program resources, as of July 31.

Prospects for success will depend on the specifics of these projects that remain unclear. When legislators do review the annual report for Hawaii Builds, they must ensure that fulfilling housing needs higher on the household income scale will help by freeing up homes for low-income groups as well.

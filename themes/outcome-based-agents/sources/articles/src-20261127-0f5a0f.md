Brussels’ New Kanal Centre Pompidou Is Adaptive Reuse at Its Grandest

The massive art museum is a boon for circular architecture but has also exposed the cracks in Belgium’s long-disjointed cultural identity.

Words: 
Adrian Madlener

There’s lots being said about the embodied weight of industrial buildings—especially nefarious, water-guzzling 
data centers 
depriving communities of basic utilities and emitting inordinate amounts of CO2. Continuing to build such massive monoliths no longer seems plausible, but the question of what we do with the monolithic structures already in place, held over from previous waves of accelerated development, is still perplexing for many. In our increasingly precarious times, there’s an undeniable necessity for innovative adaptive reuse and a significant reduction in heavy resource extraction for new construction.   

For a few decades now, the obvious answer has been to convert these sturdy, raw, and undeniably fetishized complexes into cultural venues; institutionalizing what were formerly squatted hubs for alternative living and creative expression with the purported aim, ironically, of better supporting and sustaining that output. There’s 
MASS MoCA
 in western Massachusetts, Norman Foster’s ‘greening-of-iron’ transformation of the Zollverein Coal Mine in Germany’s Ruhrgebiet region, and Herzog & de Meuron’s mid-1990s conversion of the Bankside Power Station in central London into the 
Tate Modern
.

The Kanal’s glass lantern sits at the intersection of Brussels’ Square Sainctelette and Place de l’Yser. © Koen van Damme

This week, there’s the long-anticipated opening of the 
Kanal Centre Pompidou
 modern and contemporary art museum smack-dab in the heart of Brussels, the Belgian and de facto E.U. capital, often written-off for its dreary appearance and lack of historical preservation. This is where the term Brusselization was coined: the 
laissez-faire
 influx of modern high-rises and intrusive transportation infrastructure placed over beacons of Neoclassicism and Art Nouveau (Brussels is where the movement first took root). It’s hard to deny that it’s now one of the best rail-connected cities in the world, but at what cost? Couldn’t some of this historic architecture have been better integrated, put into adaptive reuse? Among the few late 19th- and early 20th-century structures that survived this “progressive policy” is a “cathedral for cars”—the 1934 Citroën showroom and garage. With its 68-foot-tall curved glass wall, the Art Deco edifice has struck an imposing figure along the edge of the city center for almost a century now.  

Kanal is housed in car tycoon André Citroën’s 1934 “cathedral for cars” designed by Alexis Dumont, Marcel Van Goethem and Maurice-Jacques Ravazé as a massive modernist complex with a 69-foot-high showroom.

It’s in this long disused industrial site that the new multipronged venue has taken root; comprising copious amounts of galleries, performance and film screening auditoriums and free-access spaces; an indoor playground, restaurant, rooftop bar, an in-house bakery; a library and research center. The year-round exhibition program here will feature temporary shows on prominent and emerging artists; alongside a permanent display of 350 works from the early 1900s through to the present. 

Carrying out every aspect of the project; 
Atelier Kanal
, an architectural consortium formed by local firm 
noAarchitecten
, Zurich-based 
EM2N Architekten
, and London’s 
Sergison Bates Architects 
devised the overall scheme based on extensive ethnographic research. “Through the testbed Kanal Brut exhibition and Maison du Projet public forum, we engaged the community in a conversation,” the consortium told 
METROPOLIS
, speaking as a single entity, “and from that, fine-tuned ideas on both a curatorial and technical level.” 

The central nave of the building is 62 feet high and runs for 590 feet. Atelier Kanal preserved 2950 feet of handrails in their original condition throughout the building. © Koen van Damme

Still, the almost decade-long realization of the massive 430,000-square-foot “living city” platform—outpacing Tate Modern and coming second to The Louvre as Europe’s largest collecting and exhibiting institution—has been marked by several controversies. The government-backed project has been fraught with questions of financial stability (Brussels’ current debt stands at approximately €15.6 billion). Many have also argued that a Belgian institution allying itself with a French one is a form of linguistic bias. Even though Flemings (Dutch speakers) make up a larger portion of the national population, elites historically chose French as their lingua franca. The capital is officially bilingual but has never really achieved that balance. 

Technically, Centre Pompidou “is providing the core artistic content, global branding, and curatorial expertise under a structural partnership agreement that runs until 2031,” as officially stated on its website; inferring that Paris has some kind of cultural authority over Brussels and that it’s lending its clout to a city with none.  

The Turner-prize winning architecture and arts collective Assemble was commissioned by Kanal to create the Playground, a 7,500 square-foot playscape on the first floor of the museum. © Kristien Daem

Given its proximity to major, far more expensive conurbations like the Dutch Randstad (Amsterdam, The Hague, Rotterdam), Paris, and London, Brussels has long been a hotbed for creative ideation, especially when it comes to the avant-garde, but this energy gets overshadowed by better-marketing. There’s a sense of quiet self-deprecation here and in turn, an openness that has spawned a spirit of scrappy, cross-disciplinary experimentation; a willingness to at least try and break with well-worn Catholic, bourgeois, and colonialist conventions. Many innovative talents and ideas have emerged in this downcast city. If so closely tied to the more boisterous and self-assured expression of 
La Ville-Lumière,
 can Kanal truly hold true to Brussels’ cultural complexity? That all remains to be seen.  

What’s undeniably apparent is that this project is a tour-de-force of site-responsive adaptive reuse and nuanced preservationism. “Our stated ambition has been to trust the building as we found it; to embrace its industrial character and its many layers of use,” Atelier Kanal said. “We wanted to limit demolition and waste production, rethink the standards of comfort related to public interiors and uses and limit any new interventions.” 

The exhibition “A Truly Immense Journey, 100 Years of Masterpieces Centre Pompidou Collection.” © Dries Van den Brande

The energy strategy, developed with Buro Happold and Bureau Greisch, establishes three distinct thermal zones. A vast, 43,000-square-foot solar array caps the industrial roof, while the converted factory’s 1,394-foot-long facade has been meticulously restored and fitted with vacuum glazing and operable windows to facilitate natural ventilation.

Accounting for 54 percent of the total footprint, the unprogrammed public spaces act as an extension of the city itself, offering seasonal comfort, natural light, and fresh air. Two monolithic exhibition volumes—comprising 21 percent of the layout—were engineered to deliver high-performance climate control for the displayed art. Resourcefully bridging these extremes, intermediate glazed gallery spaces serve as buffers to maximize overall building efficiency. This is adaptive reuse and historic preservation at its best.

Spread across five levels, the museum incorporates column-free galleries with varied ceiling heights to accommodate diverse curatorial configurations. A 23-foot-high basement gallery can be accessed directly from the exterior and operated independently. While some of the exhibition halls connect directly to adjoining workshop spaces, others are self-contained, benefiting from natural light and views of the adjacent Brussels-Charleroi Canal, the institution’s namesake. A 59-foot-wide cross-axis corridor cuts through the ground level almost as a newly introduced street, puncturing the monolithic structure with unclaimed niches intended for local artistic interventions on either side. On an even smaller but no less impactful scale of intervention, flexible Kanal System furniture—developed by the consortium using the OpenStructures modular framework—allows visitors to arrange public seating as they see fit.

Oriented toward the city center, the iconic “cathedral of cars” showroom serves as Kanal’s primary front and “a new stage for Brussels.” Atelier Kanal has restored the iconic Art Deco edifice to function as a grand public square under cover, (it rains a lot here) with large ground-floor facade sections opening onto the newly refurbished Sainctelette Square engineered to host installations, performances, and concerts. Upper floors house a restaurant and a freely accessible rooftop bar terrace. 

“We foresee it as a meeting place for the diverse neighborhood and wider city,” Atelier Kanal said, affirming the evolutionary nature of such an undertaking. “We have approached KANAL as a movement, never finished and continually in the making. The ‘museum’ no longer belongs only to curated art but becomes part of the urban fabric. In this sense, it departs from the traditional institutional format and instead offers a place of civic gathering responding to an increasing absence of shared spaces in our cities today.” Only time will tell if the consortium’s clients, Kanal Foundation and Centre Pompidou, uphold this mission.

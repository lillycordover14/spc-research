Adapting Together: The Fourth Requirement of Adaptive Capacity&nbsp;&nbsp;SLDinfo.com

[page text]
By Robbin Laird

When I published “Between Two Ages, Again” on November 18, Deborah Westphal left a comment that has stayed with me. Deborah worked alongside Alvin and Heidi Toffler for more than two decades and later led Toffler Associates. She comes to the problem of institutions and change from the other side of a shared moment: Alvin Toffler’s 
Future Shock
 appeared in 1970, the same year as Brzezinski’s 
Between Two Ages
. Both books asked what happens when change outruns the institutions meant to absorb it.

Her question went straight to the weakest joint in my argument. I had described adaptive capacity as resting on three pillars: intellectual flexibility, institutional resilience, and social cohesion. Are those pillars, she asked, necessarily mutually reinforcing?

Democracies draw strength from diversity of thought, individual initiative, and decentralized decision-making. But every time one institution adapts, it changes the conditions to which other institutions must adapt. If interdependent institutions change at different rates and in different directions, their individual successes could undermine their collective ability to function. In her words: can individually adaptive institutions collectively produce a maladaptive system?

My answer is that they can, and that the pillars are not necessarily mutually reinforcing. The word “pillars” implied more harmony than the evidence supports. This article takes her question seriously, because it exposes where chaos management is most vulnerable and where the work has to go next.

Where She Is Right: The Pillars Pull Against Each Other

Each pillar rewards a different behavior, and the behaviors collide.

Intellectual flexibility
 rewards variance. It wants many experiments, open dissent, and tolerance for being wrong in public. It generates differences by design.

Institutional resilience
 rewards the opposite. It wants redundancy, standard procedures, trained routines, stockpiles, and continuity. A military service that rewrites its doctrine every quarter is not resilient. It is unstable.

Social cohesion
 rewards shared narrative and trust. That can protect a democracy under stress. It can also harden into conformity, which starves the flexibility the first pillar needs. Pluralism, in turn, strains cohesion.

The tensions are familiar to anyone who has worked inside an alliance or a service. A commander is told to challenge assumptions yet needs a force that performs predictably under fire. Allied partners are asked to pursue national solutions that fit their own circumstances, yet also to accept common positions that make the coalition function. Each demand is legitimate. Together they pull in different directions.

So the pillars are better understood as three requirements to be balanced continuously than as three supports that automatically bear weight together. When I wrote that adaptive capacity is 
built on
 them, I implied a stability that real institutions do not enjoy. Mutual reinforcement is not a property the pillars have. It is something leaders and system designers must achieve, and can fail to achieve.

The Mechanism: Adaptation Changes the Terrain Others Adapt To

The deeper point in Deborah’s question concerns the environment. For any institution, the other institutions it depends on are part of its environment. A ministry’s acquisition rules are the terrain on which a service adapts. A service’s doctrine is the terrain on which industry plans its production lines. When one of them changes, it alters the ground under all the others.

Biologists call this coevolution, and the “Red Queen” image from evolutionary theory captures its cost: every party must keep running just to stay in place, because the others are running too. Students of complex systems describe a landscape that shifts whenever any participant moves. Charles Perrow’s work on technological accidents adds a further warning. In tightly coupled systems, a change in one part propagates quickly to others before anyone can intervene.

Put those ideas together and Deborah’s worry follows directly. An institution can adapt well by its own standards, faster, smarter, and closer to its mission, while degrading the environment of its partners. This is the old fallacy of composition: what is rational for each part can be harmful for the whole. Three conditions make it worse:

Different tempos.
 Operational units can adapt in weeks. Budget cycles, certification regimes, and legislatures take years. The fast adapter outruns the interfaces that tie it to everyone else.

Different directions.
 Local adaptation answers local pressures. Nothing guarantees that thousands of locally sensible changes point the same way.

Tight coupling.
 The more a force depends on shared data, shared munitions, shared logistics, and shared standards, the more one party’s change becomes everyone’s problem.

The kill web is a case in point. By design it is built from many nodes, sensors, shooters, and decision-makers, that adapt at the edge. Its whole value comes from their connection. If every node adapts without regard to the web, the web degrades even as each node improves.

Evidence from the Field: Two Ways Success Becomes Fragmentation

Two cases show the pattern at different scales.

Ukraine’s battlefield software.
 Ukraine’s wartime innovation is the clearest example of democratic adaptability under pressure: brigades, volunteer groups, ministries, and companies built tools in weeks. The result, according to a 2025 survey of the landscape, was a “software zoo” of overlapping systems. A Ministry of Defence survey of around 10,000 service members found three in heaviest use (Delta, Virazh-Tablet, and Kropyva), and the same report found no clearly defined standard for how specialized military software should be built, so soldiers often ran several systems on different devices at once. Local success had created a collective problem.

The response was not less adaptation. It was a common layer. Delta, which grew out of work by the volunteer group Aerorozvidka and was later developed by the Ministry of Defence’s innovation centre, spread across the force and, as the Boston Globe reported in August 2026, became Ukraine’s unified battlefield management system. The contrast with the United States is instructive. Army Secretary Daniel Driscoll told the Senate in May that Delta integrates every drone, sensor, and shooter into one network, and he said of the U.S. Army, “Ours does not.” An adaptive system can achieve coherence when someone governs the seams.

European defense industry.
 Here the failure is slower. Over decades, each government made choices that were rational for its own industry, sovereignty, and budget. In aggregate, the commonly cited figures describe a continent fielding 17 types of main battle tank against one in the United States, and 178 weapon systems against 30 (counts of systems in service in 2016). The cost became visible once Ukraine’s Western donors, European governments among them, sent so many models that a RUSI analyst counted 14 howitzer types in its force, where most armies field two or three. Each type brings its own ammunition, spares, and training burden.

A caution belongs here. The European Union Institute for Security Studies argues that these numbers overstate current fragmentation, because they count what accumulated in inventories over decades, not what is in production today. On that measure the picture is mixed: a single EU main battle tank line (Leopard 2) since 2008, against the U.S. M1 Abrams line, but a clearly more fragmented picture in naval shipbuilding. The honest reading is that Europe’s problem is real but is partly historical residue, and partly a failure of coordination that continues.

Neither case involves a failed pillar. Ukraine’s flexibility was extraordinary. European governments exercised institutional resilience and national cohesion, each in its own frame. The failure appeared at the seams between adaptive parts.

Brzezinski’s Paradox Reappears Inside the Institutions

In my first article I quoted Brzezinski’s formulation that humanity was becoming “simultaneously more unified and more fragmented.” I applied it to the international system. Deborah’s question shows that it also operates inside our own institutions.

The same technologies that connect institutions, shared networks, common data, and global supply chains, also allow each of them to adapt independently and quickly. Connection and fragmentation arrive together. The global city has its institutional counterpart: a web of interdependent organizations that change at different rates, each one a source of stability for the others and a source of disturbance to them.

This answers her second question directly. Yes, the characteristics that make democracies adaptive can also become sources of fragmentation. Diversity of thought, individual initiative, and decentralized decision-making are one capacity with two faces. The variance that finds the new solution is the same variance that produces fourteen howitzer types and a zoo of incompatible software.

The Soviet case, which I have studied for decades, shows the opposite failure. The Soviet system achieved a kind of coherence by suppressing variance, and paid for it in innovation. Democracies hold the reverse position: they generate adaptation and cannot assume coherence. I would not claim that authoritarian systems escape coordination problems of their own. But the lesson for democracies is plain. The remedy for fragmentation cannot be to suppress the variance that gives us our advantage. It has to be to govern the connections between the parts.

From Three Pillars to a Design Problem

Deborah’s question changes my framework. Adaptive capacity cannot be left as three separate virtues that we hope will add up. It needs a fourth element that I now think belongs beside the other three: 
coordination
, meaning the deliberate governance of how adaptive parts fit together. The three pillars describe what each institution must be. Coordination describes what the system of institutions must do. Five practices follow.

Shared intent, not shared blueprint.
 Brzezinski distinguished vision from blueprint, and the distinction is also the military’s answer to this problem. Commander’s intent lets subordinates act on their own initiative while pulling in one direction. A system of adaptive institutions needs the equivalent: purposes clear enough to align thousands of local decisions, and loose enough to leave them room. Intent that is too vague aligns nothing. Intent that is too detailed becomes a blueprint and forfeits the flexibility it was meant to protect.

Governed seams.
 Be loose inside, strict at the interfaces. Institutions should be free to change how they work, provided they honor the standards at the points where others connect to them: data formats, protocols, munitions, certification. Ukraine’s software zoo is what happens when the interface is left ungoverned.

Tempo matching.
 The usual instinct is to slow the fast adapters. The better answer is usually to speed up the slow parts of the system, such as acquisition, certification, and legislative approval, and to oblige fast adapters to give notice when a change affects others, the way software teams announce that an interface is about to change.

Some assets are best held in common: trusted data, shared test environments, shared lessons learned. These are the shared infrastructure of the allied kill web, and they are what allows partners to change independently without drifting apart.

A system-level owner.
 Someone must be responsible for how the whole performs, not only the parts. Alliance headquarters, joint staffs, and standards bodies play this role when they work. They need real authority to arbitrate, and enough legitimacy to use it. That connects the fourth element back to social cohesion.

There is a test that follows from all of this. We exercise whether a unit can adapt far more often than we test whether the 
system
 can absorb the adaptation of its parts. Exercises, red teams, and after-action reviews should start asking that question directly.

What I Would Not Claim

As in the first article, restraint is part of the argument.

First, coordination is not a cure, and it has costs of its own. A system-level owner can become the rigid center that smothers the edge, which would sacrifice the intellectual flexibility the whole exercise is meant to protect. A single common platform is also a single point of failure and a single target. Ukraine’s consolidation around one system solved an interoperability problem and may have created a concentration risk. Whether the trade was worth making is a judgment I cannot settle from outside.

Second, the two cases are illustrations, not a test of a theory. Ukraine adapted under existential threat, which supplies a shared purpose that peacetime democracies do not enjoy. The pressure that pushed Ukrainian units toward a common system may not exist where the threat is distant and the incentives to go one’s own way are strong. The European figures are also contested, as the caution above makes clear.

Third, some instability is not a failure to be eliminated. A system of coevolving institutions will always produce friction at the seams. The realistic goal is friction that is visible, bounded, and corrected faster than it accumulates. That is consistent with what I call chaos management, which treats turbulence as the operating condition and not as an exception.

Finally, I do not claim to have answered Deborah’s question in full. I have tried to say why the answer is yes, and what follows from that. The researchers who study coevolution and institutional change will have more to say, and so will those who have spent careers applying the Tofflers’ ideas inside real organizations.

The Question That Remains

Brzezinski and Toffler, writing in the same year, asked versions of one question: what happens when change outruns the institutions that must absorb it? My first article argued that democracies hold an advantage in answering it, because open systems can adapt where closed ones cannot. Deborah’s question shows what that argument leaves out. Having the capacity to adapt is not enough. Democracies need the capacity to adapt 
together
.

The revised claim is this. Adaptive capacity has four requirements, not three: intellectual flexibility, institutional resilience, social cohesion, and the coordination that lets them operate as a system. The first three can pull against one another. The fourth is what keeps their tensions productive and prevents individual success from becoming collective failure.

Individually adaptive institutions can collectively produce a maladaptive system. Whether they do depends on whether we design for the seams as carefully as we design for the parts.

I am grateful to Deborah for the question. It has made the argument better, and I expect it will shape how I write the next chapters of the Age of Chaos series.

And to be clear, her inputs have led me to revise my 
Mastering Chaos

On 27 November 2026 the European Medicines Agency (EMA) published results from a DARWIN EU drug utilisation study showing that the use of GLP-1 medicines in Europe has moved towards obesity treatment, based on 
more than 1.6 million people who started treatment between 2015 and 2025
 in 12 healthcare databases across ten countries. The study matters to pharmacoepidemiology groups, regulators and research offices that work with real-world data.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Diana Nieves Castro, MD. This post reports a regulatory study and gives no medical advice.

What happened

EMA’s news item of 27 November 2026 reports a study titled “Determinants for use of GLP-1 receptor agonists”, run through DARWIN EU, the agency’s network for real-world evidence. It identified more than 1.6 million people who started a GLP-1 medicine in the participating databases. From 2022, obesity became the most commonly recorded indication for starting treatment in several of the study populations, and in most data sources fewer than 14% of new users had no recorded diagnosis of type 2 diabetes or obesity.

EMA also reports that dosing patterns and switching between GLP-1 medicines differed across countries and over time, and that in some countries prescribing fluctuated in step with the shortages EMA has reported. Semaglutide-containing medicines were the most used.

Key details of the study

Item

What EMA reports

Data

12 healthcare databases in ten European countries

Period

People starting treatment between 2015 and 2025

Size

More than 1.6 million people starting a GLP-1 medicine

Age

Median age at start ranged from 52 to 63 years across data sources

Sex

More than half of new users were women in nine of the 12 databases

Medicines

Dulaglutide, exenatide and lixisenatide (type 2 diabetes); liraglutide, semaglutide and tirzepatide (type 2 diabetes and weight management)

EMA notes that the databases capture prescriptions recorded in the participating sources and may not reflect all GLP-1 use, because some healthcare settings are not covered. The agency’s news item also records that the marketing authorisation for Lyxumia (lixisenatide) was withdrawn on 18 December 2025 and those for Byetta and Bydureon (exenatide) on 1 January 2026.

The study’s record in the EMA-hosted 
HMA-EMA catalogue of real-world data sources and studies
 gives its registration number as EUPAS1000000828, names Marta Pineda Moncusi of the Department of Medical Informatics, Erasmus Medical Center, as lead investigator, and states that EMA funded it. The record lists Belgium, Croatia, Denmark, Finland, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden and the United Kingdom as study countries. Its stated aims include describing who is prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonists, how patients switch between substances, brands and other antidiabetic medicines over a ten-year period, how trends compare with other diabetes and weight-management medicines, and patterns of off-label use.

Background and context

GLP-1 receptor agonists have been in shortage in EU Member States since 2022. The catalogue record gives rising demand and manufacturing capacity limits as causes and names “excessive off-label use for cosmetic weight loss” as a concern, which is the question this study was funded to help answer. EMA’s 
GLP-1 medicines overview
 says that in 2024 its Executive Steering Group on Shortages and Safety of Medicinal Products issued recommendations, on behalf of EMA and the Heads of Medicines Agencies, for Ozempic, Saxenda, Trulicity and Victoza, citing a sudden rise in demand and limited manufacturing capacity. The same page lists 10 GLP-1 medicines approved in the EU (four semaglutide, four liraglutide, one dulaglutide and one tirzepatide product) and says that in 2025 EMA and the Heads of Medicines Agencies warned about illegal GLP-1 products sold online.

An earlier DARWIN EU drug utilisation study of GLP-1 medicines, published in 2024, covered five European countries from 2014 to 2024, according to the same EMA page. The November 2026 study is broader in both countries and databases.

DARWIN EU itself was set up in 2022. 
EMA describes it
 as a network in which the Erasmus University Medical Center Rotterdam runs the coordination centre, about 40 data partners had been onboarded by 2026, about 110 studies had been delivered since 2022, and partners standardise their data to the OMOP common data model before analysis. EMA is the main user and requests studies to support scientific evaluations and regulatory decisions. The 
DARWIN EU coordination centre
 says it provides evidence on the use, safety and effectiveness of medicines from real-world healthcare databases across the EU, and that EMA selected Erasmus MC as provider in February 2022 after a call for tender.

What it means for researchers, universities and research offices

For pharmacoepidemiology and health-data groups, the study is a worked example of how a regulator uses a federated network: a standard protocol run by a coordination centre against locally held data, with results reported across databases rather than pooled patient records. Groups that hold or analyse health data can read the study report and study documents in the catalogue to see how the analysis was specified. Background on the method is in our guides to 
the OMOP common data model
, 
observational studies
 and the 
EMA-HMA real-world data quality framework
, and in our dictionary entry for 
real-world evidence
.

For universities and research offices, three points follow from EMA’s own description. First, a university hospital or registry that joins DARWIN EU as a data partner takes on obligations to map its data to a common model and take part in regulator-requested studies. Second, because prescribing in some countries moved with shortages, researchers who use dispensing or prescribing data from 2022 onwards should check whether supply disruption, not clinical practice alone, shaped the series. Third, the finding that more than half of new users were women in nine of 12 databases, with median start age between 52 and 63, gives investigators who plan obesity-treatment cohorts real-world background for recruitment and comparator design, though the databases are not a census of all use.

Teams planning follow-on work, such as safety signal checks in 
pharmacovigilance
, should keep EMA’s coverage caveat in view: the databases record prescriptions in participating sources, not all GLP-1 use.

What happens next

EMA has published the full study report in the catalogue, together with the study documents, and says DARWIN EU studies are listed in the catalogue of real-world data studies. EMA’s GLP-1 page says the agency monitors GLP-1 safety and takes regulatory action when it identifies new risks. The agency’s news item does not announce a regulatory decision based on this study.

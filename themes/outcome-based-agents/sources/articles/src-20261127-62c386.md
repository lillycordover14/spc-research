Quick Read

A charitable remainder trust can sell $5 million of low-basis stock tax-free at the trust level, then reinvest every dollar of the proceeds.

The tax isn't erased: each payout to the donor carries out ordinary income first, then capital gain, so she pays gradually.

Prearranged sales, debt-financed assets and the new 2026 deduction floor can undo the benefit, and the remainder ultimately goes to charity.

A founder holding $4.8 million of unrealized gain in one stock has a problem a diversification plan can’t solve on its own. Once she sells, the gain is taxed. Section 664 of the Internal Revenue Code gives her a different option. If the stock goes into a 
charitable remainder trust
 (CRT) before any sale, the trust can sell the whole position, reinvest every dollar and pay no tax at the trust level. The tax still applies, though. Section 664 also controls when and how she pays it.

What Section 664 Does

A charitable remainder annuity trust or unitrust “shall, for any taxable year, not be subject to any tax imposed by this subtitle”
[
1
]
 under 26 U.S.C. § 664(c)(1). When the trust sells appreciated stock, the gain is realized inside an entity that is exempt from income tax.

The gain stays on the books, however. Under 26 U.S.C. § 664(b), each payment to the income beneficiary is treated as coming first
[
1
]
 from ordinary income, second from capital gain, then from other income, and last from corpus. Treasury’s regulation goes further. It puts gains to rate classes and pays out the highest-taxed class first, and it applies the tax rates “in the year in which the distribution is required to be made”
[
2
]
 (26 CFR 1.664-1(d)(1)(ii)). Any gain not yet distributed carries forward under 26 CFR 1.664-1(d)(1)(v).

Who Qualifies and What the Trust Must Look Like

A unitrust has to pay a fixed percentage that is “not less than 5 percent nor more than 50 percent”
[
1
]
 of its assets, valued every year. Payments run for life or for a term “not in excess of 20 years”
[
1
]
 (§ 664(d)(2)(A)). An annuity trust instead pays a fixed sum based on the starting value (§ 664(d)(1)(A)). In both versions, the present value of the charity’s remainder must equal at least 10%
[
1
]
 of the property contributed (§ 664(d)(1)(D) and (d)(2)(D)). Young beneficiaries and high payout rates can fail that test.

The donor’s income tax deduction covers only the remainder interest. Section 170(f)(2)(A) allows a deduction for a remainder interest in trust only when the trust is a CRAT, a CRUT or a pooled income fund (26 U.S.C. § 170(f)(2)(A)
[
3
]
).

Worked Example: $5 Million of Stock With a $200,000 Basis

A hypothetical executive transfers $5 million of long-held stock with a $200,000 basis to a 5% lifetime unitrust. The trust then sells the position.

Gain realized by the trust:
 $4,800,000. Trust-level tax: $0 under § 664(c)(1).

Comparison sale outside the trust:
 taxed at the top 2026 long-term rate of 20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax, the federal bill would be roughly $1,142,400. That leaves about $3,857,600 to reinvest. (This simplification ignores the lower brackets below the 2026 single-filer 20% threshold of $545,500.)

First-year payout:
 $250,000, calculated on the full $5 million.

Taxation of that payout:
 any dividend or interest income the trust earned comes out first as ordinary income. The rest is long-term capital gain drawn from the $4.8 million pool. Gain keeps flowing out with each payment until the pool runs dry.

So the trust paid nothing, and the executive pays tax gradually, at whatever rates apply in each payout year. The benefit is deferral plus full reinvestment of the pre-tax principal. In exchange, the remainder goes to charity instead of back to the family.

Four Traps That Undo the Benefit

Prearranged sale.
 If the donor is already legally bound to sell before the transfer, the IRS can treat the gain as the donor’s own under the assignment-of-income doctrine.

Debt and active business income.
 A CRT with unrelated business taxable income owes an excise tax “equal to the amount of such unrelated business taxable income”
[
1
]
 under § 664(c)(2)(A). Mortgaged real estate and operating partnership interests can trigger this tax.

Deduction limits.
 A remainder gift of capital gain property through a trust generally falls under the cap for contributions not covered by § 170(b)(1)(A): the lesser of 20%
[
3
]
 of the contribution base or a 30% residual (§ 170(b)(1)(D)(i)). Any excess carries forward 5
[
3
]
 years.

New 2026 floor.
 For tax years beginning after December 31, 2025
[
4
]
, charitable deductions count only above 0.5%
[
3
]
 of the contribution base (§ 170(b)(1)(I)). Gifts made in 2025 and earlier tax years aren’t subject to it.

How a CRT Interacts With the Rest of a Plan

Assets in a CRT are no longer held by the donor outright. That can matter at the 2026 federal estate tax basic exclusion of $15,000,000. When a spouse is the only noncharitable beneficiary, § 2056(b)(8) keeps the marital deduction available (26 U.S.C. § 2056(b)(8)(A)
[
5
]
). For smaller gifts, there is also a one-time IRA election that directs qualified charitable distributions to a split-interest entity. It is capped at $55,000 for 2026, per IRS Notice 2025-67. A CRT is one lever among several here; QCDs, donor-advised funds and appreciated-stock gifts each have their own tax rules, and we walked through all of them in a 
free guide to tax-smart charitable giving
.

Large estates still hold a lot of the asset class this tool targets. Estate tax returns filed in 2024 for estates of $50 million or more reported $39,363,942,000
[
6
]
 in publicly traded stock and $17,471,202,000 in closely held stock.

Decision Framework: Who a CRT Fits and Who It Doesn’t

A CRT tends to fit households that:

hold a low-basis, concentrated position they want to branch out

would value a lifetime or 20-year income stream more than the principal

already intend to leave significant assets to charity

It fits poorly when:

the family needs the principal back

the asset carries debt or generates active business income

a sale is already under binding contract

the beneficiaries are young enough that the 10% remainder test fails at the payout they want

the main goal is passing the asset to children

Before going further, a donor should model three things. First, how the § 664(b) tiers will spread the gain across future payout years. Second, how the 2026 deduction floor and percentage caps limit the deduction. Third, whether the trust’s income will be worth more than the remainder she gives up.

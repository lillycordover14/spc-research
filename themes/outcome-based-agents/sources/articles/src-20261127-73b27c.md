Biting through the crusty exterior of high-quality fresh sourdough bread is a ticket to contentment, followed by the reward of spongy goodness. That dual delight explains why many cultures have bread-related aphorisms. “All sorrows are less with bread,” noted Miguel de Cervantes.

On Wednesday, Dec. 2, Nicholas D’Alessandro, founder of Hearthstone Artisan Bakery, will offer a tour of maintaining healthy sourdough starter in home kitchens. The workshop will be held in the Greenfield Public Library’s Community Room. Don’t dillydally, dear readers: spots are going fast.

Why all the fuss? Why not just grab a loaf of whatever off the grocery shelf? Julia Child is often misidentified as coining the phrase, “How can a nation be called great if its bread tastes like Kleenex?” (It originated with journalist Joan Barthel in a 1966 New York Times Magazine article featuring Child’s television show, “The French Chef.”) But the point is well taken.

In light of the pandemic-era sourdough craze, D’Alessandro sets the record straight.

“The internet is full of anecdotal, overblown information,” he said. “Don’t get me wrong: bread is a beautiful food. But it’s not exactly a ‘health food.’ There are many factors, including the quality of the wheat. Was it grown ethically, organically and free of chemicals like glyphosate? Once milled, has the flour gone rancid? Sourdough can be rife with probiotics, but it must be done correctly.”

Unfortunately, there’s no industry standard. The term sourdough carries about as much weight as the words all-natural, homemade and traditional. 

 

 

High standards make D’Alessandro a regional hero in the worlds of both sourdough breads and wood-fired pizza. He maintains a staggering schedule to bring bread, pies and cookies to farmers markets in Greenfield and Northampton. And when he hauls his mobile wood-fired pizza enterprise to regional festivals — often with the help of 16-year-old son Sebastian — he often sells out before fans can line up for seconds.

D’Alessandro tends to go his own way. After graduating from high school in his hometown of Shrewsbury, he enrolled in an exclusive creative writing program at the University of Maine.

“I left after three days,” he said. “The academic approach didn’t work for me. Later, I tried French culinary school in Rhode Island, but became impatient and frustrated.”

 

 

He continued to search for his groove.

“Meanwhile, I became a young father,” he said. “When it came to food, I wanted something different for my kids. The foods I grew up with were industrial products, very bad for human health. Some say it’s because they’re full of sugar and fat, but that’s not the point. There’s nothing wrong with healthy fat or organic sugar. But factory foods that spend months or years in cans and freezers aren’t healthy.” 

Having grown up in the suburbs, D’Alessandro later became obsessed with the homesteaders’ lifestyle, including gardening and cooking from scratch. Characteristically, he went all-in when he started baking bread for his young family.

“I found a wheat grower and milled the grain myself,” he recounted. 

To this day, he and his loved ones embark on “never-ending food projects.”

“We collect apples to press and ferment cider. We love making big dinners with big roasts. We go on benders, like when we got into frying,” he said. 

D’Alessandro had previously been anti-frying, “but it’s because many oils are gross.” When using high-quality oils, French fries, doughnuts and other comfort foods can be made at home. 

Other favorites include homemade pasta and cranberry sauce, “using cranberries we harvested from local bogs. Now, that’s a true superfood.”

D’Alessandro’s tribe takes DIY seriously, including foraging for mushrooms and sassafras, and making butter, ice cream and naturally fermented sodas at home.

Before launching his sourdough career, D’Alessandro worked for Home Depot.

“I needed a job and liked being around working-class people,” he said.

Later, he began working first in one bakery, then in another; that’s where he learned the ropes. By the time he launched his home-based enterprise, he had mastered the rhythms of commercial baking. At his Shutesbury home, D’Alessandro transformed a one-car garage into the birthplace of delicious baked goods.

“I kept the project fairly low-budget,” he said. “I dreamed about it for years while working for other bakeries, shaping billions of loaves in workspaces with no windows.”

He would see equipment for sale online; he slowly acquired items even before he could use them. D’Alessandro had a mixer and a cooler years before his bakery was built.

“They sat for years because those deals were too good to pass on,” he said.

One huge expense was his Italian oven, which can hold up to 18 round loaves. That may sound like a lot to the home baker, but in contrast with the places he’d worked before, it was puny.

“Some bakeries have ovens that can accommodate 10 times that amount,” he noted.

When not baking, D’Alessandro loves going to the ocean, writing and reading.

“I’m a thinker,” he said, “and writing is a great way to communicate and organize out-of-control ideas. I also take time to meditate and be grateful.”

He’s a huge music fan and goes to live shows whenever he can. The good news is that a new hobby is facilitating sourdough workshops, and the bonus is that each participant gets to take active starter culture home. 

Just hearing D’Alessandro speak about sourdough is delicious.

“Some people say I should do sourdough tutorials online,” he said. “I don’t have the personality of a YouTube star, but I love doing these in-person workshops.”

Those lucky enough to get into the Dec. 2 session will see for themselves that D’Alessandro is, indeed, a star, although he humbly steps aside and lets the spotlight shine on that magical substance called sourdough.
